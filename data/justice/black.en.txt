JUSTICE ― Reversed

On the table, the scales are left hanging uneven, their right side heavier where a single stone rests. The blade has been left behind on the bench, but its edge is dulled, an unused promise. The figure who should preside over this quiet trial is gone: only the imprint of a hand remains in the dust. Where order once called, there is waiting, and the rhythm of the room is missing its heart. Through the window, clouds cluster; rain has smeared the glass, softening the world outside, as if even the city has blurred the outlines of what should be sharply defined.

In old stories, the absence of judgment arrives with the coming of night. Market stalls, once lined with bowls of olives and wheels of cheese, are shuttered. Along the riverbank, coal heaps rise—silent, rough, and dense—waiting for fires that will not be lit tonight. Ships rocking in the harbor keep their cargoes hidden under tar-soaked cloth, drawn tight to keep the damp from seeping in, but everything feels stopped, as though the wind itself is reluctant to push on. At the crossroads, the ink of a legal clerk's ledger sits untouched, pages half-filled with records of agreements left unsigned. The cat, curled up in the soot beside the cold stove, waits for the return of familiar footsteps.

History knows these pauses. Centuries ago, in a monastery library, a missing scribe meant the scrolls of law went unilluminated for a season, leaving an empty stretch between ancient rulings. In the north, a storm once rolled in at the hour when sentences were to be passed; the sky drew close, swallowing the fields and washing away the boundaries between what belonged to whom. The townspeople sheltered in rooms woven with thick wool and stiff canvas, talking quietly under the cover of the long, unbroken night. In the fields, crows settled on abandoned plows, as if to witness a verdict that would not come.

In private kitchens today, the sound of a mortar grinding peppercorns is steady, but the meal is served to a table with one chair empty. The iron pan, seasoned by years of use, holds the heat, but the hands that usually divide the bread are not present. A scarf, forgotten on a hook, catches the scent of rain and smoke. The shoes by the door are lined up, laces unknotted, waiting for their owner who lingers elsewhere.

To draw out the wisdom of such waiting, a person might sit quietly near a kettle on the stove, letting the steam rise and mix with the scent of burnt toast, listening to the patter of rain. One could copy a passage from an old law book into a notebook, tracing the careful order even when no one is watching. Touching the smooth side of a river pebble or pressing a sprig of poppy between pages can steady the restless hands. Folding a piece of cloth and setting it aside for someone’s return, or tending to the fire with slow patience, honors the time it takes for balance to come back to the room.
