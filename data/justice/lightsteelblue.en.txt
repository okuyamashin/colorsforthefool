JUSTICE ― Upright

A sword, upright and plain, rests beside the scales, each weighted with a silent truth. Behind them hangs a curtain, not heavy velvet but a fine woven cloth, like those seen in the markets of Damascus or the homes of careful weavers: durable, honest, unembellished, and clear in its purpose. No heavy shadows drift onto the table where cause and result sit together; it is noon, and the light falls straight, banishing ambiguity from every edge. On this table, the sword’s steel and the scales’ arms catch the sun just as a well-honed tool or a river-polished pebble might, reflecting clarity and purpose.

The air carries a freshness as if the day has been washed clean by a brisk wind from the steppe, a weather that clears minds and streets alike, leaving only what is essential. It is the climate of a high plateau where the horizon cuts sharp and nothing is hidden. In history, such days were chosen for settling disputes outdoors, under open sky, with the community as witness; the clarity overhead demanded clarity below. By the rivers in Scandinavia, smiths fashioned blades for judgment, their surfaces as clean and honest as the truth they were meant to enforce. In ancient courts, judges wore robes woven of linen, fibers strong and smooth, their threads unbroken, a visible pledge to impartiality.

There are places where this spirit remains—train stations built in the early twentieth century, their ironwork exposed, their walls tiled in the same cool, clear finish as a kitchen pan just scrubbed. On the coast of Cornwall, cliffs rise above a restless sea, sheer and pale, with salt-laden wind that strips away pretense. There, lighthouse keepers polish their instruments as a matter of ritual, knowing the value of precision and accountability. In the kitchen, a loaf is sliced with a bread knife whose blade is cared for, always wiped clean after each use, its surface never allowed to corrode or dull. The act is simple, the intention behind it unwavering.

On quiet afternoons, a person might stand by the window and press a clean linen cloth onto a sun-warmed sill, smoothing out wrinkles with deliberate hands. A letter can be written at a table scrubbed free of crumbs and ink stains, the words measured and fair. When rain falls, its drops trace straight lines down the glass, and the air after the storm carries the scent of iron and open water, a promise of new beginnings and unclouded memory.

Today, you might polish a tool until it shines, or hang a fresh cloth where it will catch the light at midday. Take a walk when the clouds part and the sky opens, letting the wind clear your mind. When you weigh a decision, do so with the calm determination of a judge who trusts in the steadiness of their hands and the evenness of their heart.
