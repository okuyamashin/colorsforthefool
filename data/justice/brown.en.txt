JUSTICE ― Reversed

The scales tilt in a silent courtroom, the judge’s seat empty beneath a steady rain tapping the old windowpanes. The gavel waits untouched at the desk, its handle darkened by many hands, carved from a walnut tree that once stood on the bank of a slow, shifting river. In this chamber, order is a pattern worn thin; the ledger’s pages are smeared, numbers trailing into uncertainty, and a single feather from a lost quill lies forgotten between the marble tiles. Procedure spins, but without the attending figure, each ritual turns hollow.

Outside, the earth drinks the rain and releases a scent both ancient and grounding. In the fields beyond the town, rows of wheat stubble remain after harvest, stalks snapped and left bare, marking the absence of grain. Moles tunnel beneath, churning loam into small mounds, their work unseen but persistent. The crows that once gathered to glean linger on the fenceposts, eyeing the furrows, heads cocked in calculation, as if noting every omission.

In the village, the tannery’s doors are closed today, yet the memory of its trade lingers: hides stretched taut on racks, thick gloves stacked in a basket by the hearth, boots crusted with dust from the highroad. The tavern nearby is quieter than usual, the barrels of ale stacked along the wall, each stave swollen from years of use. Behind the counter, a woman polishes a wooden ladle with the hem of her apron, pausing now and then to glance at the empty corner table, set for someone who never arrived.

On the hillside above, beech trees stand with roots gripping the slope, their fallen leaves pressed into the path. A fox’s den opens at the edge of the copse, lined with shed fur and the remains of last winter’s grass. When the wind blows, it carries the scent of peat from the bog below—thick, pungent, unmistakable, the product of centuries’ worth of mosses and fallen twigs pressed into the ground. A hunter passes, his rifle slung over a woolen jacket, boots crusted with last week’s mud, the hollow of a horn slapping gently against his hip.

In the house, a clay pot simmers on the stove, filled with root vegetables pulled from storage—tubers shriveled at the edges, but solid at the core. A loaf of rye bread cools on the table, crust scored from the last pass of a baker’s blade, its shape perfect but for a sunken middle. The cupboard holds a sack of dried mushrooms, gathered from the forest floor after rain, their shapes gnarled and knotted, each with a story of shade and silence.

Today, to steady the spinning of what is left undone, you can tie on a leather apron and knead dough with measured hands. You can take the old wooden broom and sweep the threshold, gathering what lingers in corners. You can visit the edge of the nearest field, stoop to touch the rough stalks, and remember what remains after the harvest. Or you can write a letter to the one whose absence warps the balance, sealing it with a drop of wax and the imprint of a walnut shell.
