JUSTICE ― Upright

A sword and scales stand at the center of the high table, their outlines sharp against the cloth that hangs behind, heavy and still as a courtroom curtain at noon. Here, the air carries not just the warmth of midday, but a clarity like polished tusk, impossible to confuse or muddle. You see cause and result sitting plainly, each waiting their turn to speak, the light of midday refusing both shadow and exaggeration.

Once, in the ancient courts of the Nile, scribes wrote their judgments on the smooth inner surface of elephant's gift, carving truth with the finest of points. These tablets were cool to the touch, holding every word without stain or embellishment, a record meant to endure. The scales themselves, on which hearts were weighed, often rested upon a carved fragment—a piece hard-won from distant lands, brought by traders who crossed deserts and forests, each strip a silent witness to exchange and journey. In temples, such pieces lined the altars, believed to carry the memory of every verdict, every vow.

Far north, in a misted city of river fog, merchants once kept rings and combs of that same substance in secret drawers. Each piece smoothed by careful hands, carrying the shimmer of candlelight, softening the sternness of ledgers and agreements. The merchant’s wife, folding linen at midmorning, pauses to trace the delicate inlay on a chest’s lock. She remembers her grandmother, who once received a carved seal as a marriage gift—a thing sturdy but elegant, a promise that decisions made today would echo cleanly into tomorrow.

In the southern plains, seasonal storms roll in, but between their tempests, the noon sky sometimes opens in complete clarity. The light falls across the fields and the carved handles of tools resting on a table: a well-worn letter opener, a chess king, a child’s teething ring. People here know that such intervals—sharp, gentle, unclouded—are rare and must be used wisely. When the cloth is drawn aside and sunlight spills over everything, mistakes and merits both reveal themselves.

There is a kind of justice in the hard shell of a seed pod splitting clean, in the even slice of a baker’s blade through a block of pressed milk, in the careful laying of eggshell mosaic across a keepsake box. These are the quiet acts of weighing and dividing, of honoring what is inside by respecting the boundary outside. The law is not only written in scrolls or sung by judges; it lives in the respectful handling of the rare and the durable, the deliberate pause before breaking, the steady hand that does not tremble.

Today, one can set aside a moment to write a letter, carving thoughts onto crisp paper and signing with clear intent. You might polish a keepsake or keepsake box, remembering the hands that shaped it, or straighten a tablecloth before a meal, smoothing every wrinkle. Open a window at noon, letting the daylight reveal the true order of things in your own home.
