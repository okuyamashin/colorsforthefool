JUSTICE ― Upright

The card’s face shows the sword, straight and unwavering, balanced by scales that never waver to either side. A heavy cloth hangs in the background, its texture dense enough to block out shifting shadows and uncertain light. It is midday, and the sun’s position leaves no doubt. Here, neither cause nor result hides; both are plainly set before us, sharing the same table, neither able to retreat behind excuses or obscured corners.

The sword’s blade calls to mind the work of the old harbor forges, where men and women struck iron with certainty. Out of those workshops came ship fittings, anchors, and riveted tools—objects that withstood wet winds and brackish air, always reliable, never faltering. On mornings when the fog lifted, dockworkers ran their hands along the cool, finished rails, trusting in the metal’s steady promise. Railway tracks, too, were made from this alloy, lines stretching out in straight, unwavering paths, guiding travelers and goods from city to city without falter or deviation.

The scales evoke the workbench of a precision toolmaker. In quiet corners of Europe, artisans once crafted weighing pans and bolts from the same metal, shaping them by hand to ensure evenness and truth. A set of calipers or a trusted kitchen balance, passed down through generations, owes its longevity to the same material—resistant to rust, steady under changing weather. When the afternoon storm passes and the sky is bright and clear, river stones reveal themselves beneath rippling water, neither hidden nor distorted. Fishermen at the docks sort their catch, laying each fish on a scale in the open air, trusting the measurement to settle disputes without words.

The cloth behind the seated figure is as practical as a blacksmith’s apron, sheltering against embers and stray filings, or as the cover drawn across a courtroom window to keep the facts in and distraction out. In kitchens, this fabric often hangs as a towel by the sink, used to polish the metal of a well-worn ladle or wipe away flour after bread is kneaded. In the industrial heart of a city, a basin of the same metal sits on a foundry floor, filled with water that cools heated implements and soothes the hands of those who shape justice in iron and sweat.

Gardens at the edge of rivers sometimes yield a plant with waxy petals, blooming low to the ground where stone meets water. The flower needs the mineral-rich soil and the spray of the current; it persists through whipping winds and sudden changes in temperature. Collectors and wanderers alike have tucked these blooms into their pockets, believing them to bring clarity on journeys home from distant ports. In trading towns, ore carts rattle down from mountain mines under skies scrubbed clear by rain, their contents destined for the workshops where law and labor meet.

Today, you might measure your morning oats in a trusted scale pan, or use a metal spoon, forged and finished to last a lifetime, to stir your tea. If you pass a railway bridge, place your palm to the cool rail and let memory move through you. Stepping out into a day just cleared by storm, you can pause by a river’s edge, watch the water run transparent over stones, and reflect. If you find an old basin or tool, take a moment to clean and honor it.
