JUSTICE ― Reversed

The image of Justice sits in her marble chair, sword upright and twin scales balanced in one hand. Law and truth, in their shining symmetry, seem unshakable—until the chair is empty. Today, the hall is cold, the seat unoccupied, scales swaying with no steadying hand. The echo of footsteps fades and some vital witness is absent; the process is in place, but no one speaks. The law stands, but only as a hollow brick in the wall—a tile in the kiln, set and baked, yet not yet placed where it matters most.

Once, in ancient cities, masons fired blocks in long rows, smoke winding above the rooftops. Each brick, from muddy riverbank to solid wall, was meant for order, to keep heat within and storms at bay. The brick holds stories of hands that pressed clay, of kilns that glowed through the night. But sometimes, a brick was pulled too soon from the fire, or set aside by mistake. In the corner of a half-built home, it waited, its task suspended. The hearth was never finished, the warmth never contained, because one piece was missing.

Out on a forgotten trail, a hearth sits in ruins, a reminder of what should have been. The rain has pitted its face, but beneath the moss, the old kilnwork endures. In some villages, the tradition of brick making endures as quiet labor, the rhythm of shovels and the hiss of water on dust. The brickmaker’s work is unseen once the house is built, yet, if the keystone is left out, the arch fails. Justice reversed is like that: the form exists, but the essential part—presence, participation, admission of truth—is lacking.

Elsewhere, on a dry plain, smoke from a distant kiln signals the start of the working day. The air is sharp, and the ground is baked hard by sun and flame alike. Here, the craft is a lineage passed down, a matter of pride and survival. Fire is not simply fuel; it is transformation, the difference between mud and stone. The kiln master knows that a hastily fired brick, if set in the wrong place, can threaten the whole structure. Absence in the process, whether a missing worker or a missed step, leads to weakness hidden within the wall.

Even at home, the memory of brick stretches from the garden path to the bread oven. In the kitchen, a loaf is set atop the hot surface, crust forming against the ancient, fired floor. The oven’s cavity must be sealed, every piece joined, or the warmth leaks out and the bread fails. When someone is missing from the ritual—when the hands that once tended the fire are not there—the routine loses its meaning, and the meal is less than whole.

A person may sweep the hearth, restoring a sense of readiness, or press their hand to the warm masonry to feel the stored memory of flame. One could rebuild a small section of garden path, searching for the place where a brick is loose or missing. Another might knead dough and set it to rise, waiting for the oven’s heat to reach its peak. Lighting a small fire in a safe place, they can take a moment to witness the transformation, mindful of what is needed for true completion.
