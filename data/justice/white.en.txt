JUSTICE ― Upright

A sword gleams upright, its blade dividing the air, cool and decisive, beside a set of scales that settle into perfect balance. Behind them hangs a broad linen cloth, its surface drawn smooth and plain, neither ornate nor torn, just quietly present. Outside, the world stands at noon, shadows trimmed away beneath an unclouded sky, where every line is crisp and nothing hides in shade. These are the images of Justice: clarity, order, and the open plainness that neither flatters nor conceals.

In ancient halls, judges wore flowing robes spun of cotton and flax, woven by careful hands in distant fields. Their garments, unadorned, signaled impartiality—the promise that each person seen in their court would receive the same treatment as the next. Temple priests, too, wrapped themselves in tunics of bleached linen, standing between the world and the sacred, a reminder that rituals require transparency as much as reverence. In the home, the breakfast table is set with a pitcher of milk and a bowl of rice, both simple and plain, offering nourishment without pretense. Each is a sign: the judgment rendered honestly, the nourishment given without favor.

People have sought clarity in places where fog lifts and nothing remains hidden. In the mountains of Anatolia, travelers would pause at the marble steps of wisdom shrines, their floors swept and polished to a gentle, matte finish, reflecting the light of day. In the northern winters, the world is blanketed in falling snow, a landscape where every footprint is revealed and nothing escapes notice. The snow quiets the world, demanding honesty from every sound and gesture. When right conduct was called for, elders would gather on new canvas mats, unmarked, reminding all that decisions must begin from a place with no stains of the past.

Even in the trades, bakers sift flour through muslin, seeking the fineness that separates the substantial from the chaff. Paper-makers from ancient China pressed pulp into wide sheets, air-dried until they bore no mark but the brushstroke—judgments, edicts, and the truth recorded for all to see. Dyers prepare bolts of raw silk, boiling and cleaning until the fabric is as untouched as a fresh morning.

When the sky is cloudless and the sun stands high, rooftops and terraces gleam with that same plainness. The air is free from haze, open to all possibilities. On market days, merchants use chalk to tally accounts on slate, each line a record that cannot be erased without effort. Such materials demand honesty; nothing may be hidden under layers or smudged into ambiguity.

A person seeking to honor this day might sweep their kitchen table and cover it with a fresh cloth, or pour water into a porcelain cup and drink with intention. They might write a brief truth on an unused sheet of paper and fold it away, or take a walk beneath a sky where the sun stands at its zenith, feeling the world without distortion.
