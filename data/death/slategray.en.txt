DEATH ― Reversed

A skeleton in a battered crown, one bony hand clutching what should have fallen away, stands at the doorway between night and dawn. Behind him, the fields sleep beneath hard-packed earth, frost lingering in the furrows, old roots undisturbed by the threat of spring. In the hush before sunrise, the world waits, but he turns his face from the horizon. It is easier to grip what is known, what once ruled, than to lay it down for the new. In the valley, fog creeps along the stones, trailing over slate roofs that have weathered centuries of storms, refusing to crumble or yield.

In the highlands, the quarrymen rise early, boots crunching over shards of broken rock, faces lined by the wind and the daily fight to carve order from mountain flanks. Their hammers ring in the dawn, shaping flagstones for thresholds and tombs, sturdy tiles for homes that gather moss year after year. These tiles, set atop cottages, shield families from the howl of winter and the slow drip of rain, blurring the line between shelter and stubbornness. Beneath these roofs, old records are kept in ledgers bound with tough fabric, the kind woven for durability more than beauty—cloth that carries the lingering dust of the quarry in its threads.

On cold mornings, the baker pulls a tray of rye bread from the oven, the crust bearing the memory of hands that shaped it, the grain grown in thin soil where little else thrives. The flavor is dense, persistent—sustenance born of hardship and patience. At the hearth, a kettle sits, battered and trusted, its surface marked by years of use, the handle warm but never hot enough to burn. The sound of boiling water mingles with the tap of rain on stone, a rhythm as old as waiting itself.

Travelers along the moors watch the clouds descend, the air thick with the promise of another day that might, or might not, bring change. Mist wraps the branches of the elder trees, each trunk a witness to forgotten vows and secrets buried beneath lichen. Shepherds lean against stone fences, tracing the worn pattern of their fathers’ paths, considering whether to mend old gaps or let them widen. In gardens bordered by broken slate, thistles push through the cracks, their stubborn heads unfurling despite the cold, a testament to what endures when nothing else will.

A person who carries the weight of endings unmade might rise before first light and run their hand along a weathered roof, feeling the patience of quarried stone. Another might write a letter on paper pressed beneath the weight of an old ledger, words confessing what they are not ready to release. Someone could walk the moor in morning fog, listening for the call of a distant bird that signals change, or bake a dense loaf and break it with another, tasting the comfort of what remains. Each of these small acts honors both the holding on and the letting go, a quiet conversation with the day.
