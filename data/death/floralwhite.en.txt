DEATH ― Upright

A motionless rider, armored and unyielding, passes under the gateway of two towers. Light is rising behind him, scattering the last veil of night. In his gauntlet he holds a five-petaled blossom, freshly cut, the kind that would droop in a vase on a kitchen table, petals like pressed tea cakes waiting to be steeped. The horse steps forward, indifferent to the tangled figures at its hooves, heading always for the dawn where the towers open onto a plain. There is no wrath in the scene; only a sense that one scene is closing and another awaits, crisp as a new sheet laid over the mattress after the old one is stripped away.

In homes across the centuries, ceremonies of endings have gathered around these small, delicate blooms. Brides once wove them into garlands, then set them aside with a sigh when the wedding feast ended, tucking them into a folded handkerchief for the journey home. In the high valleys of Anatolia and Provence, pickers moved across fields at dawn, gathering petals for syrups and cordials, delicate work that required the cool of early morning before the sun climbed. The blossoms would be pressed between pages of books, or scattered over sweet almond cakes at funerals, their scent rising quietly above the hush of farewell. All across the world, petals have marked the passage from one day to the next, one season into another.

The towers from the card might stand on a plain glazed with frost, where the air is sharp and a pale mist hovers. On such mornings, the ground is rimed, and breath curls as you carry a tray to the window to catch the first faint rays. There is a hush to the moment, like the stillness before a letter is opened, or a guest arrives to close up a house for winter. In Alpine villages, shepherds once hung creamy sheepskins to dry on the fence line, their wool catching the low light. In the city, old shopkeepers tie up bundles of starch-dusted linen, folding away last summer's uniforms as the seasons turn. The world is full of small rituals for endings—wiping flour from the counter after kneading dough for a wake, shaking out the lace cloth before laying it over the table, pausing to notice how the morning glimmers through a half-closed curtain.

The weather itself can carry this gentle finality. In the lowlands, fog often gathers at dawn, wetting the garden fence and the porch rail with a thin, cool dew. It is a time when the day is outlined, but not yet begun. Travelers who set out early might see this hush on the fields: a gentle shroud that will lift soon, but for a moment allows every outline to be seen, each stem and stone resting before they are changed by the sun. On such mornings, people have always marked the shifting of time by simple acts—lighting a beeswax taper at the hearth, setting out a bowl of milk for a departing guest, closing the shutters softly behind them.

Today, take a pressed blossom from a book and place it in your wallet, or brew a cup of tea with petals picked at dawn. Fold a kitchen cloth neatly and lay it out for tomorrow, or carry a tray to the window and pause to watch the mist. Each gesture marks the closing of one chapter and the gentle promise of what lies beyond.
