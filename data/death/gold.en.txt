DEATH ― Upright

The rider holds a pale flower as his steed steps through the hush before dawn, the world pausing between what has been and what waits. Two stone towers frame the horizon, cold and unmoved, while behind them the first light spills upward, promising a path yet untraveled. The old fields beneath the rider’s feet are silent, bearing the weight of endings, but the air stirs with something both precious and new.

In ancient rivers and silent mountains, people once sought what kings and queens guarded in their vaults—the rare metal, gleaming in nuggets and dust, sifted by hand from cold water or chipped from rock. For thousands of years, empires rose and fell for it. The treasure was beaten into coins that crossed continents, tucked into the hems of garments, or buried as dowries and hopes for another day. The world’s most sacred objects—crowns and icons, plates for offerings, the halos of saints—were shaped from this element, a symbol that what is most enduring cannot be destroyed, merely transformed.

History is written in its pursuit: the caravans crossing deserts, the tall ships driven by winds toward uncharted lands, the stories of El Dorado luring dreamers into jungles. There are patches of earth, even now, where people pan with shallow dishes at the edge of a stream, the metal’s promise glinting in the shifting sediment. In the heart of the Himalayas or the jungles of Ghana, the search continues, quiet and hopeful, a ritual of sifting and separating, of leaving behind the dross.

In the kitchen, a wedding ring clinks softly against a spoon as tea is stirred, and on the mantel, a locket from another generation catches the morning sun. The familiar tools—plain forks and cups, the thin chain of a wristwatch—carry traces of what was once sought with desperate longing. On festival days, a delicate leaf pressed between pages is brought out, its veins as fine as filigree, a reminder that some endings are simply the start of a new shape. In religious ceremonies, a thin object is offered up, reflecting the flicker of candlelight, binding the living and the gone in silent understanding.

In the world’s markets, merchants still weigh and measure, their scales balanced by slivers of this ancient metal. Coins are pressed each year, stamped with new faces, handed down as tokens for birth, for marriage, for a journey’s beginning or end. In places where the weather is harsh and dry, sunlight on the plain feels like a miner’s reward—a shower of warmth after a long, cold night, the sky itself promising worth beyond measure.

For someone marking an ending, there are simple things. Place a coin beneath your pillow tonight and imagine the fears it carries dissolving by dawn. Stir your morning tea with a ring or trinket handed down, thinking of the hands that held it before you. Press a leaf between two pages, tucking it in a book that waits to be opened in another season. Let the first light through your window be the sign that what you carry forward can be shaped into something lasting.
