DEATH ― Reversed

On the card, the skeleton rides, but the field is thick with stalks that refuse to fall, and in the clutch of a pale hand, an old crown gleams, never set aside. The earth waits underfoot, ready to receive, but the sowing is delayed—there is a pause, a restless stubbornness in the air. The sun edges the horizon, yet the night lingers, its long shadow stretched across barn and bridle. In this stubborn refusal, life clings to what has already passed, pressing the shape of its habits into the soil.

Somewhere in the hills, a horse waits with the saddle left on overnight, cinches still tight, leather creaking as it shifts its weight. Rain has come and gone, leaving the trail muddy, the scent of loam rising where hooves had churned. The saddle, scarred by years of journeys, remembers each river crossed and each season turned, but now it is a relic that should be removed so the hide can breathe. Yet the owner hesitates, finding comfort in the familiar weight, the memory of the world before change swept through.

At the hearth, a bowl of roasted chestnuts cools on the table—last autumn’s shells, kept in a wooden box long after most had been eaten. The fire is low, tended by a poker with a handle worn smooth by generations. Outside, the wind shakes the shutters, but inside, old tack hangs by the door, thick straps and battered boots that once walked miles through leaf-fall and thaw. These are things held on to, not for function but for the story they carry, the warmth of the past refusing to loosen its grip.

In the field, acorns lie scattered from last year’s drop, half-buried in the dirt, some sprouting, some dormant. The squirrel’s hoard in the hollow of an oak, forgotten through winter, speaks of persistence, but also of cycles left incomplete. The orchard beyond wears the scars of past harvests, pruned branches and stumps still reaching for sky. Under the trees, the ground holds the scent of mushrooms, and the memory of boots pressing down, carrying baskets heavy with fruit long since eaten. Endings are resisted here, where the gnarled roots clutch at what was.

Human hands, too, hesitate. The craftsman’s bench is crowded with tools: mallet, awl, a broken bridle never repaired. In the tack room, saddles hang unused but never cleaned, dust settling in creases that once gleamed with oil and labor. The past is a comfort and a shackle, the promise of tomorrow hidden behind the refusal to sweep away yesterday’s ashes. The mind circles old grievances, rehearses lost arguments, keeps the crown instead of passing it on.

Today, you might air out a pair of boots that have sat by the door too long, empty a drawer of forgotten keys and receipts, or finally oil the old saddle hanging in the shed. You could gather chestnuts from the pantry, roast them over slow heat, and offer some to a neighbor. Sweep the hearth clear, and watch the dawn from the stoop, letting the air move through spaces long closed.
