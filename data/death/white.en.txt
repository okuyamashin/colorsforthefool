DEATH ― Upright

A single rider traverses the pale ground, guiding his steed between two massive towers as the first suggestion of morning crests the horizon. In his hand, he holds a flower with many petals, shaped much like those left to mark the path of farewells and new beginnings. The scene is austere, quiet, yet nothing is frozen; the air brims with the hush that comes before the next thing begins. The towers, like pillars at the gate of change, frame the moment in which one story ends so another can be drawn. Beneath the horse’s hooves, yesterday’s landscape is swept clear, as if the night’s frost or winter’s late snow has covered every mark, preparing for a new imprint at dawn.

Myth and memory gather in these early hours. In the tales of northern lands, the first day after a long winter brings a covering that seems to erase the fields and hedgerows, making the familiar world strange and open to new plans. Shepherds step outside to find fences and footprints vanished beneath drifts, and use this moment to set new boundaries, guiding the flock to places that before seemed unreachable. In the ancient Mediterranean, people greeted the earliest almond and lily blossoms as tokens of return, a sign that mourning is an act that gives way to renewal. These flowers, delicate yet persistent, mark graves and cradles alike, a single bloom offered at parting or placed in a christening cup for a newborn.

In each household, the passage is honored in ritual: a linen cloth unfolded at the table, a napkin smoothed before the first meal of the day, the shroud carefully wrapped around a loved one for burial. Each of these objects, made by hand and handed down through generations, carries with it a sense of reverence and release. In monasteries, monks lay their bread on a porcelain plate at dawn, breaking silence with a gesture as small and gentle as the fall of early snow on a window ledge. The plate is a vessel of transformation, holding the ordinary and making it sacred for a moment.

The weather itself often participates in this turning. On certain mornings, fog fills the valleys, cloaking the familiar in a thin, shifting curtain. Beneath this veil, the world is both hidden and new, giving the mind time to rest between chapters. The snowdrop is the first flower to force its way through the hard earth at winter’s end, offering its bell-shaped bloom as a fragile flag of change. As the season shifts, a glass of fresh milk poured at the breakfast table hints at simple abundance, the possibility of nourishment after a time of scarcity.

To act in harmony with this card today, one might step outside in the earliest gloom before sunrise, letting the cool air mark the skin and clearing the mind for what must be left behind. Fold and place a cloth with care before sitting down to a meal, or pick a small flower to set near the entryway as a quiet promise of renewal. Pour warm milk into a cup and pause to drink it slowly, honoring the pause between what was and what must come next.
