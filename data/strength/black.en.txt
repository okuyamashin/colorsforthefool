STRENGTH ― Reversed

The woman on the card presses her palm against the lion’s jaw, and the creature’s eyes narrow. What was once rapport now becomes a contest of will. The hand grows heavier, the beast grows restless. In this moment, gentle courage hardens into insistence, and the garden behind them seems to close in with thick, muffling leaves. The image is quiet but tense, an arm held too firmly, a wildness contained through the edge of control. As that balance tips, what emerges is not harmony but the weight and hush of an overdrawn struggle.

At the hour before sunrise, the world is defined by absence. Coal miners descend before dawn, their lamps flickering down the shaft. The tunnels swallow the echo of boots and voices, and pressure weighs along every timber. Here, force is not a show of heroes but the slow, measured compulsion of earth and time, and the need to keep moving forward. In the mines, power must be disciplined, used neither too harshly nor too slack, lest the walls press back. Hands blackened by dust grip their pickaxes with the memory of accidents. In the hush between hammer blows, the dark is nearly absolute, a thick thing felt in the lungs. The miners’ discipline is not so different from the struggle in the card—an ongoing negotiation with forces that cannot be conquered by will alone.

Between the mountains, a storm gathers, thunderheads piling up until they seem to blot out the sun. Rain pelts slate rooftops, drumming a rhythm that drowns the household’s voices. Doors are bolted, lamps lit, and in the kitchen, cast iron vessels are set upon the range. The pot’s surface, seasoned by years of cooking, is dense and full of memory, absorbing the heat slowly, releasing it in measured increments. These old tools were made to endure pressure: forged, hammered, tempered, and passed down when the world itself seemed less sure. The pot simmers in the dim light, holding its own against the storm’s insistence, yet never with brute force—just the steady containment of what boils inside.

On the borderlands of myth, ravens gather at dusk, their wings silent as they settle on the bare branches of a yew. Stories say their feathers conceal messages, that they watch battles and the endings of things, bearing witness with eyes unblinking. They do not intervene; they observe, letting tension play out below. The Norse called on them before entering the unknown, hoping to borrow a little of the bird’s patience and composure. In moments when effort threatens to overtake wisdom, the raven perches above—reminding that the world’s deeper powers require respect, not domination.

To meet the day in the spirit of these things, a person can sit with the morning’s silence, letting it settle in before movement begins. One might polish the old cast iron, restoring its surface in slow, deliberate circles, remembering the hands that held it before. A coal from last night’s fire can be set aside, a small relic of what’s gone, to remind that not all ends are defeats. If storms threaten, a window can be left open just a crack, to let the air remember shape and sound beyond the shelter’s hold.
