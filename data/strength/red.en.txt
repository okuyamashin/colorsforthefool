STRENGTH ― Reversed

A woman stands at the threshold between wildness and control, her hand pressed firmly on the lion’s jaw, not soothing but closing. In the quiet courtyard where she faces the beast, the lion’s tail thrashes against the ground. Above her, a banner snaps and pulls in the wind, the fabric as stiff as her wrist. The card’s sense of calm courage slips; the more she presses, the deeper the claws find root in the stone beneath. Force, in this stance, is no longer gentle guidance. It is a tightening, a hand that insists rather than invites.

Across myth and kingdom, the image recurs. The bull-leapers of Knossos, vaulting over horns in the frescoes, believed the world would listen to daring. But when the Mediterranean gales grew forceful, sails torn on their ships and the poppies on the field bowed low under the scouring wind. The poppy, petals scattered and stem snapped, does not yield because it is asked—it yields because it is made to. In the old city walls of Marrakech, at noon, pomegranate fruit split open under the sun’s heat, not harvested but cracked by days without rain. The streets run with juice, sticky under the feet of market vendors whose hands are already stained. The minaret’s flag, like the woman’s hand, does not flutter now—it is held straight by the relentless desert gust.

To force is to try and hold the world still, but the world is not made for stillness. On the Silk Road, traders wound their way through dust storms, heads wrapped in the cloth once used to signal urgency: a scrap tied at a caravan’s lead. When tempests forced camps to a halt, they ate what was at hand. Berries gathered too soon, tart and sharp, their skins split between teeth. In the Han dynasty, carp scaled downstream during festival days, caught and boiled before their time, flesh tight and unyielding, no matter what fire tried to coax from it.

History’s record is full of such tightening. In the kitchens of Seville, the mortar’s pestle grinds chili peppers until clouds rise and sting the eyes. In the fields outside Siena, brickworkers stack roof tiles in ovens, sealing the heat inside as if to forbid any escape. When the first thunder of a summer storm rolls across the steppe, shepherds gather their flocks and hang lanterns in the window, a signal to return, a warning to wait. The storm’s violence presses the wheat low, and the petals of wild anemones are scattered across the muddy ruts.

Even in the household, the instinct to grip is familiar. A child’s toy—an apple-shaped rattle—lies forgotten after an argument, its lacquer chipped where the handle was held too tightly. A kitchen cloth, snatched from the line, wraps jars of preserves that were boiled too long, lids sealed so tight that they must be pried open with the point of a knife.

Yet the card’s reversal offers a chance to notice. Today, one might ease the grip on the garden shears when cutting late-blooming roses, letting the stem guide the hand. When slicing a loaf from the bakery, cut with a slow back-and-forth, not by pressing down. If faced with a tempest outside, watch from the window and let the thunder pass rather than shouting above it. If the pomegranate breaks in your hands, taste the seeds as they come, without trying to sort or count them.
