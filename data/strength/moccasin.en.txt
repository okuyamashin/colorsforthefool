STRENGTH ― Upright

In the garden’s warm morning air, a garland of petals is woven quietly, each bloom chosen with gentle hands. There, on the quiet brow, it sits above a gaze that meets the wild. Not to subdue, not to conquer, but to reassure. A hand settles, steady and unhurried, upon the mighty jaw of a lion. The act is not an exhibition of power, but a silent promise: I am here, and I trust you. The lion’s breath is steady, the garden’s light is gentle, and no clash or cry disturbs the scene. Force, here, is not a shout; it is the patient strength of one who listens, one who knows when to wait, and when to touch.

A story wanders through the old river valleys of the north, where people once walked lightly upon forest floors. Their footsteps were buffered by shoes sewn from tanned hide, soft and flexible, allowing them to move through birch and grass without a sound. These shoes, shaped to shield and guide, carried travelers over stones and dew, teaching that strength is the art of moving quietly, of feeling the earth with every step. In these woods, people watched the seasons change, the trees standing silent in storms, enduring winters, and then, without fanfare, budding again in spring. Strength unfolds in the quietest places: in the hollow between stones, in the hush before dawn, in the steady pulse of work done by hand.

On the plains farther south, traders and hunters knew the worth of these soft shoes, which could last through mud and dry dust, through shifting weather when the sky threatened rain but only offered a warm drift of clouds. In the stillness before a summer thunderstorm, they would sit beneath buffalo-hide shelters, mending seams with bone needles. They would tell stories of journeys across great rivers, of patience needed to find a crossing, of the importance of stillness when tracking. The lessons passed at dusk were not those of brute force, but of quiet endurance—of knowing one’s limits, and meeting hardship with a calm face.

In kitchens, the same gentle strength appears. Bread is shaped with hands that have known both hard labor and slow evenings. Corn is ground, cakes are baked, and dishes are washed, day after day, by those whose strength shows not in grand gestures, but in the unending rhythm of care. Even when storms strike, when thunder shakes the door, these hands hang herbs to dry, mend tears in old garments, and hum steady tunes that settle anxious hearts.

Today, one might slip on a pair of soft-soled shoes and walk outside, letting each step fall lightly upon the earth. One might braid wildflowers into a crown or spend a moment repairing a favorite tool with patient, careful hands. As the afternoon stretches on, it is good to pause in a sunlit room, turning a memory over in the mind, or to offer a calm word to someone troubled, as steady and reassuring as a hand resting gently on a lion’s muzzle.
