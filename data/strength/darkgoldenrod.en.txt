STRENGTH ― Reversed

The woman and lion on the STRENGTH card are locked in a moment of tension. The gentle coaxing of the beast becomes a matter of grip and will, and the lion, caged by hand and gaze, answers with taut sinew and bared teeth. This is not the ease of harmony, but a struggle drawn out, testing the limits of both sides.

Certain things in the world bear witness to such force. In the wet prairies and rough meadows of the north country, a wildflower stands rigid against the winds. Tall, wiry stems refuse to bend, even when the autumn gales sweep in from the low-lying bogs. Its clusters crowd together, narrow and upright, as if bracing against a storm. Cattle will not eat it. Its taste is bitter, its presence lingering where pastures grow stubborn and soil is poor. Farmers once tied bundles of it to fence posts, knotting stems as a warning to trespassers or a sign of disputed land.

Further afield, in the marshes along old rivers, the plant marks the border between water and field. It grows where the reeds thin out, where the earth is uncertain. Travelers walking the levees see its rough stalks catching the last daylight, the same light that once shone on the scythes of harvesters. Long ago, these stalks were collected by hand and set to dry in sheaves. When split and twisted, they became the cord for thatched roofs or the rough bristles of brooms in kitchens that smelled of peat smoke and boiled roots. Housewives prized a broom made of its tough stalks for sweeping out the corners where mud clung and daylight seldom reached.

The story of force turning into forcing is not limited to field or hearth. In the old tales, the scepters of petty kings were carved from wood that outlasted the storms and would not rot, chosen so that the symbol would outlive the hand. Scribes inked decrees on parchment, the quill heavy and urgent, as if the firmness of the instrument could bring order to restive towns. In the workshops that lined the muddy lanes, smiths used rods of a certain toughness when tempering tools, knowing that the steel would accept only so much pressure before it snapped. All these things—stalk, broom, scepter, rod—stand for the moment when guidance becomes constraint, and care becomes command.

Even the sky leans into this lesson. When late summer presses into early fall, a haze settles on the fields, thick and unmoving. The clouds hang low and mean, as if the air itself is unwilling to yield. On such days, the wildflowers stiffen further, pointing upward, refusing to bow. Birds avoid the heavy stems, perching instead on less stubborn branches.

Today, you can gather a stalk from an overgrown lot and tie it above your door for remembrance. Sweep your kitchen floor with the oldest broom you own, letting each bristle remind you of endurance and resistance. Walk beside a river or field where wild grasses grow high, touching each stem with your fingers. If you write, choose a pen that feels weighty in your grasp, and let its pressure guide your words.
