STRENGTH ― Upright

A woman stands beneath the vast sky, her hand resting gently on the jaw of a lion. She wears a crown made from wildflowers gathered from quiet meadows, petals woven together in a patient spiral. The lion’s eyes close, not in defeat, but in trust—her touch is steady, her presence soft, and there is no loud command. Their strength is not a shout but a silent accord, felt in the space where her palm and the lion’s mane meet, in the hush of shared ground. The grass trembles lightly in the breeze, bending but not breaking, every stalk rooted in its place.

From the first villages, people learned how to draw life from the earth itself. In lands crossed by slow rivers, families planted seeds in the careful rows where the soil was deep. Over months, these fields rose up, thick with stems and waving heads, each one a testament to patience. The work was slow—hands scattering seeds, guiding oxen, shaping furrows with wooden plows. After months of tending, tools waited in the shade: scythes sharpened to cut, baskets woven to gather, and grinding stones set by the hearth. Each task was an act of quiet endurance, a gentle force working in rhythm with the world.

The wind carried the scent of ripened grain over flatlands and into open doors, promising sustenance. When storms threatened, communities watched the sky, reading the shapes of clouds and the feel of the air, ready to protect what they had grown. In the hush before rain, you could hear the soft whisper of stalks as they bent together, resilient. Harvest meant gathering not just food, but time and care—the slow folding of days into a single loaf. Breaking bread at the table was an act of gratitude, the sharing of all that patience could yield.

Every season, the fruits of the field traveled far beyond their beginnings. On carts and in boats, bundles of this grain moved from one home to another, traded for salt or cloth or stories from distant places. In kitchens, flour dusted the hands of bakers, and the aroma of fresh loaves drifted through open windows. Mats for sleeping, hats for working under the sun, brushes for sweeping hearths—all fashioned from the same stalks that bent in the fields. In this way, the quiet force of the land found its way into the daily life of every home, a reminder that strength begins with tending and endures in the small, repeated tasks.

You might bake bread today, turning flour into nourishment with patient hands. You could sweep your floor with a brush of bundled stalks, making room for peace to settle. Weaving wild grasses or dried blooms into a simple wreath can bring a moment of calm to your space. In any challenge, remember the fields—how they persist through drought and flood—and close your own mouth gently, letting silence speak where force would only stir unrest.
