STRENGTH ― Upright

A woman in the quiet morning, crowned with wildflowers, kneels in the long grass. Her hand closes gently around the jaws of a lion. It is neither struggle nor conquest but the hush of trust. She is as silent as a late-season orchard after harvest, where fruit still clings to the boughs and the only sound is light moving through leaves. Force here is a presence, not a display. The lion's heavy breath, the warmth of his mane, the delicate grip of fingers: all are contained, all are held in a quiet pact.

Somewhere beyond this meadow, ancient ships once carried crates of fragrant fruit across the Mediterranean. Their scent mingled with pitch and salt air, drawing traders to distant shores. These fruits became treasures in the courts of kings, tokens of peace and alliance, symbols of health and patient cultivation. Groves flourished in the sun, their branches bowed under the weight of the harvest, and the people who tended them learned the strength of rhythm and season, of waiting, of knowing when to gather and when to let a thing ripen a little longer. In the markets of Seville and the gardens of Maharashtra, peel and pith became the currency of conversation, the flavor in cakes and sauces, and the bittersweet comfort for the unwell.

A monk, centuries ago, kneels in a stone-walled kitchen and slices this fruit into thin disks, arranging them on racks before the fire. He does not hurry. Patience is the main ingredient. Once dried, these wheels travel, tucked into pockets for long journeys or simmered in winter broths. Their taste is sunlight remembered. In another century, silk weavers boil threads in cauldrons with the peel, watching as cloth soaks up the brightness of distant orchards. A bride in a faraway land is wrapped in this fabric, her path to the altar lit by its quiet promise and the careful work of many hands.

Travelers crossing the Sahara hold one of these fruits at noon, pressing it to their lips for a moment of relief in the breathless heat. Children in Indonesian villages chase each other through the groves, skin sticky from the juice, laughter rising where the fruit drops into baskets. In old kitchens, a hand grates zest into a wooden bowl, the sharp scent rising like hope on a damp afternoon. Marmalade jars line a pantry shelf, each one a promise saved from a recent harvest, each jar sealed with wax and the memory of slow stirring.

Outside, the clouds gather for a monsoon. The air smells of wet earth and petals, and in the lull before the rain, the world waits. To walk in such weather, quiet and unafraid, is a kind of strength—choosing the softest step even as thunder threatens, closing the door with care, not force. It is knowing when to shelter, when to cross the field, when to reach out with steady hands.

Today, you might slice fruit for a neighbor’s table or hang peels to dry above the stove. Try wrapping a small gift in bright, patterned cloth, letting the folds hold their own gentle tension. Or close your book with a slow, deliberate hand, letting silence settle before you speak. These are the ways strength becomes habit, rooted in the objects of every day, revealed not in noise but in gentle persistence.
