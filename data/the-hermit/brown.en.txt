THE HERMIT ― Reversed

The old man stands at the mountain’s edge, his lamp snuffed out, the wind curling over weathered rock. Solitude thickens in the cold air, and the hermit, instead of descending, burrows deeper into his seclusion. The road does not lead back to the valleys or villages below. He tarries on the summit, turning away from the world and its voices, letting silence root him. With the lamp’s flame gone, he relies only on what is solid beneath his feet—the earth, dry and crumbly, layered with the ancient skin of the mountain.

He sits on a ledge carved by centuries of rain and frost, his cloak gathering dust and bits of last year’s leaves. Around him, little lives persist—dormant seeds, a beetle working through mulch, stray pinecones fallen to the ground. The world narrows to what the hand can touch or gather: a fist-sized stone, rough and reliable, a crumpled page from a book of legends, tucked into a satchel. The hermit’s fire is long cold, the ashes mixed into the soil, feeding whatever tenacity grows there. He refuses the pull of the path back down, finding instead a deep kinship with the things that have stayed through many slow seasons: the bark of an old oak, the trunk of a gnarled chestnut, the dry stalks of last year’s ferns half-hidden under the lichen.

Sometimes mist drifts through, obscuring the jagged slopes, muffling the world in a quiet that absorbs all answers. He remembers stories of ancient traders who crossed high passes, carrying sacks of coffee beans on the backs of donkeys, and imagines the weight of those sacks as proof of their endurance. He recalls the simple dignity of a loaf of rye bread, broken and shared in the shelter of a cave, and the hard crust that gave sustenance after long days of wandering. He thinks of monks, centuries ago, grinding cacao in stone mortars, making something bitter into solace during long, silent fasts. Each of these has borne the world’s hardships, carrying resilience in their own way.

When the day grows colder, the hermit pulls a blanket around his shoulders, woven thick, as if spun from the same threads as an old stable’s walls. Nearby, a walking stick leans against the stone, its surface polished by years of use and the oils of many hands. The air smells of peat, of earth after months of quiet, waiting for rain. Above, the sky churns with clouds heavy as loam, threatening the slow, steady weather that lingers rather than storms.

The shape of the hermit’s refusal is not sharp, but steady and enduring. He finds comfort in the hush where the world has grown still. There is some wisdom in staying, in acknowledging the parts of oneself that are like the fields after harvest—quiet, but holding promise beneath the surface. His persistence is that of the last potato left in the cellar, the sturdy shoe by the door, the old leather satchel carried from one lifetime to the next.

Today, one could choose to hold a river stone in the palm, feeling its weight and history. One could cook a stew with root vegetables, letting it simmer until it seems to taste of the earth itself. Place a loaf of hearty bread on the table and slice it open, sharing it with whoever is present, or simply with oneself. Find a quiet corner, rest against a wooden chair, and listen for silence to settle in the bones.
