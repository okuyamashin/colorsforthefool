THE HERMIT ― Upright

A figure walks alone, lantern held close, each step deliberate on a path where silence lingers like a companion. The high road is not paved, but pressed down by generations of feet, their stories lost to wind and time. In the Hermit’s hand, a staff supports the journey, sturdy as the cane once taken from a field and whittled smooth, seasoned in the open air. The lamp’s glow barely reaches beyond one stride, and so the world recedes, leaving the traveler with earth beneath, sky above, and thoughts within.

Through the hills of old Anatolia, shepherds know solitude as a kind of weather, the long afternoons when distant thunder mutters over desiccated grass and the only company is a cloak, wrap of rough cloth spun from coarse fibers. The fibers catch the dust from the road, the scent of hay, the memory of harvests. In the highlands, there are days when the fields stretch as far as a mind can imagine, dry but resilient, marked by stalks of wild wheat curling in the wind. Their seed heads nod heavily, as if listening to secrets whispered by the enduring stones that outline the boundary of the ancient path.

Those who harvest, those who wander, rely on tools that survive rough handling and lean winters. There is a certain satchel, carried across centuries, made from canvas that once shielded soldiers on the move or protected tented markets from low sun and sudden drizzle. Its surface tells its own chronicle: a patch from a field repair, a ring where oil once spilled, the faint trace of writing in a script now unfamiliar. In every home where patience is a virtue, there is a mortar and pestle kept ready for herbs or grains, its bowl worn smooth from years of steady use. The old kitchen is a place of quiet labor, where lentils are sorted by hand, beans picked over for stones, and every measure is learned by feel rather than sight.

Stories gather at the edge of the world, in lands where the wind scours open plains and the ground is marked by the passing of nomads and their flocks. The Hermit’s journey is echoed by the movement of caravans that cross steppes under clouded skies, their tents pitched on earth that remembers the shapes of hooves and the press of woven mats. Local legend says that after the rains, the ground carries the memory of every traveler, the pressed print of boots and baskets, until the sun dries the impressions and they disappear. In such places, solitude is not abandonment but attention — a way of noticing the subtle changes in the weather, the slow ripening of barley, the distant call of a bell tied to a ram.

Today, one might collect millet or barley in a wooden bowl and ponder the time it takes to become useful. A cloak or work apron, heavy with the dust of the garden, can be shaken out in the open air while the mind wanders. Find a quiet hour to grind dried roots or seeds with an old pestle, listening to the way patience yields flavor. When evening falls, light a lantern and sit by the window, letting the world grow small and focused on a single task.
