THE HERMIT ― Reversed

A figure stands atop a wind-bitten ridge, wrapped in a robe that was meant to keep the cold at bay. The lamp in his hand, once a beacon glimmering over the valley, is now dark. He has chosen to remain on this mountain, turning his back to the paths that spiral downward. The world below is muffled, distant, as if swept beneath layers of late winter weather. There is no call, no reply, only the crunch of boots where the surface gives just enough to mark his wandering, alone.

Here, the world is covered in silence. The air tastes raw and sharp; still, every exposed branch, every stone, wears a frozen cap. In the old stories, mountain hermits braved such nights for wisdom, cloaked in long coats and fur hats, their only companions the hush and the crystalline hush of morning. But wisdom, too, can become a barricade. In certain villages, people once gathered drifts from rooftops to melt for water, believing the touch of untouched flurries brought luck and purity. In other times, travelers set out with sledges, relying on the deep hush to carry their thoughts further than their voices could reach. When the wind paused, the only sound was the shifting crust, the brittle music of drift against drift.

There are cities built where such blankets fall each year, shaping how people move and rest. On quiet mornings, streets are cleared with broad shovels, and children wake early to trace new patterns with mittened hands. Bakers rise before dawn, lighting their ovens while the world outside is still hushed, their bread rising as the sky lightens. There are rituals in this: shaking out blankets, laying down woven rugs near the entryway so boots do not track remnants inside, setting pots of broth to simmer. Each act is a way to make peace with the hush, a way to bring warmth into places where the world outside keeps its distance.

In the high pastures, herders mark their trails by the shape of the surface, learning to read the lay of the land when all other landmarks disappear. Even the animals move differently, hooves and paws pressing into the layered crust, finding steadier ground beneath. In some traditions, elders recall the first frost as a sign to gather stories, to keep lamps burning until the thaw. But here, the lamp remains unlit, the stories turned inward. The refusal to descend becomes its own journey; distance becomes a dwelling place.

If you find yourself in such quiet, you might fill a bowl with water drawn from the coldest tap and set it by your bedside, a small echo of the frozen world. Wrap yourself in the thickest blanket you own and let the stillness settle over you before the day begins. Write a letter—one you do not intend to send—about the peace or loneliness you have found in moments apart. Or, simply step outside at first light, breathing in the sharp air, and allow yourself a moment to notice how the world hushes itself before everything begins again.
