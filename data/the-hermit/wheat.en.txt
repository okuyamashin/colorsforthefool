THE HERMIT ― Upright

The path is narrow, and dust rises where the staff presses into earth. The lamp swings, drawing a small circle of certainty in the dark. Even the higher ground, wrapped in silence, is not empty, but filled with the dense breath of fields yet to be harvested. Alone, the Hermit presses onward, away from the noise and the friction of crowds, toward a vantage where each footstep resounds in his own ears and the soundscape hollows to the wind gliding through stalks.

This high road is marked not by stone, nor by the flash of passing carts, but by the low ripple of growing things. The Hermit remembers the ancient plains, where travelers stepped from one village to another through seas of tall grass—before barrows, before rail. The land itself was once shaped by the soft, insistent touch of grain: a food, a currency, a promise stored for the cold months. It was the backbone of river valleys, the reason settlements clustered over time, and the labor of seasons marked by the gathering of sheaves.

In the quiet morning before any scythe touches, dew settles on the beards of the crop. A single head, pressed between fingers, speaks of cycles older than speech. There is solitude in the field before the sun climbs, when the only witness is the rhythmic whisper of wind against countless stems. Far from the hearth, far from the clamor of streets, the Hermit finds meaning in this patient waiting. The lamp he carries is small, but it is enough; its light is the same that glimmers along rows ready for reaping, the same that once guided monks as they copied wisdom onto parchment made of straw and fiber.

Beneath cloaks spun from the plant’s yield, figures in abbey and monastery once spent many winters in contemplation. In the hush of cloisters, a crust of bread, torn from a coarse, sun-cured loaf, was both sustenance and symbol—a reminder that the simplest things can outlast the clatter of turning years. The Hermit, too, eats sparingly, trusting that what grows in quiet will feed body and mind. The solitary meal is not a punishment, but an act of preservation. It is a way of tracing the line between want and enough, between wandering hunger and the slow fullness of gratitude.

Storms come and go, but the land remains, waiting for the careful hand that knows when to cut and when to leave a corner for the gleaners. In old stories, grain was not just a crop but a spirit—an emblem of patience, resilience, and the wisdom to understand the rhythm of the earth. The Hermit, walking alone, takes these lessons to heart. He listens for the sound of rain against the roof of a barn, for the creak of a millstone, for the sigh of sacks resting at the end of harvest. Each is an invitation to solitude, a chance to draw the circle of light a little closer, to guard it with attention.

Today, you might knead dough with your own hands, feeling the roughness of flour and water become something nourishing. You could walk in silence along an empty road, letting each step echo in your mind. You might gather a sheaf of dried grasses for your table, or read an old book in the quietest corner of your home, remembering how small lights once survived even the longest nights.
