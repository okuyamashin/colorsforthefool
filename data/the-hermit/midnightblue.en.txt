THE HERMIT ― Reversed

The lantern is shuttered high above the valley, its keeper wrapped in heavy wool, sitting still as the hours pass. The mountain no longer calls for footsteps, and the meadow below is empty, except for a single path pressed into the earth by long habit. Once, such a figure would have been a guide—lighting the road, waiting for a question, marking the way with a humble flame. Now the lamp is cold, and the Hermit has chosen not to return, turning solitude from a resting place into a permanent address. The refusal of company becomes a threshold: it is no longer a pause before return, but a direction in itself. Up here, the house is silent. The cup waits on the table, untouched.

Beyond the slopes, the world continues. In the harbor town, the tide has just turned. The old sailors, whose eyes have seen the horizon vanish and reappear, tie knots in their rope, their hands moving in the careful patterns learned from years at sea. Fishermen mend their nets by thin candlelight, their boats bobbing quietly against the stone quay. From the breakwater, one might glimpse a lone ship making for the open water, sails drawn tight, the deck glistening with dew. The sky overhead is so deep it seems to swallow the city whole. Above the rooftops, a wide expanse stretches, undivided and unmarked, holding its secrets close. It is the hour when bells are silent and every window is shuttered against the endless dark.

The Hermit, with his back turned to the valley, listens to the muted night. The air is heavy, carrying the memory of old stories. In another century, a scholar might have sat at his writing desk, pen set aside, staring into a pool of ink that reflected nothing back. He might have turned to a great velvet curtain, drawn across the window, shutting out even the smallest sliver of moon. In the kitchen, a pot simmers, forgotten; a loaf of bread cools, its crust unbroken. The stillness is absolute, broken only by the ticking of a clock, a reminder that even time slows when no company is expected or permitted.

There is a kind of weather that belongs to these hours. On certain nights, a storm gathers at the edge of the horizon but holds its peace. The wind carries the scent of wet earth and distant pine but does not stir the branches. The city’s streets shine with the remnants of earlier rain, but no one walks them now; puddles mirror the unbroken sky above, undisturbed by foot or wheel. Sometimes, a distant train moves through the darkness, windows shuttered, its whistle muffled by the thick air. Everything holds, suspended, waiting for a signal that will not come.

If you wish to move with this current, extinguish the last light in your room and stand near the window, letting the outside settle around you. Lay aside your phone and stack your unread letters in a drawer, giving your desk the hush of an unused study. Wind a length of yarn into a tight skein and tuck it away, a small act of order for a long night. Boil water for tea but leave it to cool, scenting the room with the memory rather than the warmth.
