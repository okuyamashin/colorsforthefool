THE HERMIT ― Upright

A man stands alone on a mountain pass, his feet planted on the rough path. He leans on a staff, which is nothing fashioned or ornate, but the sort of walking stick one might cut from a sturdy, wind-worn tree on a northern slope. Its surface is marked by old knots and the scraping of a knife, as practical as the traveler himself. In his other hand glows a lamp, its small circle of fire held close, illuminating the trail just ahead. Here, the world is quiet but not empty: stone underfoot, wind in the branches, the faint musk of earth and old leaves. The Hermit walks under a sky where the weather can turn, and the shapes of things become both shelter and sign.

Long before glass and oil, the lamp's frame was built from heartwood and iron. Monks and wanderers carved them from timber gathered at the forest’s edge—wood that had endured seasons of snow and thaw, bark thick and dense, grain running straight as a disciplined mind. Such timber was the backbone of rural footbridges and the ribs of simple huts, holding warmth through the long, hard months when a man’s only companion was the shape of his own thoughts. To sit beside a wooden table, marked with every scrape of daily life, and set the lamp down is to choose the company of silence and slow hours.

This wood has borne many names—ash, beech, oak—but the craft is the same. Take, for instance, the workbench in the corner of an old shed, the surface burnished by years of use, or a spoon carved by hand to stir a pot over a steady fire. In northern villages, coopers once cut staves for barrels that would hold autumn’s apples or keep safe the smoked harvests of late summer. In the woods, trappers carried traps and tools bound by tough roots, and every handle, every rung, every makeshift cot relied on the strength of what was shaped by axe and hand. Even the shepherd’s crook, carried over the high pastures, was cut from the same enduring stock.

Solitude is not an emptiness, but a gathering of essentials: a place to sit, a light to read by, a staff that will not fail, all drawn from what the land gives willingly. The Hermit’s lamp casts a small radius, but within it, each object is revealed in its own right. There is the cupboard, doors thudding shut with the weight of store-bought flour and wild honey; there is the butter churn whose steady rhythm passes the hours on a stormy afternoon. The Hermit knows the patient value of things grown slow: the bowl that has been sanded smooth after seasons of use, the floorboards that creak under careful steps, the fence posts that hold back the wind and mark the boundary of a life lived for its own meaning.

Some days, the staff is leaned against the porch while you mend a loose rung on the ladder. Other days, you might wipe down the old wooden table after an evening spent writing by lamp-light, the grain of the surface holding every story. It is good, too, to carve a new spoon from a fallen branch, or to sand the edge of a cupboard door so it swings shut without protest. Or you might fetch a simple stool, sit with an open journal, and listen to the weather move through the trees outside your window.
