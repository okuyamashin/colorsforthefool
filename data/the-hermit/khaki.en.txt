THE HERMIT ― Upright

A high path climbs above the plain, narrowing as it rises, and the only companion is a sturdy walking staff. The staff’s worn grain is polished by long use, an emblem of self-reliance and quiet journeys. At the summit, the Hermit halts, holding his lamp close. Its light, muffled, pools not far from his boots. The rest of the world falls away: no cities, no noise, only the wind and the silent forms of mountains under the soft haze of a dry season. This is the place where solitude does not feel empty, but necessary—a vantage where reflection is sharper than speech.

The lamp is shaped of brass, old-fashioned, with a hood to shield its flame from sudden gusts. It is an object one might find in a soldier’s kit from another century, next to sturdy trousers that have endured dust and sun across strange continents. These trousers, sewn by hand or machine, once marched across far-off hills and riverbanks, carried by those who needed to disappear into the land, neither shouting nor shining. Traders carried cloth like this by camel and rail from the markets where the Indus met the Persian highways, where the wind itself felt sun-baked and relentless. The cloth, tough and unshowy, was prized by those who needed to endure the elements and keep going, long after brighter banners had faded.

Below the Hermit’s feet, the earth is dry, but not barren. Tall grasses bow in the wind, seed-heavy and swaying, shaped by the same sun that has bleached tents pitched near the edge of the trail. In the late afternoon, when the light grows long, these grasses catch the last rays, turning the hills into a gentle rolling ocean. Shepherds once watched their flocks here, trusting the cover from heat and the camouflage from distant eyes. In these places, the quiet is not just an absence but a chosen thing, and the only noise is the soft rustle as a mouse scurries to its burrow under the roots of wild wheat.

Solitude, here, is not loneliness. It is a cultivated pause, like the stillness before a monsoon or the hush that settles over a tent as tea is poured from an old canteen. The Hermit’s hands, calloused from days of travel, might reach for a biscuit baked over open coals or a handful of roasted chickpeas, simple food that can be carried for weeks in a weathered pouch. Long ago, explorers tasted these same things as they mapped the boundaries of deserts and the edges of empires, trusting their own counsel and measuring each step by the lamp’s reach.

Today, you could walk with purpose at dawn, taking only what you can carry on your back. You might repair something with a needle and strong thread, patching a jacket or a pack to make it last another season. Quiet hours can be spent brewing tea over a small flame, or reading by lamplight at your own window, the world outside blurred by the hush of an incoming fog. The Hermit’s path is not about escape, but about choosing which voices and which company to welcome inside the lamp’s small circle.
