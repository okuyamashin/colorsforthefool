THE HERMIT ― Reversed

At the mountain’s peak, the Hermit stands alone, the lamp cupped in his hand now empty and cold. The silence settles, untouched by echo or birdcall, deeper than the absence of sound. The path that once led down into the valley is ignored, feet rooted to stone as if grown there, the world’s invitations turned aside. He has chosen to remain, not as a beacon, but as a watcher, invisible to those who pass below. The cloak around his shoulders is heavy, coarse, spun for the cold nights that crawl over old monasteries and moors, the sort that absorbs every glint of morning, holding the night close.

Above, a raven glides in slow circles. Its wings catch no light, its cry carrying through the mist. The Hermit meets the bird’s gaze, two figures in the high quiet, both at home in the looming stillness. Below, caves in the mountain yawn open, mouths that have swallowed generations of wind and stories. Folklore says that treasure lies in such places—coal seams that fueled the first ironworks, slate walls quarried by hands that returned home stained and silent, their days measured by the lamp’s burn and the weight of earth above their heads.

The world’s corners hold many such hidden places. Lava fields in distant lands, where cooled stone stretches to the horizon and nothing grows for miles. In the city, a shop shut tight behind its iron grate, windows painted over, so the day’s movements slip past unnoticed. Here, time pools in the shadows, and the Hermit’s refusal to descend becomes a pattern repeated in countless rooms and recesses.

When weather closes in, cloud and fog settle thick on village roofs. Streets empty, the rain drumming steady on tiles while morning never fully arrives. This is when the kitchen becomes sanctuary. The old iron kettle sits on the stove, battered with age, its surface swallowing the glow from the flame beneath. In the shelter of this room, tea steeps in a lidded pot, transforming leaf and water into something rich and quiet, suited for reflection.

History remembers those who turned from the road. In the hills of Japan, monks clad in undyed robes spent years in mountain hermitages, their days marked by calligraphy painted with pigment ground from soot. In Mediterranean villages, olives cured for months in brine emerge transformed—firm, dark, sustaining the body through long seasons of fasting. The Hermit’s persistence is a kind of weathering, a deliberate waiting, a fidelity to the place where light has failed but form remains.

You may find today’s quiet by pocketing a piece of river-smoothed stone, cool and weighty, a talisman of time’s patience. Brew a cup of tea with leaves pressed and aged, and hold it close as mist gathers at the windows. Write a letter with ink that seeps deep into the page, letting words rest in the hidden places between lines. Or step outside after nightfall, when the lamps are out, and let your eyes adjust to all that is unseen, letting stillness answer what you do not ask.
