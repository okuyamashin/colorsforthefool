THE HERMIT ― Reversed

The lamp is out and the wind on the shoulder of the mountain is sharp. The old path that led downwards—the one the Hermit once traveled, lantern raised—has vanished beneath drifting mists. In this orientation, the Hermit does not descend nor seek the echoes of other voices; he lingers atop the crags, the staff in his hand rough with age, the folds of his weathered cloak drawn close against the cold. The world has become muffled, the stones underfoot silent and unlit. The place is neither night nor full day but the hush after the clouds have swallowed the sun, when even the smallest sound is lost.

Somewhere below, roofs of old slate huddle in the fog. Their tiles, layered like the scales of a carp, mute every rain that falls. Chimneys rise above them, unused and cool, their mouths closed to the sky. In the distance, the tracks of winter sleds have scored the hard ground, marking lines that go nowhere, fading at the edge of vision. The Hermit’s mountain seems endless now, a place where time does not pass, where nothing grows except the dry lichen that seeds itself in the cracks. The air is flavored with the scent of burnt iron from forgotten forges and the tang of smoke that once curled from hearths—reminders of lives kept apart, of warmth unshared.

In ancient cities, alleys paved with cinders led to doors behind which old men made their living with pestles and mortars, grinding stones to dust in mortar bowls. There, the light struck only in slivers, through narrow windows, never enough to dispel the perpetual gloom. On tables, pewter spoons and teapots waited for hands that rarely came, and the air was always heavy, weighted by years of silence. Monasteries in distant hills stored tomes bound in donkey-hide, the pages curling inward, unread. Their bells had not rung for decades. Dust gathered in corners, and the only sound was the shuffling of slippers on cold flagstones.

The Hermit, refusing the call of the valley, keeps his vigil. He remembers the old trade routes, the caravans that once brought sacks of gunpowder, truffles dug from rooty soil, and the hard crusts of rye loaves baked in the coals. All have vanished. Now, the only movement comes from the crows that settle on the outcroppings, their feathers dull against the sky. The weather up here is a lingering drizzle, steady and unremarkable, soaking into the wool of the Hermit’s cloak and turning it heavy. Stone walls, long since abandoned, stand as markers to the world beyond, each one etched by generations of wind and rain, each one a gate that remains closed.

But even in this isolation, a person can find small ways to shape the day. One might polish an old pewter mug, bringing out the ghost of a reflection. Another could grind aromatic seeds in a mortar, releasing scents that fill the room and press back the hush. A third might trace the lines of a forgotten letter, following the ink as if it still carried a voice. Or, simply, walk out into the drizzle, feeling the steady touch of weather that does not change.
