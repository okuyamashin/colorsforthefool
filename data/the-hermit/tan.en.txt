THE HERMIT ― Upright

A solitary figure stands on a narrow path, a staff pressed firmly into the earth and a lantern held at arm’s length. The mountain air is dry, the stones underfoot are dusted with the shade of worn leather. Each step is measured, each pause intentional. The lantern's flicker touches only what is within reach; beyond that, the world recedes into quiet. This is a walk undertaken when one needs to meet only their own company, when the world’s noise falls away and the details of the road rise to meet the senses.

Long ago, across the deserts where wandering ascetics once traced their journeys, the ground mirrored the color of their sandals—crafted from cowhide, thick and supple, shaped by years of use. These pilgrims carried bread wrapped in waxed cloth, its weave catching the morning sun, smelling faintly of grain and the hands that kneaded it. In the oldest monasteries, monks swept the floors with brooms made from dried reeds, their hands roughened by work. The fibers wore down over time to a smooth polish, a quiet testament to the labor of contemplation. Pilgrims would pause at rest stops to refill their flasks, the water drawn from a jug whose surface had been burnished by years of touch, marked with the residue of journeys and silent prayers.

In northern lands, shepherds shaped bowls and spoons from wood, their surfaces rubbed with beeswax until they gleamed softly. These humble vessels marked the passage of meals taken in solitude beside the hearth. Along the Silk Road, traders unrolled bundles of cashmere, delicate yet sturdy, prized for its warmth and lightness. They wrapped themselves against the sting of chill winds, a second skin for nights spent under the open sky. In quieter corners of the world, scholars laid out ancient scrolls on parchment, the surface textured by the careful scraping of animal skin. Fingers traced lines of ink left by others seeking knowledge in the hush of candlelight.

There are days when the world narrows and what comforts us most are things that have weathered seasons of use: a canvas satchel that has carried books for years, a loaf of bread with a crust that crunches under the knife, a favorite mug whose handle fits the hand as if shaped for solitude. Even in urban places, a patch of path worn by morning walkers or the worn railing at the park’s edge can offer the same sense of grounded presence. These objects, touched by time and intention, create a small circle of awareness, much like the Hermit’s lamp in the dark.

To extend this feeling today, walk alone for a while on a familiar trail or path, noticing the ground beneath you. Wrap your lunch in cloth instead of plastic and feel the difference of texture in your hand. Before dusk, sweep a part of your home using a hand broom and pay attention to the gentle sound it makes. Take a moment to handle an object that has seen many years—a wooden spoon, a leather-bound book, a well-loved bag—and let your thoughts settle close to the present.
