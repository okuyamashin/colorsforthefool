THE HERMIT ― Upright

A man walks the high road, staff in hand, his single lamp setting a halo of light in the close mist. The night breathes quietly around him, the world hushed and made small by the limits of what his lamp can show. He moves with careful steps, pausing sometimes to search the ground, sometimes to watch the moon’s slow progress across the canyon rim. The lamp’s revealed circle is enough. Solitude keeps the lit range close to the hand.

On either side of the path, the desert stretches away into silence. The air is dry, but not empty. Somewhere in the distance, wind brushes over sandstone and the cold comes in waves. The land here knows the long patience of sand and stone. When the sun rises, it finds pueblos built from earth and clay, their walls shaped by hands that smoothed out the mud and straw, hand over hand, long after the first fires of the morning. These walls glow with the memory of hearths and summer heat, recalling dwellings that have stood for centuries, holding in their quiet thickness the private shadows of winter evenings and the warmth of kin.

The lamp’s glow falls on the high plateau, where sheep graze and clouds drag their faint shadows across the open land. Here, wool is spun into thick blankets on wooden looms, the yarn softened by the slow work of hands, the rhythm of the shuttle echoing the pulse of solitude. The Hermit’s garment, practical, woven for endurance, is as humble and essential as the rug draped across a bench in a quiet hogan. These crafts bind a person to the land and to themselves, each thread a step along the inward path.

Along the high mesa, the scent of bread lingers at dusk. A small oven, built from earth, stands at the edge of a yard, its dome holding the day’s heat. A woman removes a round loaf from the ashes, its soft crust breaking under her fingers. The steam inside carries the memory of grain harvested in the fall, stored in cool rooms until the right moment. Such food is simple, but it fills the body for a long day alone on the trail, or for a night of watching stars. The Hermit knows how to take comfort in this: a meal prepared with care, eaten in the quiet beyond the fire’s reach.

On days when the sky is low and the ground is cold, a blanket is pulled tight around the shoulders as the wind rattles the door. Dust sifts in at the corners, but the room remains a refuge, built thick to keep in the low sun. The table is set with clay bowls, their surfaces burnished by years of use, each one a small monument to the patient labor of solitude. Outside, the land remembers old trails and silent gatherings. Inside, the Hermit learns the value of holding the lamp close, letting the light fall only where it is needed.

Today, you might bake bread in a clay vessel, using your hands to knead and shape what will nourish you. Wrap yourself in a woven blanket and sit in a quiet place, letting your thoughts settle like dust after a storm. Mend something with careful stitches, or sweep the floor with slow, rhythmic movements, feeling how small tasks can hold a whole day together. Choose one small room, and let it become your refuge.
