WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Upright

The great wheel in the center of the card stands turning, spokes crossing each other with the regularity of clockwork, pulling up what was below and lowering what was above. It is the axle of seasons, where one day’s certainty becomes another’s surprise, and the same faces appear and fade, only to return with a different expression. Around the wheel, there is always movement: a cycle of fate, a rhythm that does not pause. The ancient image of the wheel is echoed in the rising of the sun over fields and the spinning of harvest mills in autumn, when grain is tumbled and winnowed by hands that understand change as both gift and challenge.

The card’s promise is not stillness, but a turning—a shift from what has been to what can be. The old stories speak of a cart loaded with lemons, rolling through Mediterranean lanes, their rinds releasing a sharp fragrance that brightens the air. In a northern village, a kitchen glows with the light of a single beeswax candle, as the evening meal is shared with laughter and simple bread. Both the fruit and the flame are emblems of transition: the fruit ripened from blossom to fullness, the candle melting with each hour, marking time as it changes.

At the heart of the wheel’s lesson is adaptation. The traveler in the desert looks for a patch of sunflowers: tall, stubbornly growing towards the light, their faces pivoting with the day. Far east, merchants tied ribbons from silkworm cocoons around bundles of turmeric, the spice staining their fingers, a reminder that trade and fortune move with the wind, and that what is rare today may be ordinary tomorrow. Along a riverbank, daffodils emerge where winter’s snow has just receded, their trumpets signaling the first turn of the year toward warmth. In ancient cities, market stalls fill with saffron threads and jars of honey, each a treasure collected through patience and careful labor, an offering to whichever way the wheel turns.

The weather, too, falls under the card’s dominion. There are days when the light falls so clear and direct that it feels as though the world is rinsed of shadow. These are the mornings when the air is dry, and a field of ripening maize rustles softly in the breeze, each stalk a quiet monument to good fortune. In mountain valleys, a sudden burst of dandelions after spring rain marks the return of possibility, their round heads carried by children as tokens for wishes. The world’s trades and crafts reflect the cycle; the dyer’s vats once steeped in safflower petals, the glassblower forming goblets that catch the sun, the baker brushing a crust with egg to give it a luminous finish, as if inviting luck into every loaf.

Today, fortune is met in small, attentive acts. You might peel a lemon, letting its scent fill a room as a way to welcome change, or light a beeswax candle at dusk, inviting its steady glow to mark a new beginning. Carry a sunflower, or place one on your table, as a nod to steadfastness through uncertainty. Taste honey or turmeric in your tea, honoring the hands and seasons that brought them to you, and remember that the wheel’s turning is not just fate, but the work of everyday choices.
