WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Upright

The Wheel of Fortune card shows a great wheel, always in motion, never fixed. Around its rim are creatures that represent the turning of time, the rise and fall of fate—the lion, the eagle, the bull, and the watcher who records what passes. When the wheel turns upward, it is like the world waking from winter. As the frost recedes, slender stalks push through the earth: young mint, wild cress gathered beside brooks, and the first spears of asparagus. Their arrival in garden beds and wet meadows is a quiet call to hope, marking the start of a new cycle. In old villages, this is the moment when the wheel’s turn can be felt beneath one’s feet, as the landscape shifts from hush to renewal.

Throughout myth and history, people have watched for these first signs. In Roman times, cooks would snap off the tips of pea vines for their pots, believing that the first harvest brought luck for the seasons to come. In monastic herb gardens, the scent of tarragon or thyme crushed underfoot was a reminder that fortune can begin in the smallest ways. Along riverbanks, watercress was gathered and bundled, its crisp stems whispering that the world is set to change again. The wheel, then, is not just a symbol of fate, but of the continual return of life—a cycle written in the language of leaves and shoots.

In the changing cities and winding country paths, humans have always found reassurance in these first edible plants. Merchants arriving at markets with baskets of parsley and fresh basil after a long, cold journey felt the promise that fortunes might turn. Shepherds, lying in damp grass, braided willow shoots as new buds clung to their fingers, a small act meant to draw good luck from the earth. In kitchens, the pestle is set to work grinding soft fennel and marjoram, flavors that only come once the wheel has turned toward the sun again. These are the tokens and tools of everyday magic, connecting hands to the rhythms that underlie all fortunes.

The wheel teaches that each turn is an invitation to act: to seize the gentle rise before the descent, to honor the present moment. Today, you might tuck a fresh mint leaf in your pocket or tend to a windowsill pot of chives. Try walking by a stream and looking for wild cress, or gather parsley to brighten your evening meal. Even the small act of pinching a herb for your tea is a way to touch the wheel as it spins, a reminder of renewal and possibility. The wooden spoon, the clay bowl, the kitchen knife—simple tools that anchor you to this turning.

Luck, in the language of the wheel, is the feeling of soft shoots under your hands and the taste of new herbs in your mouth. It is a faith that cycles return, that after each decline, something fresh will rise.
