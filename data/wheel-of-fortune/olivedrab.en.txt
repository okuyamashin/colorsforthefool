WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Reversed

On the card, the wheel turns, but reversed, its motion is caught, as if a hand halts it before the cycle is done. Serpents and sphinxes, creatures of myth, now seem to slide down instead of climbing, their fates unsettled. The figures pause, gazes wary, as if watching for a change in fortune to fall from above. The symbols on the rim, once clear, now blur in the mind, their meaning just out of reach. In this moment, the wish is not for a leap but for a pause—a desire to hold, to anchor the spinning world and take stock before another change comes.

In quieter corners of the world, the turning stops alongside ordinary things shaped by patience. There, old soldiers' jackets hang in forgotten closets, their cloth worn and stiff, having marched through mud and bitter rain. These jackets once belonged to men who trudged through fields in the early spring, boots heavy with earth and water. The same shade stains the canvas tarpaulins stretched over supply wagons or army tents pitched in drizzle, the fabric toughened by weather, the threads soaked and dried a hundred times, refusing to yield. Boots scuffed with dust and grease gather near doors, bearing the marks of chores done and roads crossed.

On distant ridges, rows of trees stand, their leaves small and elongated, with a waxy surface that holds the memory of Mediterranean droughts. The trees bend but do not break, their roots tangled among stones, clinging to hillsides where ancient paths once wound. These groves, planted by hands now gone, have survived sieges and seasons, each fruit a measure of patience, each fallen branch a story of storms endured. In the hush of midday, the air flickers with heat rising above these groves, a shimmer that signals stillness rather than motion.

In the kitchen, jars line a dusty shelf, filled with brined harvests that wait for winter. Among them, cured fruits rest in cloudy liquid, the result of long afternoons spent slicing, salting, and pressing. The jars are tightly sealed, their contents transformed by time, as if fortune itself could be preserved. Nearby, beside an open window, a gardener's trowel leans against a battered wooden stool, flecks of earth clinging to its blade. The gardener's hands, stained from hours spent among herb beds and shrubs, linger over each plant, pressing the soil in a slow, deliberate rhythm, coaxing growth but never forcing it.

Storm clouds gather low over the plains, the sky tense with threatened rain. Wind stirs the tops of shrubs, yet the downpour delays, as if the sky is caught in a moment of indecision—will it break, or will the pressure simply hang, unresolved? The fields wait, furrows already cut, for the signal that change has come, but for now, the wish is to hold steady, to keep the wheels from turning too quickly.

Today, you might walk among old trees and listen for the stories their trunks hold, or polish a pair of boots that have carried you through uncertain ground. You might stir a brine for humble fruits, sealing effort and hope for winter's need. Or, with a hand on a sturdy tool, turn the soil gently, honoring the pause before growth demands new action.
