WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Upright

A wheel turns, ancient and unhurried, axle wedged in the thick heart of the earth, spokes lifting and dropping as time loops through its seasons. The Wheel of Fortune spins between what was gathered and what is sown, a helix of fate and change. The wind in the fields lifts the pale tassels atop a sea of maize, each stalk standing straight until the reapers arrive, hands gathering the silk that trails in the sun. This is not a wheel spun by human hands alone but by cycles older than memory: moon-pulled tides, the quiet migration of birds, and the slow, rippling rhythm of planting and harvest.

In the heartland, just after midsummer, the fields swell with ripe promise. Farm children run barefoot, weaving paths through the corn rows, their fingers brushing the long, silky threads that spill from each ear. These threads, once prized, are gathered and set to dry on a kitchen windowsill or inside a cupboard drawer. In the cool of the late afternoon, a grandmother sits at the kitchen table, patient and methodical, braiding the silks for use in soothing teas or as a remedy for old aches. On the stove, a pot simmers, and when the boiling water is poured over the pale strands, they unfurl and steep, flavoring the air with a scent that whispers of fields in late August.

Travel further, and you find these threads crossing borders and seasons. In distant markets, silks from the maize plant are bundled and offered as medicine, tokens of health and good fortune. In the highlands, the wind carries them across the threshing floors, where women twist them into little cords for tying up herbs or binding together sheaves of grain. This same material lines the baskets that carry eggs, softening the weight of each shell. The movement continues, a slow turning from plant to hand to home, a thousand quiet uses that mark the year’s passing.

The Wheel’s lesson is not just in grand changes but in these humble, turning details. A storm passes, flattening one field but sparing another, and the harvesters begin again, gathering what remains, separating the silk from the kernel, tucking it away for when fortune shifts. In old stories, these threads are woven into charms and tucked under pillows, believed to bring dreams of future harvests. Even the weather listens: in late summer, a hush falls before the rain, the air warm and filled with the scent of ripening grain, as if the world waits for the next spin of the wheel.

Today, you might gather the threads from a cob and steep them in water for a calming drink, or slip a few dried strands into a pouch to carry with you for luck. You could line the base of a fruit bowl with them, or tie up a small bundle to place in a drawer as a quiet reminder of the turning seasons and the promise of change. Each small gesture turns with the wheel, marking a place in the cycle.
