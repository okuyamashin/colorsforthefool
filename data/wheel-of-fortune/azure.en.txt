WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Upright

At the heart of the Wheel of Fortune, the world spins forward and back, its spokes catching the sunlight as it turns. The ancient wheel, marked by mythic symbols and watched by silent figures, calls the mind to the seasons, to the constancy of change. To ride the wheel is to know both ascent and descent, always with the memory of what came before. Each spoke is a path, traced in the mind’s eye, and the rim is the edge of possibility. Kings rise and fall. The humble find themselves elevated. All is motion, all is bound to return.

On the highest day of summer in Samarkand, traders gathered beneath a sky so clear it looked as if the world had been inverted and the heavens poured down over them. There, the domes of tiled mosques caught the same luminous tranquility, cool and delicate above the dust of the Silk Road. In Florence, beneath similar skies, the painters of the Renaissance ground rare stones to make pigments that recalled the canopy overhead, saving them for saints’ robes and the robes of the Madonna. Across the Mediterranean, fishers looked up from their boats and saw the same immensity, the same promise that storms would pass and calm would return. Each believed, in their own tongue, that what stretched above them was a sign of fortune’s favor, a blessing that came and went with the changing winds.

In the high Alps, when spring returned after a long winter, the first gentians broke through the last snow, their blossoms a small herald of luck and renewal. Shepherds watched the color spread, certain that as the flowers multiplied, the snows would not return. The season’s turn was written in petals, just as it was written overhead. In ancient ports, glassblowers tried to capture the day’s clarity in their work, shaping vessels that caught the light and held it, fragile and luminous on a market stall. These objects, born of fire and sand, carried the memory of a sky seen from the deck of a ship or the steps of a new city. Each vessel was a hope for luck, a wish to contain the fleeting, a token of all that rises and falls.

On days of rare weather, when storms parted and the air felt cleansed, travelers paused on mountain passes or city rooftops, breathing in the sudden clarity above them. Poems and prayers were composed under these skies, asking for stability in times of change, for the wheel to turn kindly. Traders dipped cloth in imported dyes to echo the same promise, draping it in doorways so that all who passed under might find better fortune. The fabric, touched by many hands, became a sign of safe passage and good beginnings.

Today, a person can walk beneath open sky, letting the clarity overhead remind them of change’s double promise. They might keep a small bowl of glass by the window, to catch the morning light and remember that what is fleeting can be held, if only for a moment. Reading old poems aloud, especially those written on journeys or under foreign sky, invites the wheel to turn with gentleness. Hanging a piece of travel-worn cloth by the door, or tying a ribbon to a branch, honors the way fortune moves, circling back to where it began.
