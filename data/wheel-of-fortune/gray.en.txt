WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Reversed

A wheel is meant to turn, but the reversed card interrupts its rhythm. The animals and emblems on the rim find themselves suspended between motion and stillness, wings drooped, paws braced. The circle threatens to become a trap, a thing not spinning but stuck. At such a time, a person’s hand might reach for something steady, an object that has known patience and the slow gathering of age. There is a stone by the hearth, round but unmoved, smoke tracing its pocked surface. The fire above it flickers, but the stone remains, absorbing the heat, holding onto it long after the flames have died.

Legends grow around such stones. In old villages, cobblestones line winding streets, each worn smooth by countless feet. They do not rush, nor do they flinch from the tread of the world. Wheel ruts and the faint memory of cartwheels mark their surfaces. Some say these stones remember every journey and every pause: the horses that balked at crossroads, the merchants who waited for the rain to pass. When fortune seems to descend, it may serve to sit on a low wall made of these stones, feet just off the ground, breath mingling with the cool air. The world narrows to the weight beneath you, the sense that even when wheels halt, there is ground enough to bear you.

Travelers and their stories often gather in places where the sky hangs heavy. At dawn, a mist rolls off the river and covers the city’s rooftops, softening edges, blurring the architecture. Ships idle at the quay, ropes creaking, the day’s business postponed until clarity returns. Fishermen sip their stew beside weathered nets, waiting out the hush. The cloud-laden sky is neither day nor night, holding back the sun’s verdict. This is the hour when the world seems paused, every intention caught in the hush before action. When the wheel’s turn is inverted, one might find assurance in the company of such weather, letting the passage of time slow to a crawl, the landscape emptied of sharp contrasts.

In the home, small tools and vessels accumulate by the window: a metal pail, a bell without its clapper, an enamel pot marked by years of boiling water and cooling tea. These objects catch the pale morning and gather it quietly, reflecting nothing brilliant. They speak of continuity, the slow passage from one hand to another. A humble woolen shawl, folded over a chair, has held warmth through many winters and will do so again. It is a thing that endures, no matter the shifting fortunes of the house.

In the wild places, lichens crust the bark of old trees and spread across forgotten gravestones. Their forms are neither leaf nor flower, but something more persistent. Travelers touch these totems to gauge the age of a place, tracing the slow, deliberate growth. Even in harsh weather, these companions do their work, softening and binding the hardest surfaces, a patient force that outlasts storms.

Today, if you feel the wheel’s slow descent, you might pause to rest on a bench carved from stone, letting your thoughts settle like morning fog. You could wrap a favorite shawl around your shoulders and wait for the kettle’s whistle, or step outside to watch the clouds gather over roof and field. As dusk falls, run your hand over the timeworn railing at your door, remembering all it has steadied. These moments bring you into the company of things that wait, things that hold steady when all else is restless.
