WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Reversed

A heavy wheel, carved with ancient symbols, sits stubbornly in its groove. In the silent pause before the next spin, a hand reaches out—skin prickling with the wish to stop what will come. The old fates peer down, their gazes caught in the thick, velvet folds of a curtain that lines the edges of the scene. This curtain belongs to a traveling troupe, weathered by dust and stories, its fabric saturated with years of spilled wine and spit blood. The turn of fortune is arrested for a breath; possibility stalls in the dusky darkness between what was and what might be.

Beneath the wheel, the earth is hard. In the West Indies, on an island rutted by storms, a runaway builds a home from what he can find. The soil is rich, but it is the pods clinging to thorny branches he seeks—beans shaped like sickle moons, their insides sharp with tang. These pods, in another place, might have been used as an offering, split and soaked in rum, scattered before the wind in hopes of steering fate. Here, though, they are ground by mortar and pestle, mixed into a paste that stains the hands and lips of those who taste it. A bitter food, heavy on the tongue. Eating it is a small refusal, a hand put out to slow the world’s descent.

The sea presses close. Storms rise suddenly, flinging their weight against the shore. The sky fills with clouds so thick they seem to touch the roofs of the houses. Rain falls angry and hard, leaving streaks on shutters, pooling in the narrow gutters of old port towns. When the tempests pass, the air is dense, close as the velvet curtain in the tent. Those who live in these places knot strips of dyed cloth at their waists, braided into sashes, worn as a mark: once, this meant freedom, defiance, and the refusal to be spun by the wheel’s momentum. The cloth is stiff, woven by hand, and smells of spice and dust.

In the shadowy light, someone polishes a chest in a parlor. The wood is soaked with the scent of evening, dark and gleaming under their careful touch. Inside, old coins spill out, caked with the memory of trade from distant lands—coins that once passed from palm to palm for indigo, for tobacco, for the pods that stain. In the kitchen, another person steeps the seeds in a small pot, letting the flavor deepen. The taste is metallic, almost medicinal, and it lingers long after the meal is done, as if tying the past to the present with an unbroken thread.

If you wish to echo these motions today, you might cook with pods or beans that recall the past, letting their scent fill your home. You could knot a sash around your waist, even if only for a moment, or polish a wooden keepsake with slow, methodical care. You may steep seeds in hot water, tasting the bitterness, remembering the patience of those who pressed pause on the world’s spin, holding still in the eye of fortune’s storm.
