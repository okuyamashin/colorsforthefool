WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Upright

The Wheel of Fortune turns ceaselessly, like the slow, measured sweep of a scythe harvesting fields that stretch to the horizon. The ancient symbol of the wheel is not only a sign of change but a mirror of the earth’s cycles—how a single seed can become a field, how a field becomes the storehouse of a village. In regions where the seasons are marked by planting and reaping, there is a rhythm that binds families and generations: the ritual of preparing the ground, the careful sowing, and the long months of watching weather and sky. The fate of a community once balanced on the turning of these cycles, the promise of harvest ever dependent on nature’s favor.

For millennia, myth and custom have grown in the furrows alongside the grain. Reverence for the harvest is found in the stories of Persephone and Demeter, in the blessings offered by Ceres, in the songs sung as the first sheaves are bound and carried. In the oldest villages, a shock of stalks is placed in the hearth or tied to the rafters to invite abundance or ward off hunger. The reaping and threshing are labor shared by many, each person’s effort turning the wheel of fortune just a little more. Songs and laughter ride on the warm winds, mingling with the rustle of stalks as the sun arcs overhead.

In these storied fields, the march of history is felt underfoot. Armies and caravans have crossed the plains, drawn by the promise of a full granary and the stability it brings. Great cities grew where rivers could feed the fields; marketplaces filled with sacks of meal, loaves, and straw hats. The seasons left their mark in the ledger of every household—years of plenty meant new tools, sturdier roofs, and festivals, while lean years taught patience, saving seed for a better turn of the wheel. The millstone in the heart of the village ground kernels into flour, its steady turning echoing the larger cycles of fate.

The tools that touch harvest are humble and enduring: the sickle hung above the door, the spinning wheel in the corner of a sunlit room, a woven basket for carrying loaves to market. In kitchens, hands knead dough, shaping it for the communal oven. During storms, families gather near the hearth, listening to the rain and hoping the fields will recover. On certain days, sunlight fills the air with the scent of dry stalks, and children braid them into small figures to hang in window frames for luck.

To draw a bit of this fortune into your day, you might bake bread by hand, letting the scent fill your home as it has filled kitchens for centuries. Walk where the land is open and cultivated, observing the pattern of rows and the work that shapes them. Mend a garment made of natural fiber, honoring the cycle from field to thread to cloth. Or take a moment before a meal to trace the invisible path from seed to table, recognizing your place in this ongoing story.
