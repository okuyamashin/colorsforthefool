WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Upright

On the old woodcut of the wheel, four animals sit at its quarters: lion, bull, eagle, human. The wheel itself turns on a fixed center, spokes radiating like the hands of a clock, marking an invisible cycle of fortune. It is not a wheel rolling blindly in the dust; its motion is the seasons, the slow circling of the year, the endless rising and falling that binds one moment to the next. So it is that people, too, find themselves sometimes at the top, sometimes at the bottom—bound to the turning, but never to the same place for long.

Above such a wheel, there is always the open sky, a dome of air that both shelters and exposes. At dawn, when fate turns toward a new chapter, the sky over fields and rivers stretches clear and fresh. It is the color of the afternoon seen through a farmhouse window, linen curtains wafting in a gentle breeze that signals change without hurry. In ancient stories, travelers looked upward to read the sky for omens—a flock of cranes flying high, the promise of fair weather, the clouds melting away to reveal vast, patient air. Mariners once measured their passage by the steadfast vault overhead, trusting its calm to steady restless waters below.

In old cities, this is the paint chosen for kitchen walls where sunlight lingers longest. It appears in glass beads threaded onto a child’s necklace, a keepsake found in a grandmother’s sewing box, smooth and cold to the touch. The same shade lives in glazed pottery bowls that hold morning porridge, set out after a night of storm or trouble, signaling that daylight always returns. In distant lands, it is the cotton cloth stretched between market stalls to create pockets of quiet shade for the day’s trade, a soft canopy that promises both shelter and possibility.

The phenomenon returns in gardens, where hydrangea petals lift their faces in early summer, and in the memory of wild forget-me-nots gathered along a riverbank’s edge, fingers wet with dew. On mountain passes, travelers pause at midday to watch the sky open wide above ridgelines, cooling sweat and worry before the descent begins again. The same weather watched by shepherds and pilots, pilots and poets, all measuring luck and fortune by what the day’s canopy holds. When the wind changes, the fishers on the coast read their fortunes in the expanse above, deciding whether to row out or stay ashore, knowing the great wheel turns with every tide.

Today, a person might open a window to the first clear air after rain, letting the breeze turn thoughts toward hope. They might wrap a scarf or shirt in the same calming shade around their shoulders for a meeting or journey, trusting in the luck that comes with clarity. Steeping a cup of morning tea, they could choose a mug glazed to recall the promise of a sky without threat, holding it warm between their hands as a reminder of new beginnings. Or they might step outside when the clouds part, raising their face to the open air, breathing in the luck that waits just above the horizon.
