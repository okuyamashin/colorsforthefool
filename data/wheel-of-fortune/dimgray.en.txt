WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Reversed

The great wheel, once radiant with stars and fates, now turns downward in a slow hush. In stories, this wheel is not metal but stone, heavy as the milestones that border old roads. It moves not on command, but with the slow drift of seasons. When its edge points down, the clatter softens, and there is the wish to stay the hand—halt the inevitable. This is the hushed interval between an ending and a beginning, a breath held beneath thick cloud.

Out on the moors, under the wide sweep of sky, morning fog wraps the land. It blankets slate roofs, drapes itself along the ridges of old mills. Here, in the dimness, fortunes are measured in moments—sometimes in the silence after rain, sometimes in the pause before the wind moves again. The world is muted, as if waiting for a sign. A shepherd might stand by a drystone wall, boots sunk into the cold turf, watching sheep huddle on the lee side. Above, weathered church spires slice the mist, their stonework darkened by centuries of smoke and storm. In towns built of quarried rock, alleys gleam with last night's drizzle, and iron railings bead with water.

Within these walls, hands rest on objects shaped for endurance. The hearth is swept, and the kettle, old with use, sits on the stove, its surface dulled from generations of steam and flame. On the workbench in a quiet room, a whetstone waits beside a stack of nails and screws. The mason’s apron, streaked by years of labor, hangs by the door. In the kitchen, rye bread, crusted from a slow bake, sits on a pewter plate, crumbs scattered from the first cut. Each tool and vessel holds a memory—the miner’s lunch pail, the gardener’s spade, the locksmith’s pick, all touched by loss and return.

History clings to places where fortune faltered or paused. In the highlands, old castles slumber under heavy skies, their doors sealed against winter. Travelers on narrow roads pass boundary stones, set deep by hands long gone. At market, traders lay out wares—pottery, rough-woven cloth, a bushel of truffles dug from roots. There is a hush in the exchange, the sense that every bargain is a gamble, watched over by the unseen.

Such weather is neither storm nor clear, but the in-between—a ceaseless mist, a drizzle that soaks into wool and bone. The air is filled with the smell of wet earth, the taste of cooled tea, the touch of rough linen. It is the hour before sunrise, when lamp chimneys smoke and the last embers in the grate shift quietly. At such times, wishes are made with caution. Luck is not sought, but steadied, as when a lathe is braced before the cut, or when a falconer steadies the bird before its flight.

If you find yourself in this turning, you might take up a stone from the path and hold it until it warms in your palm. You could polish the old key that sits in your drawer, or sweep the hearth and let the sound carry through the rooms. You might take a quiet walk along a lane bordered by slate, or write a letter to someone whose hand you miss. In each act, there is patience, and the shaping of chance into slow, steady hope.
