WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Reversed

The wheel halts mid-spin, the cast of fate interrupted. In ancient stories, this was the moment when a hand, tired of relentless change, set itself upon the rim and pressed down, trying to hold the pattern of the moment in place. The Wheel of Fortune reversed is not the wild churning of seasons and fates, but rather the stillness of a weight that resists. The old symbols—beasts, kings, the sphinx with its riddle—pause in their arc, gazing into a dusk that neither deepens nor lifts. In this orientation, fortune's cycles do not sweep us off our feet; instead, they gather in the quiet corners of lived days.

A hillside in late summer, covered by rows of olive trees, stands beneath a sky that threatens rain but never resolves into it. The fruit is mature, glossy and heavy, waiting for the hands that will come with baskets and rakes, but the work is delayed: the ground is slick, and the air smells of earth and leaf. The trees have endured centuries, their trunks twisted by time and weather. Their leaves, narrow and tough, catch the wind, and in moments of quiet, their movement is barely perceptible—an invitation to wait rather than to act, to let the cycle pause for breath. Underneath, moss and fallen twigs create a soft carpet, absorbing the footsteps of those who move between the trunks.

In the kitchen, a clay jar sits on a shelf, stained by oil that has seeped through its pores. It holds the pressed essence of last autumn’s harvest, waiting for use in measured amounts. Nothing in this room is hurried. A hand reaches for a pestle and mortar, grinding herbs harvested from the garden outside. Among them, rosemary and bay, dried and sharp, are reminders of preservation and patience. The scent rises, mingling with the oil, carrying the promise of meals made with what endures, not with what comes swiftly or easily.

In history, soldiers marched across landscapes where wild olive groves bordered the edge of empires, their armor dulled by dust, their rations flavored by what they could forage. The wood of those trees made shields and trinkets, simple things meant to last. A merchant's ledger records shipments of pressed oil, its pages spotted by rain, the ink running only slightly as the weather held back from full storm. Stories linger in these objects, the patient work of farming, the purposeful halt of commerce when roads became impassable mud rather than pathways of fortune.

Weather has its own way of pausing the wheel. A storm gathers over the same hills, not yet breaking. The clouds are thick and low, the light turned inward. In this suspended moment, sheep gather beneath the olive trees, their movement slow, chewing on grasses that have persisted through drought. The shepherd waits, leaning on his staff, listening to distant thunder that seems as hesitant as the day itself. There is a sense of something being withheld, of energy stored in roots and soil, of cycles refusing to turn until conditions are right.

To honor this moment, you might walk beneath old trees, letting your feet find steadier ground between their roots. You might set aside a jar for tomorrow’s use, resisting the urge to empty it all at once. You could press herbs between the pages of a book, keeping their shape for a slower season. Or you might simply sit at a window, watching the sky gather itself, accepting that fortune sometimes needs to rest as much as to turn.
