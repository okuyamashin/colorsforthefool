WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Reversed

The Wheel halts mid-turn, its carved spokes groaning as unseen hands grip the rim. The arcane creatures that ride its edge—lion, bull, eagle, angel—seem to glance downward, as if bracing for the gravity that pulls them from the comfortable cycle. Some wish to catch the Wheel and hold it in place; others merely fear what lies beneath. At the heart of the Wheel’s downward face is a weight, solid and insistent, like a door jammed by a wedge or a cartwheel stuck in damp earth. In this moment, fate feels not like a river but a furrow: a rut, pressed into the loam by countless repetitions of foot and hoof.

The old stories say fortune’s wheel is rimmed with the substance of the earth itself, shaped by hands caked with the residue of field and forest. In ancient Babylon, scribes pressed their records into tablets the color of tilled ground, each mark a permanent record of chance. The gamblers of medieval Italy rolled dice carved from hazelwood, their surface worn smooth by restless palms. Even now, in villages strung along riverbanks or nestled in hollows, wheels break the crust of harvested fields, their motion slowed by last week’s rain. In these places, destiny is not a spinning coin, but a loaf rising in a hearth’s gentle heat or a seed pressed into topsoil, waiting for spring.

When fate’s wheel declines to turn, the atmosphere grows close and heavy, like a barn after threshing. There is the scent of stored grain, the quiet presence of baskets plaited from willow and oak. Outside, weather settles: the air is thick with the damp of autumn, when sky and land join in a haze and boots leave clinging traces on every threshold. Stalls at the market are laden with the season’s yield: potatoes, beets, and roots that have drawn their substance from deep below. In such weather, fortune is neither wild nor cruel—it is patient, a slow accretion, a chance to rest beneath the eaves.

To stop the wheel before the turn completes is to reach for things that endure. The furrowed field, turned by generations, holds the memory of yokes and plows. The shopkeeper, counting coins on a broad counter, knows the worth of patience and the weight of small gains. There are stories of travelers pausing at roadside inns, tracing their journeys along benches polished by years of elbows and mugs. In kitchens, wooden spoons stir stews that simmer all afternoon, their flavors deepening as the sun droops behind the fence line. When the Wheel faces downward, it is time to remember the worth of slow work and the quiet dignity in repetition.

Today, fortune favors those who ground themselves. You may bake a loaf of bread, feeling the dough yield and rise beneath your hands. Sweep the floor, gathering the day’s dust into the pan with mindful care. Plant something—an acorn, a bulb, a hope—in the nearest patch of earth or even a pot by the window. Or simply sit with a cup of coffee or a square of chocolate, letting their steady warmth root you in the present moment. Each of these is an act of patience, a gentle hand on the Wheel, allowing it to pause before the next inevitable turn.
