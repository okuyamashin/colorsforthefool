WHEEL OF FORTUNE ― Reversed

The wheel in the card spins, a circle marked with ancient symbols and crowned with figures of strength and vision. When it rises upright, the promise is of cycles, fortune’s impartial march. Reversed, the turn falters; the rim is pressed by a deliberate hand, wishing to hold or halt the next chapter. Here, the energy flows not upward but seeks instead the weight and memory of earth, a longing for what resists change and remains, even as the world circles on.

In the fields near the old city of the same name, earth is gathered and shaped by generations of hands. There, beneath olive trees and the sweep of Mediterranean sky, the ground is known for its gift—a fine, dense soil that artisans dig from the riverbanks and hillsides. It stains their palms, is sifted and carried in woven baskets to their workshops, and becomes the base of mural and vessel. The kiln fires glow through the night, turning the pressed earth to something permanent: tiles for courtyards, pitchers for water, bowls for lentils and for wild honey. This soil, both pigment and substance, finds its way onto altar walls, into the paints that decorate the ceilings of chapels and the modest hearths of village homes. It is the mark of the countryside, of things fixed and made useful by labor.

Weather comes and shifts over these hills. In late summer, dust rises on the air, and the scent of the earth blends with the smoke from distant hearths. Shepherds know it by the way it clings to their boots and dyes their tunics as they cross the fields in the dusk. Roof tiles, shaped generations ago, guard the houses from sudden rains. In the market square, old trades gather—potters, tilemakers, painters—each holding close to their families the recipes for preparing the earth, the secret ratios for mixing it with linseed oil or lime. When the wheel turns downward, these crafts remind us of the pause, the anchoring, and the hand that stills what might slip away.

In the morning, a baker dusts a board with flour and spreads a cloth woven from local flax, colored by the very same natural pigment. The round loaves that emerge from his oven bear a stain from the stone, and the crust cracks with a promise of deep, sustaining warmth. Nearby, a kitchen mortar, seasoned by years of use, grinds herbs for stew, the bowl etched with the memory of countless meals. These small rituals—the mixing, the shaping, the slow firing—are ways of holding fortune still for a moment, of making the everyday sturdy against the unpredictable.

Today, if you wish to steady the wheel and draw fortune’s pause, you might press your palms into garden soil, feeling its coolness slow your thoughts. You could cook with clay vessels, honoring the old ways of heat and patience. You might set a tile or a stone in your home, or write a letter with a pen dipped in earth-based ink, sealing a wish within its steady lines.
