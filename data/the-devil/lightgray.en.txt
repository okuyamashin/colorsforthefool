THE DEVIL ― Reversed

The card is set in a chamber beneath a ruined keep, where a pair of old iron shackles lies open at the foot of a crumbling pedestal. Once, these shackles bit into the ankles of wanderers who knelt before a horned idol, but now they rest quietly, their weight only a memory. A thin layer of dust coats the links, softening their edges, muting the threat—yet those who once wore them linger in the hall, sitting in the draft that drifts in through broken windows. They run their fingertips across the metal as if it were a bracelet, unconcerned by its past.

Beyond the keep, morning fog creeps along the ground, swallowing the hills and the silent, stunted orchard at the edge of the estate. The trees stand stripped and patient, their bark cool and even, their trunks bearing witness to years of careful neglect. Fallen branches and stones in the field are cloaked in dew, and the sky above is featureless, pressing down like a blanket. A shepherd’s cottage, set apart from the road, breathes the scent of wet stone and washed-out thatch; its roof, patched with river clay, holds the memory of countless rains.

When the bell in the town square rings for midday, shopkeepers push open their shutters, and the cobblestones gleam beneath their feet. In the apothecary’s window, a porcelain mortar and pestle sit next to a paper-wrapped package of sage and dried mushroom. The tools are worn smooth by years of use, each bearing the thumbprint of someone who ground herbs to powder while waiting out an afternoon storm. In the kitchen, a cook lifts the lid from her soup pot, watching the vapor spiral up before it disappears into the rafters. The day’s bread, set to cool on the window ledge, is wrapped in clean linen, its folds pressed flat with careful hands.

At the edge of the square, a mason smooths the surface of a fresh headstone, his chisel tapping out the name of someone whose story is already receding into silence. He sits back on his heels, wiping stone dust from his fingers, and listens as the wind rattles the bare branches overhead. For a moment, the town holds still—neither shadowed nor bright, simply present, suspended in a clarity that needs no embellishment.

The chains are open, but they remain close by. You can sweep the dust from an iron tool and set it aside, or you can polish it, letting each mark remind you of what you have endured. Step outside into the damp morning air, letting the fog settle against your skin, or kneel to gather fallen branches in the orchard, hands steady and unhurried. If you light a fire in the hearth, tend it with the same care you use when wrapping bread in linen: gentle, watchful, remembering what it is to bring warmth into stillness.
