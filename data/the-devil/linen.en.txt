THE DEVIL ― Reversed

On the card, the figures remain at the foot of the altar, but now the chains droop around their necks, slack as forgotten ribbons. They do not look at each other, nor at the figure with horns and torch. Instead, they gaze downward, as if contemplating what it means to stay when the lock has vanished and the world is brighter than before. No force holds them—only the habit of weight, the memory of captivity that lingers long after the fetters have ceased to bite. The air is clear but heavy with what has not yet been chosen. Denial and inertia hold sway here, as each person tests the loosened chain, uncertain whether to rise or remain.

In distant fields, the quiet answer grows. There, in the midst of turning seasons, slender stalks rise from the earth. Their flowers are pale and delicate, quick to shed their petals in the bright sun. For generations, these plants have been sown and reaped by patient hands. Their stems are pulled just as the morning mists lift, bundled and left to soften in the dew. Nearby, water flows—rivers and streams winding through land that has seen armies and empires, but still yields its harvest to those who tend with care. Each stalk is a promise, a transformation waiting among mud and root.

Inside the home, the ancient labor continues. The stalks are soaked, beaten, combed until the fibers give way. Spinners sit at wheels, twisting threads with practiced fingers, humming tunes older than memory. The threads are woven on looms, stretched and beaten and coaxed into long, smooth lengths. There is a rhythm to this work, a steadiness that is at once humble and dignified. Each length of cloth tells its own story: of hands that shaped it, of mornings spent at the river’s edge, of evenings spent by the hearth. The transformation does not shout; it unfolds in gentle persistence, in the slow turning of a wheel, in the soft clatter of the loom.

These cloths find their place in every corner of life. They become the cover on a table set for a simple meal, the garment a child wakes in, the sheet pulled over tired shoulders at dusk. They line baskets and swaddle the newly born. They wrap bread fresh from the oven, catching the warmth and letting it rest. When travelers set sail, they carry this fabric with them; when homecomings are celebrated, it is there too, spread flat and smooth, a surface for gathering, for breaking bread, for starting anew. In weather fair or foul, these fibers endure—dried in sun, saved from rain, pressed and folded in quiet corners. Each piece, whether new or worn, holds the memory of work and the imprint of gentleness.

Some moments, the shadow returns: the urge to cling to what is known, to wear the chain as ornament. But the world is full of small ways to move past old patterns. One may stand barefoot on cool, woven mats at dawn, letting the day begin without hurry. Another may bake, laying a cloth over dough to help it rise, patient with time. Folding a favorite shirt, smoothing its surface before placing it in a drawer, can become a meditation on simple care. Even gathering a handful of stalks in a field or garden, and soaking them in a bowl, can honor the slow art of transformation.
