THE DEVIL ― Upright

A pedestal stands at the center of the old stable, worn by the pressure of countless boots and hooves. Lantern light grazes its surface, flickering with each draft that enters the doorway. Two iron chains, once looped tightly, now hang slack beside a horse’s saddle, their purpose diminished when vigilance fades. Desire, like a restless hound, circles the room; it is tamed only when watched, yet the tools to restrain it sit always within reach. The saddle itself, supple from years of polish and sweat, carries the memory of distance traveled and burdens borne. The glow from the lantern’s flame—steady when cupped, wild when left to the air—illuminates the partition between mastery and surrender.

In ancient market towns, the saddle was a mark of trade and trust. Craftsmen shaped it for long journeys across dry plains, where travelers learned the need for endurance and the wisdom of knowing when to dismount. In legend, a torch set upon a pedestal never burned without a keeper; left alone, its flame either devoured the air or faded out, and only the attentive hand kept its light alive. The same is true for those chains—slackened, they pose no threat, their shadow nothing but a ripple on the packed earth. Yet, a moment’s inattention and they could bind, a warning from myth etched into every link.

There is an old story from the borderlands, where cattle trails cross riverbeds and the air smells of leather and smoke. On humid afternoons, the storm clouds gather but rarely break. The herders collect around the tack-room bench, dusting saddles and telling tales of bargains struck and broken. The pedestal in the corner, carved from the same sturdy wood as the stock fences, supports both the saddle and the memories of those who have ridden out under clouded skies, seeking fortunes or fleeing debts. The torch, a simple pitch-soaked staff, is ready for the night’s first darkness, always lit with purpose and never left burning as a careless ornament. Each object is part of a ritual: the saddle for passage, the chain for caution, the torch for clarity.

In the home, the evidence of these tools persists: a bridle hung by the doorway, a boot scraper at the threshold, the leather-bound handle of an old hammer resting on the worktable. The threads that hold these things together are visible, and their frayed ends speak to vigilance. To recognize one’s own desires, to see their shape and purpose, is to hold the torch and keep the chain slack. The stable’s hush is a lesson in balance—a place where the animal nature rests, yet the means to ride or restrain remain close at hand.

Today, a person might polish a well-worn belt, paying attention to each crease and groove; they might fasten a clasp or buckle on a bag, feeling the weight of the object settle into their palm. They could sit by a wooden bench and mend a leather strap, or light a candle at dusk and watch how the flame responds to each breath of wind. Each small act is a way to see desire clearly, neither shunned nor mistaken for the self, but used as a tool in its time.
