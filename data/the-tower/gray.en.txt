THE TOWER ― Upright

The night stands still, split open by a single bolt. The old stone turret, solitary against the sky, has weathered so many storms, but this time, the cloud's surprise is a thunderclap that sends the carved lintel tumbling. On the ground, the fallen crown lies among loose cobbles and crushed pebbles, relics of the collapse. The air is sharp with the scent of rain and something ancient, as if the mortar itself has exhaled. What once was hidden inside the fortress—secrets, prayers, or withheld warnings—spills out across the flagstones in the sudden openness.

Beneath the tower, the path is hard-packed, the kind that is neither dry dust nor slick mud, but a middle ground worn by centuries of feet. In the old stories, the traveler crossing the moors would find shelter in a stone hut, its roof stitched with lichen, its hearth cold. The walls, rough with the memory of chisels, might echo with tales of storms past. Every ancient castle holds a chamber where nothing moves but time, where the fire is a memory, and the only company is a kettle set aside for a future that never arrives. A morning after, the mist curls low over the field, blanketing the wreckage and the survivors alike, softening edges but never quite hiding the new shape of things.

Beyond the ruined walls, the river moves on as ever, carrying bits of the past in its quiet flood. On days shrouded in fog, the world loses its distances; hills shrink to silhouettes, while the calls of distant crows are swallowed. In the city, high above the avenue, rooftops gather the sky’s heaviness close. The rooftiler, hands marked by slate dust, pauses to watch the shifting sky and thinks of his father’s trade. In a quiet kitchen, a grandmother’s teapot sits cooling on the counter. Its once-bright glaze has dulled to the hue of weathered pewter, echoing the clouds that threaten to break again. The handle is warm, waiting for someone to return and make the day right.

The work of change always leaves residue. The mason gathers fallen fragments, their edges sharp from the break, and wonders which to keep, which to let wash away in the next rain. On the shore, pebbles worn smooth by uncounted tides remind those who walk there that each storm clarifies, strips away, reveals. In the wake of collapse, the survivor’s cloak—once woven for finery—serves now to ward off the chill that seeps in when the sun is a memory and the sky coldly anonymous. From the depths of a drawer, a flint, kept for emergencies, finds new use in rekindling the hearth. Outside, the day offers only the weather common to ports and highlands: a chill, a damp, not quite rain but enough to settle old dust and draw out the scent of earth.

Today, a person may face the wind on a walk, letting the air clear the mind of what no longer stands. They might find an old pot or stone and set it by the door, marking the change for themselves. Preparing a meal from what the larder holds, quietly, can honor what is left after upheaval. Sweeping the stoop, collecting the loose gravel, or mending the stone wall—each act lays a new foundation, practical and plain, for what comes next.
