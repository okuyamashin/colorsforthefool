THE TOWER ― Reversed

A single bell tower has stood, for centuries, above an old stone village. Its corners are squared, its edges chipped by wind. The tower once promised warning: from its windows, smoke was watched, storms tracked, invaders seen by the night watch. But lately, the bell inside has not rung. The cracks spider deeper, yet the stones cling in uncertain suspension, as if the whole could still be called a tower so long as its name remains. There are places like this on the edge of the northern fens, where the weather serves as both warning and delay. Long before thunder splits the sky, a low shroud of cloud hangs over the marsh, pressing roofs and fields into a hush. Stone and sky, both holding secrets behind a veil.

In the mythology of this landscape, the tower is not only watchfulness but denial—a refusal to admit the true extent of damage. There is a legend that after a great fire, the villagers returned not to rebuild, but to prop up the hollowed tower with beams scavenged from shipwrecks and fences. They painted over the soot, patched the holes with mortar scavenged from collapsed cottages. The bells were recast from found scrap, their shapes uneven but their purpose undiminished. Even now, the tower’s silhouette rises above the river, its outline uncertain against the dusk when the light fails and everything merges into a heavy dusk, neither night nor day.

Along the banks, reeds cluster thick, their stalks brittle from last year’s floods. In the morning, fishermen set out before the sun, their boats sliding through a mist that sits close above the water. Their boots, dried by many seasons in mud and ash, carry the marks of survival—a rough clay pressed into every fold, flecks of charcoal ground into the seams. They bring home their catch in old tin pails, battered and dulled by years left in the rain, a memory of what once gleamed bright in the hearth’s glow. The houses too tell this story: roof slates gone smooth by time and weather, hearthstones scored by soot and careful hands.

Inside, families gather around iron stoves, the kind that have warmed generations. There are stories of storms that nearly took the roof, and tales of the watchman who never left his post, even after the bell ceased to sound. Each evening, as the wind presses the shutters, someone reaches for the old woolen blanket, spun from the fleeces of sheep that grazed the hillside before the flood. Its threads are thick, the color of rain-soaked stone, and it muffles the world outside. The lamp on the table is shielded by a battered kettle, its surface mottled by years on the flame. There is comfort in the ordinary: in mushrooms gathered from the edge of the woods, in bread baked on the stone, in the feather left behind by a starling fleeing the coming storm.

Today, if you wish to answer the call of the tower without surrendering to ruin, you might walk along the old road, tracing the cracks in the paving with the toe of your boot. You could polish a cast iron pan until its surface reflects the muted light of the hearth, or mend a woolen scarf by the window as you listen to the lull of distant thunder. Place a smooth river stone on your desk, a token of what endures beneath the changing sky.
