THE TOWER ― Upright

A single bolt carves the sky, splitting the night with a shout of thunder. Stone shakes and something once high and still begins to topple. On the tower’s peak sat a coronet, now cast down so all can see it is empty. Light floods the winding stairs and hidden chambers, scattering secrets that were piled in the dark. It is the hour when what was locked away demands to be known, and the world outside rushes into the breach.

Here, in the ancient cities where towers rise above orchards and dusty roads, the fields beyond are aflame with autumn harvest. In lands where the sun leans low, the groves burst with fruit, a thousand lanterns swaying in the wind—persimmons clinging to bare branches, their skins lit by the last light of evening. The people gather them into straw baskets and clay bowls, the roughness of fingers brushing off a memory of rain. In the markets, peppers dry on strings, curling slowly in the smoke that drifts from crumbling stone ovens. Each pod holds a hot spark inside, a warning and a promise.

At the foot of the ruined tower, the ground is scattered with fragments: pottery shards and ember-streaked bricks from old kilns. In the myth of the kiln, it is here that clay is changed by flame, once soft and silent, now ringing when struck. The potter cannot see within when the door is closed, only trusting the heat will reveal what form, what cracks, burn inside. When the kiln is opened, truth pours out with the steam.

Through the centuries, travelers have crossed deserts where the air shimmers as the sun sets. In that hour, the horizon blazes, and the wind carries dust and stray petals from flame trees blooming at the city’s edge. Sometimes, lightning forks through the clouds, and rain hisses as it meets the scorching stone. The monsoon breaks the quiet, carrying down what must fall. In kitchens, the same force is captured in a flicker on the stove, a single chili dropped into the pan, releasing its sharpness to clear the air and awaken the tongue.

Stories speak of traders on the Silk Road, their caravans trailing after the heat of the setting sun, their packs filled with spices that once filled a broken jar at the tower’s base. Saffron threads, rare and precious, were weighed with trembling hands, their fragrance rising with the dust each time the jar was opened. A sign that what was hidden travels far, carried on the breath of change.

Now, as the old crown lies on the flagstones and the walls breathe out their old secrets, the present waits for a sign in the kitchen or on the hillside. One might harvest a persimmon at dusk, slice it open and taste the sweetness that survived the fall. Someone may grind dried peppers with mortar and pestle, releasing the fire locked within, or sweep the hearth and recall the kiln’s lesson: only through the break can the real shape appear. A traveler, watching the horizon as storm clouds gather, may dare to step outside, feeling the air shift and the world turn.
