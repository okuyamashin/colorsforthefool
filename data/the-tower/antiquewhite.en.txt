THE TOWER ― Reversed

Lightning splits the sky, but the tower holds. At its highest window, smoke drifts out rather than fire; the stones below are scorched but still stacked. Time has stumbled into an uneasy pause; the disaster is coming, but not yet. The walls’ cracks are packed with the dust of old calcined mortar, the sort that gathers in the folds of curtains forgotten in attics or in the grooves of plaster moldings in abandoned ballrooms. Once, there were feasts here, beneath ceiling tiles that glimmered with the gleam of old bone china, perpetually cool to the touch. The tablecloths were thick and heavy, woven by hands that knew the right warp and weft to last through generations of spilled wine and thrown bread.

In the garden, the light sifts down like flour, thickening the air. It’s a late morning after the storm: the grass is damp, but the benches are dry, protected by the thick canopies of magnolia. Along the gravel walks, eggshells from hens in the half-ruined coop glint in the sun, reminders of quiet routines persisted in the shadow of collapse. The wind smells faintly of sweet bread rising in the kitchen—someone is still baking, even as the kitchen tiles are loose underfoot.

Beyond the garden wall, the landscape blurs into chalk downs, hills etched by sheep and centuries of wind. These are the same fields where traders once traveled with lumbering wagons, their wheels creaking under loads of raw flax and ivory soap. Villagers washed linen in the river and laid it out on the grass to dry, the fabric billowing like sails in the faint breeze. The softest cloth—worn thin over years of scrubbing—now lines the basket where a child gathers mushrooms, careful not to disturb the powdery coating clinging to their stems.

Myths gather in this place, as persistent as the dust in corners. The oldest tales say that, after a fall, the tower’s bones remember their shape—the way that limestone remembers the pressure of seas long vanished. They tell of a weather too gentle for the season, a hush after thunder that feels like a held breath. In these moments, the collapse seems delayed by the smallest acts: a grandmother’s hands sifting flour for a loaf, a carpenter resetting the handle of a wardrobe passed down through five households, a gardener brushing sand from the shell of a buried snail.

Today, the rubble is still called a tower. Its silhouette remains against the sky, softened and dignified by years of smoke and candle grease, the slow accumulation of stories and stray grains of sugar. Inside, the memory of safety endures—not as a shield, but as a quilt, patched and faded, wrapped around the shoulders of those who persist. The disaster is not denied, but deferred, as if the world might yet be held together by the threads of old linen, the glaze on a cup, the way sunlight pools on the floorboards.

You might set bread to rise in the old way, under a linen cloth that has covered loaves for decades. Open the attic trunk and polish a fragment of bone china, tracing the hairline veins with a steady finger. Fold a faded tablecloth and lay it across the table, then sit beside the window, letting the gentle light settle into your hands. Or walk in the garden after rain, gathering eggs in a basket lined with the softest household rag, careful not to hurry the morning.
