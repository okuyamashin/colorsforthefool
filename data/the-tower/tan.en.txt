THE TOWER ― Reversed

A jagged tower survives in the distance, though cracks have crept up its sides and the air is thick with the hush that follows a lightning strike. The ruin has not come crashing down—yet—but inside, the old order sags under its own tired weight. The people below do not see the storm’s true shadow; they sweep the steps and keep the doors latched against weather, calling what stands a fortress, even as the mortar loosens. In the tower’s shadow, the land stretches out, a low plain made of shifting dust and trampled grasses, where herds once pressed their shapes into the afternoon and the earth kept the story of every footprint.

Nearby, tanners work in the open, the scent of curing hides threading through the breeze. Their hands are calloused, skilled in the ancient practice of turning animal skin into something useful—boots, aprons, pouches, and belts. The vats are set into the ground, lined with pale, worn stones, and the labor belongs to those who understand how to wait, how to wring durability from decay. A tanner’s trade is patience, the ritual of washing, scraping, and soaking, a trust that what feels ruined will be made whole and sturdy again, even as it is changed forever. The aprons hung outside the shop have absorbed the story of every hand that tied their cords, and though torn at the edges, these garments serve as shields against the sharp teeth of the world. In the quiet hours, the tanners speak softly, their voices woven into the rhythm of water poured and hides rinsed clean.

Beyond the town, the plain rolls toward a riverbank where wild grasses lean together in the wind. The stalks are dry, tough, and flexible, bending but not breaking as they weather the seasons. Shepherds bring their flocks here in the pale light of morning, boots dusted and cloaks drawn close, whispering to their animals. The sheep nose through the stalks, searching for what is left after last year’s floods. The grass is not lush, but it endures, and the shepherds have learned to tie dried sheaves into mats for resting, or to weave baskets for carrying embers home from the fire. Over centuries, tents have stood here, pitched by travelers who chose this flat land for its gentle welcome and for the way the grasses muffle footsteps at night. In the space between what collapses and what endures, the plain holds its breath.

In town, there is bread in the oven, and the flour dust drifts across rough worktops as hands knead dough. The loaves are shaped by repetition and memory; the crust forms thick and stubborn, crackling as it cools on the wooden board. A child’s hands reach for a slice, and the crumbs tumble to the ground. The kitchen’s table is covered in a cloth that has seen many washings, its fabric softened by years of use but still strong enough to gather the warmth of the day. In small, daily ways, the household holds together, even as the rafters above creak in the wind.

If you wish to move in step with this postponement, wear an old leather belt, polish a wooden spoon until it fits your palm, or gather dry grasses to lay beneath your shoes by the door. Make bread with your own hands and offer a slice to someone who lingers in silence. Every act that cares for what is worn—yet still standing—becomes a quiet pledge against collapse.
