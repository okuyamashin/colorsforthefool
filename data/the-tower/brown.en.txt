THE TOWER ― Reversed

The Tower, in its upright drama, is the lightning-struck spire, stones crumbling down, the force of change abrupt and undeniable. Reversed, the collapse is suspended, as if the fractured walls refuse to fall. Tension lingers in every brick, and the tower’s silhouette still cuts the sky, though its insides are hollowed by old storms. The echoes of thunder fade, but the air remains heavy, smelling of mortar, earth, and damp timber—like a fortress in the aftermath of a long, uncertain siege.

In the old cities, remnants of the past stand in layered walls: clay shaped into bricks, baked ages ago by hands that smudged wet soil along wooden forms. These ancient blocks remain, defying wind and rain, their surfaces rough beneath one’s palm. Scattered among them lie broken tiles, shards of vessels once used to hold grain or water, and the forgotten tools of labor—handles smooth from generations of use, their shapes molded by sweat and necessity. In the shadow of these ramparts, farmers gather what the earth gives: potatoes, sprouting in mounds of loam; chestnuts falling in spiky armor to the forest floor. The landscape is steady, unwavering, even when the world above it is split by lightning.

History remembers cities swallowed by fire or flood. Yet sometimes the disaster retreats, and the foundations endure. In the aftermath, people return to what is solid: floors made from pressed earth, beams of walnut supporting the roof, cupboards filled with the fruit of last year’s harvest. In quiet kitchens, hands knead dough on sturdy tables, the scent rising from baked loaves, crusty and warm. A piece of pottery, repaired with seams of solder, sits on the hearth. These objects do not boast of their survival, but they hold the story of what has not fallen.

The Tower reversed is the pause before the inevitable, a moment when the walls do not yet give way. In the fields, trees hunker low against the wind; their roots grip tightly, twining through ancient humus. In the mountains, rocky paths wind along the slope, the ground beneath firm after a long rain. The world here is not smooth or gleaming. It is textured, pitted, stained by weather and use—a landscape that absorbs the shock and shelters memory.

Sometimes, weather itself mimics this stillness. Clouds pile overhead, heavy with unshed rain, pressing down on barns and fences. The air carries the scent of peat and woodsmoke. Mud squelches underfoot, and travelers lean into the wind, boots caked with the proof of the journey. There is patience in these moments, a waiting that is neither defeat nor surrender. Even as cracks spread through stone, the tower stands, refusing to forget its shape.

Today, one might walk among the furrows after rain, turning potatoes from the wet ground with gloved hands. Or patch a worn work boot, stitching the sole back to leather, so it will last another season. Pour coffee into a thick earthenware mug, feeling the weight of it, heat seeping into the fingers. Sit on an old wooden bench beneath a chestnut tree, collecting the spiny husks and breaking them open for their hidden sweetness. These are gestures of grounding, tying us to what endures.
