THE TOWER ― Reversed

In the worn-out ruins of the Tower, the disaster is not yet complete. The lightning has struck, but the stones hold their shape, defiant and trembling. What remains is both a warning and a promise. The scaffolding creaks in the wind, and dust hovers where the floor threatens to give way. All around, there is the sense of collapse delayed, as if the world is holding its breath. Amidst this, a workhorse stands tethered to an old harness, leather dry and cracked from hard use but not yet broken. It waits, calm, in the shadow of the ruined tower.

The old saddle that rests on this horse speaks of countless journeys across wild plains. Stitched by hand, it carried its rider through borders and storms, over mountain passes and into towns where the air was thick with the scent of roasting chestnuts and the heat from open hearths. The saddle’s surface is marked by sweat and years of sunlight, shaped by the weight of those who depended on its strength. Once, it was new and unscarred, carried by traders along the Silk Road or lone messengers bringing urgent news to distant cities.

Elsewhere, on the kitchen table of a weathered house, a wooden bowl holds roots pulled from the ground at the end of autumn: parsnips and tubers, their skins rough and earthy, promising nourishment through the leaner months. The bowl itself was carved long ago from the trunk of an oak that grew at the edge of the old forest, where the ground is firm and the wind shivers through the canopy. Each groove in the vessel’s surface remembers an axe, a hand, the steady work of shaping something useful from the remains of what once stood tall.

In the workshop beyond the kitchen, stacks of tanned hides hang from thick pegs, their surfaces bearing the imprint of countless hands. Here, the cobbler sits hunched over a half-finished pair of boots, the sole stitched tight, ready to shield the foot from rocky trails. The craftsman’s hammer taps quietly, echoing the sound of hooves on packed earth. In the corner, a battered bridle hangs beside a plow handle, both waiting to be called back to work.

Outside, the fields stretch toward the horizon, the soil moist after last night’s rain. The air holds the scent of horses and loam, and the wind carries the memory of distant brushfires. At the edge of the pasture, wild mushrooms push up through leaf litter, their caps ringed with the traces of last year’s storms. The sky is low, promising more rain, but the ground beneath remains solid for now.

If you seek steadiness amid upheaval, polish an old saddle or oil a wooden bowl with your own hands. Prepare a meal using root vegetables, honoring the slow work of the earth. Mend or care for a much-used tool, even if its best years are past. Walk the boundary of your home as dusk falls, noticing what stands and what waits to fall.
