THE TOWER ― Reversed

In the image of the Tower, lightning forks across a dark sky, stones loosen from their mortar, and walls tremble. Yet, when the card turns, the structure stands at the brink: the foundation is cracked but endures, and the shape is still called a tower. There is a pause after the warning, a hush that settles like dust after thunder. The collapse is not complete; upheaval mostly hides beneath the surface, waiting for the next strike.

Across centuries, people have recognized the moment after disaster is not always ruin but an uneasy continuation. On a storm-struck night in the Caribbean, a bell signals the gathering of those who refused to be defined by disaster. These people, whose ancestors escaped bondage and built their own settlements in the thick heart of the forest or the high ridges of the mountains, called themselves by the name that echoes with endurance. The story of these communities is built from the patience to survive. In their kitchens, cast-iron pots simmered with beans slowly soaking up the essence of salt pork and allspice, the meal thick and sustaining. Sturdy shoes, scuffed by muddy paths, rested near the doorway, waiting for the next errand or escape. A tangle of sweet tamarind pods spilled from a basket, sticky and tart, promising relief from hunger and thirst.

In distant markets, cloth for dignified ceremonies arrived from across the ocean—woven thick, shot through with deep hues that signaled status and self-respect. Swathes of this fabric draped altars and shoulders alike, a mark of pride and survival, even as the old order threatened from beyond the treeline. On the table, a dish of roasted chestnuts cooled in a carved wooden bowl, their shells split to show the flesh within, a quiet reminder that transformation is not always visible at first glance. The evening air pressed close, heavy with the breath of distant rain, neither storm nor calm, but something in between.

Within the home, a mortise chisel lay beside a half-finished joint, the patient work of repair paused but not forgotten. The structure endures through this labor: walls patched, beams reset, even as old fractures remain beneath new surfaces. Under the bed, a tattered satchel held dried leaves and secret notes—a memory of flight and a promise to return if needed. By the hearth, a pot of hibiscus tea cooled, its steam rising in the lamplight as an offering to the night. The hour after alarm is not wasted if it is used to gather, to cook, to mend, to remember.

Today, you might simmer a pot of beans with a piece of cured meat or a handful of spices, letting the warmth seep into your kitchen. You might restore a worn tool, oiling its handle and tightening its joints. You could lay out thick cloth for a small ritual: drape it across your table or shoulders and claim a moment of dignity. In the evening, keep a bowl of roasted chestnuts at hand, breaking them open to mark small victories and acknowledge the work still to come.
