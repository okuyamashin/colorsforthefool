THE TOWER ― Upright

There is a night, long remembered, when the sky is split by a single flash, and a tower stands solitary on its height. The stone blocks, quarried from ancient slopes, have held against centuries of weather and war, but tonight is different. Thunder shakes the earth. The lightning uncrowns the highest battlement, ripping through the walls, sending fragments tumbling down onto the ledge below. What was kept inside—the secrets, the pride, the vulnerable heart—spills out into the cold, unprotected dark.

This night, the land around the tower is a sweep of slate, a ridge where miners once dug for roof-tiles and inkstones, where hands labored at the break of day, and the wind scoured the exposed rock clean. Below the tower, the ground is scattered with chipped fragments, like the shards of a broken promise. The miners’ tools lean against the slope: a chisel left beside a half-split piece, a hammer dark with the memory of work, a whetstone smoothed by careful hands. The collapse echoes the ancient trade, for every slate roof was once raised from the earth with risk and resolve, just as the tower was built on the belief that something could stand against the storm.

Elsewhere, on a kitchen table in a distant city, a writer sets her mug on a slate coaster and listens to rain tap against the window. She thinks of the mountain quarry, the men and women who walked home at dusk with dust in their hair. On her desk, a letter opener lies beside a pile of unwritten notes, its handle carved from the same stone as the tower’s walls. She turns it over in her palm, feeling the cool, even weight—a reminder that things shaped by pressure are reliable, if not unbreakable. The city’s roofs, shielded with overlapping tiles, wait through the storm. Beneath them, people gather around hearths, stirring pots, lighting candles, whispering stories of upheaval and renewal.

When the lightning splits the night, travelers shelter beneath a slate outcrop along an old road. The air is charged; the first heavy drops fall, bouncing off the ledge. The travelers press their hands to the chilled stone, their faces lit for a moment by the sky’s fierce glare. They know the mountain’s ledges are both shelter and exposure, that the strength of the stone is proven by what it endures. In the aftermath, the world smells of rain and crushed lichens. The living rock absorbs the water, slow and patient.

Today, a person can clear a patch of ground in their garden, laying down flat river stones to make a path, remembering the quarries and the builders. One might write a letter on a smooth surface, letting the pen move steadily, as if engraving a message into something enduring. On a stormy evening, place a kettle on a trivet quarried from old mountains and listen to the rain, as the roof holds fast. Step outside after the downpour, and gather small stones from the roadside—a pocketful of reminders that what falls can be gathered again.
