THE TOWER ― Upright

A sudden break in the night, a jagged fork of lightning, and the crown atop the stone tower tumbles. The walls split, stones raining down, smoke and sparks flung into the storm-swept darkness. The old world, once sealed and guarded, is cracked open by force. With the collapse, what had been hidden—fears, secrets, truths—rushes outward like wind through shattered glass. Every structure stands only so long as its foundation holds; when the earth shifts, or the sky strikes, tradition gives way to something raw, urgent, and alive.

In myth, towers call down the wrath of heavens. On the plains by the Euphrates, clay bricks baked in summer heat rose higher every season, reaching for the forbidden. When the spires fell, ancient fires leapt skyward, tongues curling, consuming what was built in pride. In the city, fire engines clatter past, bells pealing, a coil of hose ready to douse the sparks that leap from the old tenements. Ash drifts in the alleyways, where the baker’s oven once glowed in the morning. Civilization, again and again, is rebuilt over ruins.

The orchard at the edge of the village, untouched by the flames, still holds its fruit. An apple drops in the darkness, splitting open on the stones, releasing a scent like memory or warning. In the kitchen, a woman lifts a kettle from the stovetop; the water inside hisses, steaming, like breath after a run. On the table, a dish of pomegranate seeds glimmers, each shining globe holding a world inside, waiting to be crushed between teeth. The sharp taste marks a new beginning, a promise made in crisis.

Under storm clouds, the path to the mountain is slick with mud. Here, poppies press themselves against the rocks, petals torn by the wind, defiant in their fragile splendor. At the summit, a rooster stands atop a ruined wall, announcing the day in a voice that cracks the silence. Once, a signal fire burned here, warning of danger, or summoning help. On festival nights, dancers whirl around the firepit, feet stamping, hands clapping, torches high. Sparks spiral upward, as if to undo gravity itself.

History is scattered with moments like this: a stroke that splits a nation, a revolution ignited by the word of one mouth, the breaking of a king’s seal. Each time, the old order falls, and the world rearranges itself around the new. In the aftermath, survivors gather what they can—iron nails, a piece of cloth, a single chili pepper hung by the door to ward off evil. They sweep up glass, tend burns, and pass a cup of tea from hand to hand, letting the heat center them amid the ruins.

Tonight, you could split a pomegranate and let the juice stain your fingers, or fold a piece of silk and tuck it in your pocket as a sign of courage. You could strike a match, lighting a candle against the darkness after a hard day, or cook with a measure of cayenne, letting the bite clear your head. The world may shift suddenly, but in small acts you claim the right to begin again.
