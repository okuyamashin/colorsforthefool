THE CHARIOT ― Reversed

A chariot drawn by two animals, each straining in its own direction, staggers at the crossroads. The wheels grind deep into the earth, not forward but in place, as if the ruts themselves have become the journey. The driver’s hands grip leather straps, cracked and softened by years of sweat, yet the reins offer no command. The animals sniff the air and paw the mud, unheeding, their nostrils flecked with dried grain and river dust. The land beneath is a field that has seen too many feet—flattened, rich, and churned—the same ground that once produced wheat and root vegetables, now trampled and uncertain.

Elsewhere, ancient stories tell of the ploughman who was lost in his own furrow, circling his field beneath a sky the color of heavy loam. His boots, caked in last year’s harvest and this morning’s rain, carried him nowhere new. He listened to the lowing of cattle in the byre, the fidgeting of horses against their posts, and the distant crackle of a hearth where potatoes turned in hot ash. All around him, the signs of passage: a farmer’s hat hanging by the door, stiff from winter’s work; a mug left beside a wooden chair, stained by decades of morning coffee; a coat thrown over a fence, heavy with the smell of damp and smoke.

The road itself, winding through forest and pasture, is bordered by stacks of cut timber, stripped of bark and drying in the cool air. Wagons creak as they pass, each bearing the weight of harvest—sacks of beans, jars of honey, baskets of mushrooms gathered near the roots of old trees. This is the way of movement for movement’s sake: ruts formed in clay, boots pressed into sod, stories swapping between travelers resting on logs or sharing slices of bread darkened by the fire. They tell of traders who crossed wide valleys, their journeys marked by the scent of tilled fields and the sound of rain against soil.

Within the home, the kitchen table is scattered with the remains of supper: a hunk of bread, rough and crusty; a knife with its handle darkened by years of hands; a bowl carved from walnut wood, filled with the last of the season’s nuts. In the garden beyond, rows of tubers wait beneath mounded earth, patient as the world spins above them. A spade leans against the shed, its blade dulled by pebbles and time, ready for tomorrow’s digging or today’s aimless lifting of turf. The world spins in cycles—seeds to harvest, dawn to dusk, question to question—while the wheels of the chariot turn, but not ahead.

If you sense the day slipping sideways, step outside and walk until you find the smell of earth after rain. Tie up a pair of old boots, tracing the same path again, and notice how they make every step heavier and more deliberate. Roast a few root vegetables in the coals, letting their sweetness deepen, and watch the steam rise as you open them. Sit upon a log or at a wooden table, lingering over a mug of strong coffee. These are ways to let stillness deepen into steadiness, to find rest within a journey that circles on itself.
