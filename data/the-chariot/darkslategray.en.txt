THE CHARIOT ― Reversed

On the Charioteer’s path, two powerful animals strain against their harness, each intent on its own direction, hooves scraping over the uneven flagstones. The chariot shudders. Forward is barely forward; every step becomes a tug-of-war. Command slips from the rider’s hands, and the journey dissolves into the mere act of motion, no arrival in sight. At the edges, the ancient roads run through moors where flat stones jut from the earth like the weathered backs of forgotten champions. Mist gathers in the hollows, drawing a curtain between now and next, and all around, the world is marked by the stoic patience of rock.

In the north, miners once broke open hillsides, seeking slate for the roofs of towns battered by rain and time. They lowered themselves down narrow shafts, leather boots scraping loose chips on the ladder’s rungs, faces smeared with the dust of uncounted centuries. That same slate waits in storerooms now, stacked and heavy, each slab remembering the pressure of ancient oceans pressed into sediment. In kitchens, cooks use boards hewn from that rock to serve cheese or bread, the platter grounding a meal in the weight of the earth. Craftsmen shape it into tabletops and garden steps, its surface cool and patient beneath the hands.

Elsewhere, along the high mountain passes, travelers pause beneath the outcrop of towering cliffs, their surfaces veined and furrowed by rain and frost. Fog drapes itself across these heights, muffling sound, blurring distance. Old shepherds learned to watch for changing skies: clouds thickening and lowering, heralding afternoons when rain comes steady and cold, hushing all conversation until only the steady drip from slate roofs fills the air. On such days, the world shrinks to the hearth and the thick coat sewn from felted wool, stitched by hands familiar with wind and salt. The fire’s glow throws long shadows on the walls, creating a sanctuary from the unsettled world outside.

In cities, the stone finds its way into alleyways, fitted into pavements worn smooth by thousands of hurried feet. The chill of early morning lingers there, echoing the quiet before traffic swells—an hour when the only movement is the sweep of a caretaker’s broom, bristles whispering over the close-joined seams. Between buildings, the air is damp, heavy with the promise of rain and the memory of storms. People passing through might find themselves pausing, tracing the line of a wall, feeling the steadiness that stands beneath paint and plaster: the reliable, unsung strength of quarried earth.

To ground oneself today, touch the top of a stone wall and note the pattern of moss in its crevices. Write a letter at the kitchen table, tracing the edge of a sturdy slate trivet as you think. Stand outside as the evening mist thickens, and listen to the softened sounds of your street. Wrap yourself in a coat that has weathered many seasons, and remember the patience of things that outlast decision and direction.
