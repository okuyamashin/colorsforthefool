THE CHARIOT ― Upright

Under the canopy held above the charioteer's head, the world narrows to the forward path. The two beasts at the harness stand poised, neither identical to the other, their breath misting the dawn. The city's stone gate waits ahead, boundary between the known and the open road. The grip on the reins is steady, not forceful—both creatures move when guided together, not tugged apart. The movement comes not from the will alone, but from the pairing and the partnership in motion. The canopy, stretched and unyielding, shelters the charioteer from the uncertain sky.

The road ahead is paved, yet scattered with bits of coal fallen from wagons that once rattled to the smithy. That ancient forge at the city's edge, with its hammered iron and smoldering embers, has long sent a line of smoke into the air—its soot dusts the chariot's wheels as it passes. The scent of that forge hangs in the air, patient and deep. The beasts' harnesses shine with the polish of old shoe-leather, remnants from the cobbler's bench where hands shaped thick hides into boots and satchels for those who would travel. In the city's low quarter, a baker draws a tray of rye loaves from the hearth, the crusts crackling and charred where grain met flame.

Beyond the gate lie fields where the crow walks, head bobbing as it plucks a beetle from the furrow. This bird, ink-feathered and sharp-eyed, rides the air on a morning wind. The charioteer's mind drifts to the tale of the raven that marked old boundaries, signaling where the forest ended and the long road began. Travelers, too, recall nights under a sky without stars, lost in the velvet pitch of a new moon. Beneath such a sky, the compass and the keen sense of direction are sharpened by necessity; a lantern's light seems brighter when it must cut through such deepness.

History rides with the chariot—traders in lacquered wood from distant lands, their boxes and bowls sealed by a polished finish that resists the damp, prized for the way they preserve and protect. Along the empire's river, barges once carried cargo wrapped in silk, weighed down with obsidian from volcanic shores. The stone, cold in the hand, was shaped into arrowheads or polished into mirrors, reflecting a determined face back to itself. In the market at the foot of the city wall, the charcoal-seller pours powder into a clay pot for ink, traded by scribes who record victories and treaties onto scrolls.

Weather gathers at the horizon, heavy with the promise of a storm. The air thickens, and the charioteer recalls how farmers pull their cloaks close when thunderclouds roll in, and horses are led into the shelter of barns roofed in tar. Rain, when it comes, coats the cobblestones, pooling between the stones so that the chariot's wheels send up a fine spray.

In quiet rooms at dusk, families burn dried plum branches in the brazier, smoke curling as tea is brewed in kettles turned dark with age. On winter mornings, the hearth is swept with a brush made of willow, the bristles singed from last year's fires. The city's night watchman, lantern swinging, checks the locks and boards on market stalls, his footsteps echoing as he passes the old anvil in the smith's yard.

Today, a person can polish their shoes with the same care a charioteer gives to leather harness. Place a crow's feather on a windowsill and watch the breeze lift it. Brew a cup of roasted barley tea, letting the aroma drift through the room, or write a goal on a sheet thick with ink and tuck it away for safekeeping.
