THE CHARIOT ― Reversed

A pair of untamed animals, yoked to an uncertain chariot, strain in opposite directions. The driver holds the reins, but the wheels grind—not forward, not back, only spinning in place. The road beneath is layered with old stones, shaped and laid by hands long gone, rough to the feet, pitted by centuries of rain and cartwheels. This is not the shining parade ground, but an alley behind the fortress, where nothing is painted or polished, and the task is only to haul and to move, for the sake of moving.

There is a roof of slate above the driver’s head. Each shingle is heavy in the hand, cut from beds of rock pulled from the earth in cold upland quarries, stacked and nailed with careful labor. Storm after storm, sleet and biting wind, the roof endures; long after banners rot on the main gables, these tiles remain, steady and unspectacular. Water beads on their surface, then runs off, finding the gutter’s cracked iron pipe. The sound is steady: a slow, even drumming, louder in autumn winds. The house beneath is warm but dim, the rooms close and practical, the floor swept but never shined.

In the valley beyond the town, morning fog drifts off the river, thick and patient. The sheep are out, their coats tangled, the grass flattened by dew. Farmhands walk the rocky path, boots slick with mud. They carry bundles of split slate to mend a wall that’s given way under its own weight, piece after piece fitted together, not for beauty, only for strength. The quarry is silent now, but the scraps remain—broken slabs, sharp-edged, stacked in heaps, waiting to be used or weathered down to nothing.

On the worktable by the window stands a stone ink well. Next to it, a ledger with a cover of rough-dyed wool, spine cracked and corners frayed. The pen is steel, the ink a slow trickle, each line a record of small transactions—milk sold, tools repaired, cart hired for the next uncertain journey. The ledger’s pages are smudged, not from carelessness, but from constant handling, thumbed over each market day.

Outside, the clouds settle low and stubborn. Rain has been falling since dawn, a steady curtain, making puddles in the yard, sheeting down the church’s slate spire, dappling the windows with cold moisture. Children press their palms to the glass, tracing paths in the condensation, drawing imaginary maps of places they might go if the streets ever dried. The wind carries the chill of high moorland, a scent of lichen and stone dust.

To step into this day, one might set a kettle on the stove and begin to patch an old slate on the garden shed, or sweep water from the doorstep with a stiff-bristled broom. A letter could be written in careful script, finished and sealed, then carried to the postbox through the drizzle. There is quiet resolve in mending what endures, in walking out even when the road is uncertain, and in tending small tasks without fanfare.
