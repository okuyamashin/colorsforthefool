THE CHARIOT ― Upright

A figure rides beneath a canopy stitched high above a sturdy chariot, where two creatures—horse and stallion, lion and bull, twin hounds—stand alert at the gates of a city. The reins, held firm, bring order to their eager force. This is the moment before the city opens, when the charioteer, poised and resolved, stands beneath a gleam that catches on the metal crest above, reflecting the first clear day after storm.

In ancient times, such a vehicle would bear a plate hammered from the ore that miners drew from deep earth. The craftsman’s hands, blackened by soot, would turn that raw bounty into shields, cups, canteens—each one prized by merchants, travelers, and those who judged value by weight and purity. In the market streets beyond the city walls, coinage minted from this same substance passed from hand to hand, promising safe passage, fair trade, and the right to keep moving forward. A knight’s armor bore the same glint, fashioned for parades as much as for battle, promising both spectacle and protection. The chariot’s accouterments drew the eye of passerby, catching the changing weather—raindrops, morning haze, the flash of sunlight after cloud.

When myth takes the reins, the messenger gods speed across the sky in sandals and helmets crafted from this metal, declaring the start of journeys and the swift completion of tasks. Even in the hush of a monastery, the chalice reserved for great ceremonies sits heavy on the altar, its surface smooth and cool to the touch, reflecting candlelight in shifting patterns. Some believe that the element harnesses the moon’s quiet pull, steadying the heart’s rhythm for those who must travel by night, granting assurance as the city’s gate swings open.

The canopy overhead, though, is more than a shield. In far-off lands, it becomes the tent of a nomad, its frame set with threads worked through with fine wire. In kitchens, utensils made from the same lustrous substance serve at feasts and humble tables alike—spoons and forks, trays and saltcellars, all bearing the same promise of dignity and trust. Healers, too, have valued its properties, fashioning tools delicate enough to mend wounds and strong enough to endure heat and pressure. The doctor’s scalpel and the bishop’s spoon belong to the same family of objects, each carrying the sense of purpose that guides the disciplined forward.

Sometimes, the weather itself brings a clue. The morning after a cold front, rooftops and roads are glazed with a sheen that invites boots to step surely and wheels to roll faster. In the north, fog lifts to reveal the river’s surface, shining and calm, the boundary between what has been left behind and what lies ahead. The city’s gate is not so much an obstacle as an invitation—step through, keep pace, match the movement of the two beasts. The canopy, the harness, the gear: all are extensions of the will, the objects that let one direct motion and claim a new direction.

Today, someone can polish a keepsake cup that echoes old journeys, or use a favorite spoon to stir an early-morning drink, feeling the weight in the hand as a reminder of resolve. Place a coin in a pocket before stepping out, or trace a finger along the rim of a compact mirror, letting it catch the light as the day begins. The chariot’s movement is not a rush but a gathering of intention, a steady, paired progress toward what waits beyond the city gate.
