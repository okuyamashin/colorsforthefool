THE CHARIOT ― Reversed

On the old mosaic floor, two sphinxes face away from each other, their paws set in different directions, the traces slack between them. The driver, instead of holding the reins taut, lets them dangle. The vehicle rattles forward, not because of intention, but because the wheels must roll somewhere. In the ancient city, the chariot is a thing of pride, but today its journey is restless, circling the same stone streets, turning corners only to see the same facades again: the forge with its iron scraps piled near the door, the distant smoke of coal drifting from the bakery’s oven, the flagstones worn smooth by countless feet. The air is heavy with the promise of rain that never quite arrives, and the clouds overhead are the same as the soot gathered on the rooftops.

Legend tells of times when the charioteer would drive with purpose—toward the gates, through the mountain pass, wheels slicing through morning mist. But now, with beasts unfocused and roads uncertain, the chariot moves only for movement’s sake, its axle creaking. Along the roadside, river stones lie scattered, picked by children for games or carried in pockets for luck. They are cool to the touch, their surfaces dulled from years of water passing over them, reminders of patient journeys made by the slow, persistent current. A merchant, passing by, hoists a sack filled with metal tools—hammers, hooks, and horseshoes—each worn with the dull shine of frequent use. In another lane, an old umbrella leans forgotten against a doorway, its fabric crumpled from too many storms. Above, the sky remains close, the color of morning fog lying stubborn over the harbor.

The great cities of history—London after rain, Tokyo in the hush before dawn—have known these days: when the streets are veiled by thick, low clouds, and chimney smoke drifts along alleyways, weaving between towers and temples. In the countryside, sheep shelter near boulders flecked with lichen, stones gathered over centuries and holding the memory of countless cold dawns. Sailors once trusted the steady hue of smoke from distant ships as a sign of coming weather, reading stories in the curl and stretch above the horizon. Miners underground worked by the faint gleam of graphite, tracing lines on rough stone when daylight could not reach them, trusting their hammers and chisels forged in shadowed workshops.

In kitchens, cast-iron pans rest atop the stove, their surfaces seasoned by years of careful tending, ready to take on the next meal. In quiet corners, an old journal waits, its cover soft and worn, pages filled with sketches of far-off mountains and moody seas. Even in the bustle of markets, coins pass from hand to hand, cool and solid, bearing the imprint of places and times beyond the present. The world pauses under this sky, and movement becomes its own pursuit, directionless as the chariot circling its familiar block, yet persistent.

Today, someone may choose to sharpen a tool, tending to what has grown dull through use, or to draw a map of the neighborhood, tracing paths that always circle home. Another may set a pot of water to boil, listening for the first slow bubbles, or simply walk beneath the heavy clouds, matching their own breathing to the steady rhythm of shoe against cobblestone.
