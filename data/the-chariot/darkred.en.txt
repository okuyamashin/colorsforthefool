THE CHARIOT ― Reversed

On the card, the driver stands between two creatures, harnessed but straining in opposite directions. Their paws worry the ground, neither pulling forward nor yielding, their energy feeding only a struggle against each other. The chariot itself rocks and trembles, wheels locked by indecision, while dust gathers at its base. The journey has lost its destination; the motion is all that’s left.

This tension recalls the relentless river that runs through old canyons, carving stone but sometimes caught between two jagged cliffs. In ancient lands, travelers followed the trail of iron-rich clay, walking between hills that looked almost scorched after rain. At dusk, the sky sometimes thickened with cloud so heavy and low that it pressed down like a lid, promising a storm but delivering only a slow, smoldering drizzle. Shepherds knew not to wander in such weather; they watched from under eaves as the earth darkened, as if drawing in the memory of old wounds.

In cities, the forge once glowed late into night, its hearth lined with brick from the river’s banks. The smith hammered away, chasing shape from chaos, but when the fire burned unevenly, the metal lost its temper and bent in the wrong ways. The apprentices swept the floor, gathering fallen nails and small, rough coins, their faces lit by sparks that danced and died before settling onto the worn leather apron. Sometimes, the youngest would pocket a pomegranate, rolling it in their hand, its skin tough and almost impenetrable, its juice staining their fingers in secret.

Storytellers spoke of the vineyards far from the city walls, where grapevines twisted against old posts, their clusters hidden beneath thick leaves. In late summer, workers came with knives and baskets, careful to avoid spilling what would later be pressed and stored in jars sealed with wax. The wine cellars held air that was always cool and sharp, as if holding its breath, waiting for the next festival or the return of the one who had gone away. Under torchlight, the amphorae glimmered, lined up like sentinels in the dark.

The kitchen holds its own reminders. On the stove, a cast-iron pan waits, seasoned by years of use. Root vegetables roast inside, their skins wrinkling and splitting just slightly, releasing an aroma that clings to the walls. On the counter, a wooden pestle crushes dried chili pods, scattering seeds across the cutting board. A cup sits nearby, filled with cherry jam, its surface set thick and sweet, the spoon resting like a flag after battle.

There are ways to gather yourself when everything pulls in different directions. One can walk along the riverbank after rain, listening to the water argue with the stones and letting each step anchor the wandering mind. In the kitchen, stirring the pot slowly or pressing fruit into jars can turn restless hands into ritual, a reminder that not all movement is progress, yet some is necessary. If the sky threatens a heavy storm, closing the shutters and lighting a small fire can steady the air inside. Or, one might simply hold a pomegranate, turning it in hand, feeling its weight and strength, and knowing that even divided seeds can shape a harvest.
