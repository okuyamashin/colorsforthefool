THE CHARIOT ― Upright

The canopy above the chariot whispers of shelter and command, a stitched testament to movement’s direction. Beneath, two beasts are poised: neither identical nor wild, but partners joined by the length of paired reins. They wait before a city’s ancient gate, stone sentinels carved with the marks of a thousand departures and arrivals. The driver stands, not sitting, body braced to the cart’s low rail, cloak gathered against the morning wind as if he has just cast off the dust of sleep and now prepares to push beyond the boundary.

In old stories, the charioteer is likened to a captain at sea, and the beasts to the twin prows of a royal barge gliding along the Thames during a coronation. The canopy—embroidered with the crest of a house, sturdy as the tent of a campaign—reminds one of the stalls of London’s covered markets, where fishmongers and bookbinders convene beneath fabric roofs, and each morning, the city wakes to the clatter of cartwheels on stone. The gate might be that of an ancient capital, where messengers once arrived with scrolls sealed in wax, and the beasts could be the faithful breeds bred to pull such burdens: horses with manes like the wild rivers of the north, oxen steady as the hands that fit crown to brow.

Forward motion gathers as the rein is paired, the driver tightening his grip. The chariot does not swerve because its path is a channel; the wheels follow the ruts left by those who came before. The reins themselves may be cut from old sailcloth, the kind dyed for naval uniforms, tough enough to bridge a river or hold a sail against a gale. The fabric of command and journey, once made only for those with the right to bear a fleet’s colors, now hangs from the shoulders of travelers, their coats fastened with gleaming brass buttons, their boots stained with the salt of distant harbors.

When the gate opens, the air is clear after rain—the kind of weather that arrives suddenly in northern kingdoms, when a downpour cleanses the city’s rooftops and leaves puddles reflecting spires and banners. In the distance, a regiment drills in the palace yard, their standards catching the late sunlight; every motion precise, discipline tied to a heritage of guides and victories. The chariot moves, passing through the gate, not with haste but with purpose: the driver’s eyes fixed on the road’s vanishing point, the beasts in step, every muscle tuned to the pull. There is no hesitation, only the discipline of coupling intention with action, as the household matron couples her sewing needle with the cloth to mend a favorite jacket for her child’s first crossing of the threshold.

Today, one might draw a curtain back and watch the sky after a storm, or walk the avenue of a park where the air is sharp and flags ripple along the tree-lined path. Write a letter with ink on heavy paper, sealing it with wax before sending it out into the world; polish the brass buckles of old boots, or mend a jacket at the kitchen table, remembering every journey that begins with a hand on the reins.
