THE MOON ― Upright

Night shimmers across the water as the tide rolls in, moving between two silent towers. The path here is not wholly clear, shrouded in soft, uncertain light. A dog and a wolf pause on the shore, both creatures alert to the changes in the air, one tame and one wild, both beneath the pull of something older than memory. Overhead, a lantern of the sky is veiled by slow-moving clouds. The mudflats and pebbles glisten as the water recedes, revealing a trail that seems to go nowhere, or everywhere, at once.

Somewhere in northern hills, layered stones are split from ancient quarries. These stones, hard as old oaths, have roofed cottages and walled gardens for centuries. Fishermen know their weight by feel, matching their heft to the tools that scrape barnacles from boats. Past the fields, these broken stones border the heather, where the air turns cold and the wind carries the scent of rain. Here, under a moon like tonight's, villagers have gathered for generations. Stories are passed around a table where the old tea set sits, its surface glazed to a gentle sheen, recalling the subtle dusk outside.

The path between towers is the one walked by night-watchers and those who keep secrets. In the city, the same shade clings to the high slate of rooftops, where chimney sweeps once worked at dawn. Chimneys and tiles shelter hidden gardens, pots of violets pressed to the glass where the moon’s reflection is caught and held. On such a night, markets are closed, but behind shuttered stalls, drapers’ cloth waits folded on the shelf. This is the shade chosen for a certain kind of velvet, lining trunks and jackets, as if to guard against the cold or keep a whisper safe within its folds. Under that fabric, letters are hidden, pressed petals, or a coin meant for luck.

Long ago, the same color was prized by merchants who traded along the coast. Dyes brewed from wild plants would stain hands and barrels alike, the color deepening as it dried in the evening air. Sailors’ scarves and banners were made from it, so that even on misty mornings at sea, their ships could be known to friend and stranger. In the distance, storms rolled over the horizon, their thunder softened by the fog, their danger felt but not yet seen. The weather on such nights was uncertain, the earth slick with dew, and the only sure thing was the promise of the returning tide.

Tonight, the moon’s glow is uncertain, but it traces the edge of a path in stone and cloud and cloth. To honor it, a person might walk beneath the towers where the night air is cold and the stones are worn smooth with centuries of passage. One might brew tea in the oldest pot, letting the steam rise in the quiet kitchen. Another might open a trunk to find a scarf, velvet and timeworn, the scent of dried heather pressed into its folds. Or, in the hush before dawn, fingers might trace the ridge of a slate roof, feeling for the place where the rain always lingers.
