THE MOON ― Upright

On the edge of midnight, under the gaze of the shifting moon, a winding trail shivers between two old stone towers. The ground is soft, dew rising, pawprints pressing close where a dog sniffs with ears low and a wolf pauses, nose lifted to the damp air. The path is never fully clear, the world half-glimpsed as the moon’s light drifts over brambles. Pools along the roadside hold the sky, rippled with the tide’s distant pull, and the hush is broken only by a far-off bell.

Between these towers, the world smells of dusk and brush, but also of something more tender—the scent the wind carries from the fields beyond, where rows of small, pale flowers are gathered in the cool hours before dawn. Hands moving through these rows have learned a gentle patience, plucking spikes for satchels, tying them in bundles to hang in kitchens or tuck into linen drawers. The fragrance lingers on fingertips: clean, sharp, a little sweet, and always hinting at places out of sight. In Provençal hills and English wolds, these fields have lain for centuries, their harvests prized for the oil pressed from tiny blooms, stored in glass and cork and carried over long roads to markets.

In ancient apothecaries, the dried sprigs were bound into wreaths and sachets to ward off shadow and soothe dreams, tucked beneath pillows by those who feared what prowled the night. On the threshold of sleep, a cup of steam rose from water poured over those same gathered flowers, filling chambers with warmth. The vapor marked the passage from waking to dreaming, a guide for those stepping onto uncertain paths. Seafarers rubbed the leaves between their fingers, counting on the sharp scent to steady their thoughts as tides pulled them far from home.

Weather sometimes brings the bloom, though not by hand: in the high valleys, a morning mist sweeps low, softening the hills, and the earth exhales the same perfume as the first sun glances off the fields. Shepherds, alone with their flocks, have known that hush and its comfort; the sound of wool brushing through rows of stalks, the pulse of bees, the slow turn of clouds above. There, the world renews its boundaries between waking and the half-lit, between the tame and the wild.

In homes, small bottles wait on shelves, ready to be tipped into bathwater or pressed onto the temples before sleep. Old tablecloths, hand-dyed and faded through many washings, keep their subtle scent after years in cedar chests. Letters posted from far-off places might carry a pressed flower within, a keepsake for those left behind. The flower’s presence, whether as scent, herb, or memory, is quiet but insistent—a thread through shadows, marking a way when light thins.

Someone today might brew a gentle tisane, letting the steam rise as a reminder to move slowly through uncertainty. You can rub a sprig between your hands before sleep, or slip one into your pocket before crossing a threshold that feels strange. Hang a small bundle over a doorway, or write a note to someone who needs reassurance, tucking inside the promise of a small, faded bloom.
