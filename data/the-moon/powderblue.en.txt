THE MOON ― Upright

Between two towers, a path coils along the edge of what can be seen. The way is half shrouded by mist, half revealed by a lantern’s thin light. Tides draw gleaming lines across the sand, glimmering as they retreat into the tangled marsh. A dog’s ear pricks at the distant sound of the surf. A wolf, wild-eyed, stands at the path’s far bend, listening to the call of a world both familiar and unknown.

Myth gathers here like dew, delicate and uncertain. On the old salt roads near the sea, travelers told stories of a rare mineral—ground to fine powder by hands stained with labor, sifting through the shadows of the cliffside at dawn. This mineral, prized since the time of the ancient glassblowers, was said to steady the mind through nights of uncertainty. In the moon’s half-light, it was scattered over bread dough to keep it from sticking; it was blended into soap to lift away the traces of fatigue from the skin. Sailors tucked it into little canvas pouches, hoping its presence would guard them on foggy crossings, when the lighthouse beam flickered and the compass needle quivered with doubt.

Far from the coast, high country mornings sometimes open under a sky that lingers after rain, when the air is washed clean and quiet. Old-timers in such places remember spring floods when river ice, crushed and swirling, left a residue along the banks. At dawn, these deposits caught the earliest light and seemed to glow from within, a gentle promise that the way forward—though not fully seen—was possible. In towns built on trade, clothiers once dyed their finest muslin with this same powder, using it for christening bonnets and festival ribbons. Young mothers lined cradles with it, believing it would soften their children’s dreams. On the continent, a certain weather would roll in off the steppes, a fine haze that settled on the windowsills and softened the outlines of everything it touched.

Inside the house, someone sits at a worn kitchen table, stirring water in a glazed jug. On the shelf above, a jar holds the last of the year’s dried violets, pressed between layers of starch dust. The air carries the scent of the tide, faint but insistent, reminding the listener of both the wolf’s longing and the dog’s loyalty. In such moments, the only certainty is the path beneath one’s feet, the knowledge that the towers will stand through another night, and the hope that the tide will bring what was lost back to shore.

Today, you might knead dough with a patient hand, letting the flour trickle through your fingers. You could walk at dawn, pausing where river mist hovers above the path, letting the silence steady you. Perhaps you open the window a crack to let in morning air, or place a washed stone on your desk as a reminder of what the tide brings and takes away.
