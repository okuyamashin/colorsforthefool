THE MOON ― Reversed

The card turns, and the night becomes opaque and mutable: the old stone bridge is lost behind the swelling mist, and the dog’s familiar bark is swallowed by the echoing silence of the riverbank. Where the wolf once crouched in shadow, only a damp outline remains, as if the creature has faded into the marsh reeds and the shallow pools that gather beneath the willows. The world has dissolved into a hush so deep that even the breeze seems to hesitate. Out on the path, the moon is veiled, clouds drawn tight, and the difference between wild and tame is pressed into the same sodden earth.

In such a landscape, everything takes on the patience of slate. The roof tiles on the abandoned cottage near the water’s edge have weathered decades of rain and frost, their rough faces slick with dew and the memory of old footsteps. Masons once hoisted these stones from a quarry carved into a hillside, each one sheared by hand and set in careful rows, meant to bear the weight of seasons and the passing of generations. The same stone lines the old village well, its circular lip worn smooth by the touch of a thousand hands, each lowering a bucket, drawing up a mirror of the clouded sky. At dusk, children used to press their palms to the cold rim, daring each other to listen for whispers in the dark water below, believing that the well kept old secrets in its cool depths.

Just beyond the cottage, the hillside is laced with the remains of a path once used by shepherds. On misty mornings, sheep would gather along its uneven edges, their wool heavy with droplets, their movement silent except for the gentle tap of their hooves on the compacted earth. In quiet weather, the shepherd’s crook rested against a patch of lichen-spotted flagstone, overlooked now by bracken and nettles. Sometimes, when the fog sits low, the outlines of old tools appear: a forgotten chisel, part of a spade, left behind by those who shaped the hillside’s bones into shelter and hearth.

On days when the air is thick with mist and nothing is certain, the village leans into shelter: women draw heavy shawls across their shoulders, woven with the coarse threads spun from hardy mountain sheep. By the fire, the kettle hums, its battered tin rim holding the warmth of tea and the comfort of hands curled around clay mugs. The hearth itself is ringed with slices of quarried stone, each one a memory of labor, laid together in the hope of holding back the dark. On such nights, it is right to light a lantern and place it in the window, a beacon for travelers picking their way through the uncertain dark, their boots sinking into the mossy, rain-laden pathway.

There are ways to meet this returning night: walking the lane in the hush after the last rainfall, pausing to place a palm on the cold stone of the well, or sitting by the window with a shawl drawn close, listening for the distant call of an owl. One can set a kettle to boil and watch the steam curl and vanish, or press a found stone into the hearth as a quiet remembrance of those who shaped the path before.
