THE MOON ― Upright

By the thin light of the moon, a dog lifts its nose to the night wind, while a wolf moves beside it, each step slow along the uncertain path. The tide’s hush marks the edge where land slips to water. Two towers rise, watching, but they show little about what lies ahead. Between them, the ground is pale and shifting, as if the night itself cannot decide what to reveal and what to keep secret.

There is a field outside a northern village where the harvest waits. Each stalk is brushed by the moon, standing close together, a sea of waving heads that whisper when the wind crosses. During the day, these fields feed the village—people walk the furrows, watching for the damp shimmer that means morning dew, working hands sweeping scythes low, gathering the year’s promise in armfuls. At night, the same field becomes a place of shadows, the line between furrow and footpath blurred, the dog and wolf’s world. There are old stories that say the wildest dreams take root where these plants once lay thickest, that the moon’s glance at the field makes the line between thought and reality thin.

The grain from these fields becomes bread, the bread that sits on the rough-hewn table inside a cottage, covered with a woven cloth. That cloth, plain but sturdy, is made from the fibers left over after the harvest, spun in the long evenings when the wind rattles loose stalks against the wall. Sometimes, in the hush before dawn, someone will rise and pull open the door just to watch the mist curl over the stubbled earth, imagining the ancient caravans that once crossed the plains, each sack of grain a measure of trust and livelihood.

In the city’s market, sacks line the stalls—some bound for busy kitchens, others for distilleries, yet more for merchants whose family names are known along every river route. The trade is older than any map, and every handful suggests the old bargain between risk and reward, the pact of rain and drought. The scent that rises from warm, freshly baked loaves in a baker’s window recalls countless seasons: long days under a sky too wide for comfort, nights filled with the distant sound of jackals and the nearer song of frogs tucked into the reeds by the stream.

On some mornings, the sky is low and thick, and the light that falls on the field is gentle, neither one thing nor another. The air is scented with earth and the sharp, grassy memory of storms past. Farmers pause, boots planted deep, to gauge whether the day will bring rain or clear, and a woman in the next cottage watches the weather to know when to set the bread dough by the window, trusting the air to be kind.

Today, you might walk a quiet road beside a field, letting your hand drift across the tops of tall grasses. You might knead dough in your kitchen, thinking of the hands that have done the same for generations. You might lay a woven cloth on your table at dusk, or steep in the hush before dawn and imagine being guided by the uncertain light that makes each step a question.
