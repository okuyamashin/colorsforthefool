THE MOON ― Reversed

The card gazes down, a pale coin blurred by unseen breath. The towers that keep their silent watch on either side of the path are now indistinct, guardians with softened edges. Between them, the muddy track winds through a landscape where the familiar marks of paw and claw dissolve into one another. Night has returned, but not with stark contrast. Instead, it comes wrapped in the hush that settles after a late spring fog, when even the call of the owl is muffled, and the dog and wolf by the water's edge seem as if they have always shared the same name.

There is a countryside in early dawn where the orchards bloom and the air is thick with the scent of petals. When the wind drifts off the river, it carries with it a dampness that clings to the old climbing roses, their blossoms cupped and heavy, each flower a brief exhalation of color held in the creases of a morning that never quite grows bright. Gardeners once gathered these blooms before the sun, laying them atop embroidered linens spread for tea in the grass, the cloth stitched with recollections of herb-collectors who walked out before breakfast to pull dew from the petals. In the kitchen, rosewater was poured into small glass bottles, a faint perfume to brush wrist and collar, a memory of vanished gardens.

Not far from those kitchen windows, the mist wandered in from the sea, settling over trading harbors and fish markets. On certain mornings, the fog would be thick enough to obscure the edge of the quay, blurring the line between land and water. The bakers, up before any bell, dusted their hands with flour and folded it into morning loaves. Their aprons, washed and sun-dried, hung by the door, soft with the residue of warmth and years of gentle laundering. Fishermen returned home, hands curled around baskets lined with cloth to keep the catch cool, the fabric the same shade as the almond cakes their wives shaped from ground seeds and honey, eaten while the mist still lingered outside.

Along the old Silk Road, traders packed dried rose petals and spun sugar into tiny confections to sustain them during long crossings. They wrapped the sweets in tissue-thin paper to protect them from the wet air that sometimes crept into their tents at dawn. The memory of home traveled with them, a scent that clung to their scarves and sleeves, a comfort against the dreams that came with sleep beneath a strange moon. Elsewhere, the first daughters of a northern household learned to embroider, tracing out petals and curling vines onto squares of muslin that would, with time, be stitched into christening gowns and laid away for generations.

Tonight, you may gather rose petals from the garden, scattering them in a shallow bowl beside your bed. A linen square, pressed beneath a favorite book, will hold the faintest memory of a forgotten bloom. You might stir a drop of rosewater or honey into your evening tea, or rest your hands on a soft cloth as you listen for the last footsteps fading into mist. In the silence, the path through the uncertain night offers only the names you give it, and the gentle comfort of things kept close.
