THE MOON ― Reversed

On the card, the wolf and the dog raise their snouts to the shifting sky, a boundary blurring in the darkness. Tidal water creeps cold over stones. The path dissolves into reeds and mist, and even the moon’s face seems hidden behind layers of cloud. In the hush, the familiar becomes strange; voices are muffled, and the safe outline of home is edged away by confusion. Tonight, the known gives way to the unknown, as if the land itself slips into a memory not wholly remembered.

This is not the night of revelation, but of pause, where a footstep forward may land in dew-heavy grass or in the sting of a bramble. The fields lie fallow under the hush. Out past the gate, the wild plants that thrive in forgotten corners stand watch. Their stems are strong, their leaves serrated, their crowns a crown of spines and tufts—tough, uninviting, yet stubbornly alive. Once, these plants marked the rough edges of ancient pathways and the borders of land no plough dared claim. Their roots grip the soil where little else will grow, threading through legend and song. Highland hills, low stone fences, and wind-carved pastures know this shape, a sentinel against hunger, an emblem of endurance.

Folk tales traveled on the wind, telling of guardianship and bitterness, of the day when an invading army’s march was betrayed by a barefoot soldier’s cry. The plant’s defense became the people’s shield, its stubbornness a rallying sign, embroidered into banners and etched in coins. In frostbitten kitchens, its young shoots were once boiled to sustain hungry families, the flavor sharp, the promise of nourishment won only by patient fingers willing to strip away the armor. Elsewhere, in the hush of late summer afternoons, nimble hands twisted fibers from its stalks to weave coarse thread, patching holes in worn-out cloth or tying bundles for market.

Beneath the clouded moon, dew gathers on the spines and petals, a chill that settles into clothing and seeps through thin walls. In cottages that lean into the wind, dried heads of the plant are hung above doorways, believed to ward off misfortune and wandering spirits. Travelers, uncertain of the path ahead, pluck its flower and tuck it into their packs for protection. Storms roll across the moors, and even the sheep leave these plants untouched, their prickly defense undiminished by weather or time. Some say that when dreams are troubled or direction lost, steeping its leaves with honey can settle a restless heart.

Tonight, you might wander the threshold, marking places where wild things root and thrive. Steep a tea from foraged leaves, carefully prepared. Carry a sprig as a token in your pocket, or place a dried flower under the pillow to invite uneasy dreams to speak. Pause by a field’s edge and trace the outline of ancient stories, letting old symbols keep their silent vigil through the night.
