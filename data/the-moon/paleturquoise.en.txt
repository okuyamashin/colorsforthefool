THE MOON ― Upright

A narrow road winds through marshland, uncertain in the dimness, its surface slick from the recent rise of the tide. A dog lopes along, tongue drawn to the damp air, while a wolf emerges at the path’s edge, wild eyes reflecting the faint light. Between them, a strip of earth forms a bridge over shallow water. Towers stand far apart, looming out of bulrushes. Clouds gather around the moon, thin enough to let through a gauzy glow that spills across the land, tracing only the outlines of things: the stones, the reeds, the pools that mark where the sea has crept in. The horizon is barely visible—everything hovers on the edge of mystery.

In distant lands, stories begin at river crossings where traders once waited for ferries, their goods bundled in cloth dyed with minerals drawn from the earth. These bundles, wrapped tightly, hold polished stones that people believe were found at the bottom of mountain lakes, smoothed by centuries of water and wind. In some places, house walls are covered with plaster made from the same riverbed silt, giving rooms a cool, tranquil feeling on humid nights. Old tales say these walls can shelter dreams, keeping out the confusion of the world beyond.

Within an old village near brackish water, a bathhouse stands, built with tiles shaped like fish scales. The pools here are filled just after dusk, the surface still as glass, and steam rises quietly into the rafters. If you dip your hand, the water feels like the air before a rainstorm—soft, almost slippery. On the benches, folded towels, woven by hand, rest beside simple wooden combs. The attendants speak in low voices, reminding visitors to move slowly and to listen for the sound of returning cranes from the distant marsh.

Somewhere inland, far from the sea’s reach, shepherds keep their flocks beneath the open sky. They wear scarves dyed with plants that grow only where streams bend and slow. These scarves are prized not for their showiness, but for their capacity to soothe—a gentle fabric to wrap around the neck at sundown, or to fold beneath the head when sleeping on the grass. On festival days, families string beads onto cords, each bead a memory of calm waters and clear mornings. They exchange these as tokens, a promise that no matter how winding the journey, there is always a way back to safety.

In the kitchen of a weathered house, a pot of rice simmers beside a bowl carved from river-stone, its surface cool to the touch. On the windowsill, pressed leaves from a far-off shore dry in a ceramic dish, their scent mingling with the salt carried in from the estuary. A journal lies open on the table, its cover embossed with the image of a heron. The ink inside flows steadily, recording dreams that seem just out of reach, like the moonlit path outside—half seen, half guessed.

Tonight, you might prepare a bath and let the steam calm your thoughts. You could string your own necklace from smooth stones, or wrap a soft scarf around your shoulders, pausing to breathe deeply at the open window. Perhaps you will write quietly in a journal, letting the world outside settle into its own slow rhythm.
