THE HIEROPHANT ― Upright

The hall is hushed, filled with the scent of polished wood and ancient parchment, and at the center stands the Hierophant, draped in ceremonial layers. Between his fingers, he holds the keys—one for the world of devotion, one for the world of the flesh. At his feet, two attendants bow, mirroring the rituals passed down through generations. Behind him, columns rise, etched with prayers and bearing the marks of a hundred rites. The air here is thick with the weight of tradition, each gesture an echo of the past, each word a step along a path shaped by countless feet before.

On the altar rests a set of carved tokens, each shaped from material once grown as part of a great beast, carried across deserts and rivers by traders who bartered in the glow of oil lamps. The tokens are cool to the touch, smooth as river stones but far more precious, for their story precedes memory. Centuries ago, kings and priests alike prized them, fashioning the hilts of ceremonial daggers, the tips of scepters, or the slats of ancient chessboards. In the great temples of Africa and India, artisans once carved entire scrolls of wisdom onto their surface, trusting that what was inscribed here would endure.

Elsewhere in the sanctuary, a low table is set for a quiet meal. A small spoon, its handle shaped as a swan, rests beside a bowl of rice—a vessel whose sheen is the result of careful burnishing by hands skilled in inherited craft. The spoon’s material, rare and coveted, is the product of a network of merchants who risked monsoon and drought, hoping for a season of peace. There are stories, too, of tools and buttons, fans and tiny beads, all modeled from this same precious matter. In grand houses and modest cottages alike, tea is poured into cups whose smoothness recalls the discipline of artisans long gone.

In certain corners of the world, rain falls so softly and evenly that it hushes the city’s anger, cloaking morning in a gentle veil. This is the weather that slows time, drawing families closer around old piano keys, their surface worn with years of sonatas and hymns. The light that filters through temple screens reflects off altar ornaments, casting a gentle glow on the faces of the faithful. In these moments, one feels the persistence of custom, the gentle assurance of a way that has not strayed far from its source.

Tonight, to honor the ways handed down, one might run a hand over the surface of a carved keepsake or light a candle in a family shrine. Preparing a meal with an old, heirloom utensil can evoke the patience of those who came before. Reading aloud from a book with pressed, heavy pages, or writing a letter with a pen tipped in rare material, will invite reflection on lineages both spiritual and familial.
