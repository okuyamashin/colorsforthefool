THE HIEROPHANT ― Reversed

The Hierophant sits in his ornate chair, hands raised in benediction, a book open before him and pillars framing his throne. The scene is one of tradition, of order held by custom and rite, but when the card turns, the weight of centuries flickers. The solemn chant echoes in an empty nave. Incense smolders in an old iron censer, its smoke trailing in coils toward the vaulted ceiling, but the meaning behind the ritual has wandered off, leaving only the hollow sound of footfalls on flagstones and the slap of rain on the abbey roof. The Hierophant’s robes, once symbols, now drape around him like a workman’s overcoat hung on a peg after a long day.

In the world outside, the town wakes beneath a sky heavy with low clouds, the kind that press over slate roofs and make the air chill and still. Along the lanes, the cobblestones shine with last night’s rain, muddied water collecting in the dips, carrying the memory of footsteps and carriage wheels. The stone mason’s hands are stained with dust as he shapes another weathered lintel, his chisel echoing through the morning. In the fields beyond, lambs huddle under the drystone wall, its surface rough and cool to the touch, mapping the boundary between the ordinary and the sacred. The old shepherd’s cloak, woven for resilience, is thrown over his shoulders, patched at the elbows where it has worn thin from daily use.

The hearth in the kitchen is hardly more than embers now, but someone stirs a pot, the surface of the iron kettle catching the faintest gleam. The cook’s apron has been splattered through years of stews and bread, and the mark of flour on her hands is never truly washed away. Each day, she grinds the chestnuts for porridge, the sound steady and unchanging, like the prayers whispered in the church long after the congregation has left. In the storeroom, jars of mushrooms—harvested from the cool shadows beneath ancient oaks—sit row on row, their surfaces dulled from age and handling.

Up on the moors, the wind shifts, carrying the tang of peat and the memory of winter’s chill. The shepherd’s crook leans against a stone, polished from years of pressing into the ground. Ravens settle on a fence post, chattering and waiting for the rain to ease. A distant bell tolls, its sound muffled in the mist, repeating the pattern but not the meaning. In the city, roofers patch the slates above the cathedral, balancing on beams slick from the drizzle, careful not to disturb the moss that clings between the stones.

There is a kind of comfort in repetition, but the reversal asks for more. When the form remains but the spirit has left, it helps to pause by the window and listen to the rain. One might stack books in a quiet corner, or clean the old teapot, letting the surface reflect the steady hands of all who have tended it. A walk along the boundary wall, fingertips tracing the pitted stone, can remind the heart of old stories, even if their words have shifted with the weather.
