THE HIEROPHANT ― Reversed

In the candlelit hush of old temples, the Hierophant is a figure of solemn transmission. Rites unfold with careful hands: incense rising, ancient words spoken, a sequence of gestures passed down by memory more than meaning. Upright, he holds the thread connecting today to yesterday, weaving the visible world to the invisible. But now, seated backwards in the tarot, the figure finds his hands empty, the incense merely smoke, the rituals a hollow echo of former power.

The cathedral’s stones have watched generations of seekers and clerics, their faces softened by centuries of rain and time. Underfoot, the floor is paved with slabs hewn high in the moors, quarried from cliffs where seabirds wheel in the mist. Outside, in that bleak landscape, shepherds have worn smooth the footpaths between crags, guiding flocks through drizzle that blurs the boundary between earth and sky. The trade of those who split and shape the hard ground is both ancient and necessary, yet, over years, the words that once blessed each new slab have faded, leaving only the sound of hammers and the rhythm of labor.

In the kitchen, a slate board waits at the end of the table. It once served as a surface for bread and cheese, or as a makeshift notebook where recipes and reminders were scrawled in chalk—messages written for a household that no longer listens. Now, it sits unused, its purpose lingering like a half-remembered tune. The rites of the house—passing the bread, blessing the meal—reduce to habit, while the flavor of togetherness quietly slips away.

Far away, in the weather-beaten gardens behind aging cottages, roof tiles overlap in careful repetition. They keep out the storm but also carry a kind of resignation, the knowledge that shelter can become separation. Moss grows in their seams, and the wind rattles loose slates after nights of relentless rain. Inside, a cold stove waits for the kindling of purpose, remembering the years when the fire was an anchor for the gathering of family and the telling of stories.

There is a mountain crest, far north, where the fog comes in low and lingers until midday. Travelers crossing the pass have learned the shape of the land by the crunch of stone underfoot, the chill that slips beneath collars and cuffs. The old ways said to greet the mist with a word for safe passage, yet now, the custom is mostly forgotten, and footsteps are hurried, eyes lowered, as if the meaning of the journey has become a mere commute.

When tradition feels rote and the ceremony no longer stirs the heart, one can still find the core by returning to the simple. Today, someone might write a promise or a poem on the kitchen slab, or run a fingertip along the edge of an old roof tile, remembering how it was laid by hand. They can step outside in the morning, breathing in the wet air after a night of rain, or pause mid-journey to listen to the echo of their own feet on stone, recognizing the path as wholly their own.
