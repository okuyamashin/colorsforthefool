THE HIEROPHANT ― Upright

The Hierophant sits in his temple, robed and composed, the keys gleaming in his hand. At his feet, two attendants kneel, heads bowed to the ancient ways. The stone pillars around him carry the grooves of countless years, silent witnesses to inherited rituals. Tonight, the old pattern is not just remembered, but embodied, as if the world’s instructions are encoded in the steps taken through this sacred space.

In the sanctuary’s hush, the priest’s vestments carry the shimmer of silk threads woven stiff and straight. On the altar, a pomegranate cracks open, seeds tumbling across polished oak, each one a story of discipline and promise. Incense curls upward, but the air is also salted by the memory of early autumn apples stacked in monasteries and the waxy sheen of ripe cherries, harvested at the orchard’s edge and offered in bowls beside solemn prayer books. Each of these things, ancient and ordinary, marks tradition’s continuity—a way handed down through hands and hearts.

Beyond the temple doors, the world offers its own confirmations. The tiled roofs of Kyoto’s Fushimi Inari shrine, rising in thousands of arches through forest, echo the silent march of worshippers seeking guidance. In the evening quiet of a Spanish courtyard, a clay jug sits next to a bowl of steaming paprika-laden stew, the aroma as grounding as any scriptural reading, reminding those gathered of labor and home. In a village in Anatolia, a child ties a silk thread around her wrist, a gesture taught by her grandmother to guard against misfortune, as the family’s painted rooster crows from a wooden post.

The old paths wind even into the natural world. In a monastery garden, a single rose blooms late, petals layered and perfect, while a fox passes through brambles in the dusk, fur caught in hawthorn just as stories curl into evening prayers. Even the weather bends to the inherited way—tonight, the sky glows as if warning of wind or warmth, shepherds pausing in the fields to check their nets and the burnished apples they’ve gathered before night falls.

All of this becomes the night’s procedure: the careful tying of a scarf before stepping onto a lamplit street, the slicing of a ripe tomato to lay across fresh bread, the folding of a letter sealed with wax and hope. Each act, no matter how small, is a link in a chain that stretches quietly back through time, made firm by repetition and reverence. To enter this current is to feel both guided and guiding, receiving and transmitting the ways that endure.

Tonight, you might choose to polish a copper kettle and let water come to a boil on the stove, or hold a shining apple in two hands and think of the hands before you who did the same. You could write a blessing on a slip of paper and tuck it into a book or share a bowl of cherries with a friend at dusk, speaking aloud one thing you wish to continue. Each gesture is a key, unlocking the next moment in a sequence both ancient and newly alive.
