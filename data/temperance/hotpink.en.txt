TEMPERANCE ― Reversed

The angel of Temperance stands with one foot on the earth, one in the running stream. Upright, her hands pour water from one vessel to another, calm and precise. But today, the vessel tips, and water arcs out and over, spilling past the rim, soaking into the ground. The careful exchange is lost; the river cannot be contained in crystal. On the table nearby, where she should have set down her cup, there is a single camellia, lush and almost audacious, its petals curling outward, open to the blundering wind.

In myth, the camellia has been a token of longing or promise—brought from old gardens in East Asia, it was traded across seas and settled in Victorian hothouses, where its blooms made the glass rooms blush through the bleakest months. Its petals will not close once open, cannot be pressed back into bud. The flower’s heart is always on display—a reminder that things spilled cannot be unspilled, and that some sweetness cannot be quietly hidden away.

In the city’s market, there’s a baker whose window is lined with cakes crowned by candied rose petals and glazed strawberries. Under the glass dome, a single frosted éclair stands out, its coat glossy and brash. It is bought for celebrations—birthdays, apologies, reconciliations—revealing the inescapable drama of appetite, the way we hunger for intensity when balance slips. The baker shrugs, admitting he only makes this pastry on days when the sky looks wild enough to rain sideways. When the winds pick up, sugar sticks to fingertips, and laughter leaks out on the street.

Further along, a silk scarf is knotted to the strap of a traveler’s bag, fluttering in the air. The scarf, bought in a souk in Marrakech, once drew the attention of a tea seller, his stall crowded with glass pots and painted tins. He said women wore such scarves in festival season, when drums called people into the night and petals showered the dancers. The fabric, unruly, could never be pressed flat for long, and its pattern told stories of risk—of stepping out, of claiming space.

Long ago, a kind of weather swept across the plains of Montana: thunderheads roared, and in a freak storm, hailstones crashed down, denting roofs and flattening wheat. When the sky cleared, children rushed outside, finding the fields littered with the battered stalks and, inexplicably, the petals of peonies from a nearby wedding caught on the wind, plastered to doorframes and car windshields. The next day, the townspeople gathered to clean up, but some let the petals dry where they fell, seeing in the disorder a strange kind of beauty—a story of what happens when the usual order collapses and something bright takes its place.

Under the sign of Temperance reversed, it helps to set out a camellia bloom at your table, letting its presence remind you that imperfection is its own form of grace. You might eat a pastry chosen for its brashness rather than its balance, tracing sweetness with your tongue and not worrying about the mess. Tie a scarf, unruly and bold, to your wrist or bag, letting it move as you walk, a signal of openness to the unexpected. And if a storm shakes your town, step outside when it’s safe, find the remnants—the petals, the shimmer—and let yourself marvel.
