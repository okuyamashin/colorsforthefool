TEMPERANCE ― Reversed

The angel’s cup tips, water streaking down the rocks, soaking into the thirsty earth before it can be shared. What was meant to be blended leaks away, leaving a patch of ground unevenly damp, a warning whispered through the grass. The garden recalls another time when balance failed: when ancient merchants, having journeyed from the distant hills of Persia, cracked open the shells of small, hard beetles to release their treasure. The secret they carried across the deserts was just as easily spilled—a crushed harvest that stained their fingers and seeped into wool, into parchment, into the hems of robes waiting to be dipped.

Every marketplace in the old Mediterranean once smelled faintly of this—sharp, metallic, alive with stories. In Constantinople, women knelt in quiet courtyards, stirring pots of boiling water over coals, lifting skeins of silk into the steaming bath that would fix the dye. Hands grew stained, nails edged with the mark of their labor. The color was commerce, power, and sometimes ruin: a merchant’s fortune won or lost on the safe passage of a single shipment. In the long corridors of palaces, the same shade bled into the velvet drapes and pooled beneath the feet of kings, but it also seeped outside, into the streets, running in little rivulets with the day’s rain.

The clouds outside grow heavy, dark with the sign of a storm that will not settle gently. The wind carries the promise of a sky split open, and with it, the memory of Spanish fields planted with a new harvest. There, pomegranates burst against the stone, seeds scattering with every careless step. Fruit once reserved for temples and treaties now left to ferment beneath the trees, their juice running into the soil. The orchard becomes a map of what is lost before it can be preserved. Travelers tread carelessly, shoes sticky with sweetness, and the land itself seems to lean toward imbalance, tipping from plenty to waste.

Under the kitchen roof, a small knife glimmers, its blade stained by the work of slicing late-summer berries—currants and cherries, boiled with sugar, meant for a cordial that now runs too thin. Someone wipes their hands on a cloth, the stain refusing to fade. In attics and cellars, old tapestries tell the story of this restless shade: threads pulled from the past, woven, unraveled, and woven again, never quite matching, always searching for harmony lost at the first careless spill.

Today, let your hands seek the quiet weight of old wool, holding it as you remember the people who colored it before you. Pour the last of a bottle of sharp fruit wine, letting it splash onto the stone as an offering for things unblended and undone. Score the tough skin of a pomegranate, letting the juice run wild, uncontained. Tie a ribbon, newly stained, to your wrist, and feel the connection to every hand that has carried such a mark.
