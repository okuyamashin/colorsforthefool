TEMPERANCE ― Upright

A quiet figure stands at the edge of a shallow pond, one foot touching water, one foot pressing gently into cool earth. In her hands, two chalices tilt fluid from one to the other, a careful stream. Above, morning air carries the scent of thaw and a faint sparkle of dew, as though a cloud had only just wept onto the grass. The stream she pours does not rush or splash, but finds peace in the slow mingling. Beyond, a narrow footpath winds through reeds and wild mint, toward a distant ridge dusted by the sun’s first light.

In the stories of old, there are stones in the Egyptian desert, chipped and polished by hands who knew the waiting patience of water over years. From the mines at Nishapur, traders found river-worn pebbles the same shade as mountain lakes at dawn, and carried them in camel trains across Silk Road passes. In the temples of Mesoamerica, carvers shaped the same into beads pressed to the lips of the dead, a promise of safe passage. Each trade route, each quiet workshop, became a place where elements were coaxed into harmony: copper drawn from earth, limestone dust, a human breath fanned over embers, smoke rising in a thin spiral. The result was neither metal nor stone, but a suggestion of both: hard, yet gentle, as if the sky had lent a fragment to the hand.

Inside the farmhouse, dawn’s light slips through a windowpane and glimmers on a porcelain bowl, its surface glazed with the same watery sheen. The bowl rests alongside a pitcher, both waiting their turn at the washstand. Nearby, a soft shawl, woven from silk and flax, lies folded on a chest. Its threads recall the mineral-rich Mediterranean shallows, where divers once gathered fragments for trade, and weavers learned to blend dyes with the patience of a monastic scribe copying a text. Hanging above the hearth, a single feather of a kingfisher is pinned to a wooden beam—a traveler’s token, brought home after fishing beside a glacial stream, far upstream from where the river meets the plain.

Temperance is the work of mixing. The weather outside is kind—a sky brushed clear after a night of gentle rain, the edges of the air neither dry nor heavy, balanced just so. Along the stony path, wild forget-me-nots and sagebrush press against one another, their roots tangled beneath the surface, each drawing what it needs from the same patch of earth. By the pond, dragonflies hover, their wings trembling with light. The ancient alchemists named their art after the patient cycling of water: dissolve, blend, distill, reunite. This is the daily work of the world, as it is for the spirit.

Today, a person can find a moment to fill a cup with cold spring water and pour it, slowly, into another, watching the smooth stream. They might polish a piece of old jewelry, tracing its smooth stone with the pad of a finger, remembering the hands that shaped it. Walking outside, they could pause beside a puddle left by last night’s rain and see the sky mirrored in the surface, clouds drifting between their toes. Or, at home, they may wrap themselves in a scarf and breathe in the peace of gentle, flowing air—neither too warm, nor too chill—before setting out to meet the day.
