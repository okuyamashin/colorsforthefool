TEMPERANCE ― Reversed

The angel’s jug slips and water pours out, darkening the dust at her feet. Where once there was patience and slow blending, now the liquid finds its own way, carving a path beside the river rather than into it. Control loosens and seeps away. On the table, the pitcher is set too close to the edge. The kitchen towel is damp, stained, forgotten. Such scenes mark the moment when diligence falters and what was meant for careful mixing runs free.

Southwards, in fields reclaimed from wild grass and stone, vines once held their bounty in quiet balance—sun and rain measured, roots tested by heat. Yet there are afternoons when the sky’s weight breaks, releasing bursts of rain that strip the soil, sending clusters tumbling before their season. Wind shakes the leaves, and the ground grows patchy with fallen fruit. In the market baskets, some are bruised, split open, seeds sliding against the wood. Farmers sort quickly, saving what can be carried, knowing that too much haste or too little vigilance can ruin the crop.

Long ago, travelers carried seeds across the ocean packed in pouches, nestled among cloves, beans, and small tokens of home. They brought them not knowing what would grow where the soil was unknown and the river's path unfamiliar. In new villages, these seeds found their own luck, sprawling into kitchen gardens, slipping beyond fences, rooting where they pleased. There is always the risk of overgrowth—too much, too soon, or in places unprepared. Recipes from grandmothers’ tables sometimes call for what is not yet ripe or what bursts open in the pot, flavor escaping before it can be caught.

In southern towns, where afternoon storms gather with little warning, rain slaps tin rooftops and leaks through seams. Windows are propped open, and the scent of earth mingles with cut stems. Stains gather under window boxes, drips tracing crooked lines along old bricks. On such days, children run barefoot to gather what the garden has dropped, their hands sticky and their shirts marked. The kitchen fills with the sound of chopping, the peelings and seeds swept away, as someone tries to salvage enough for tonight’s stew.

In the quiet of evening, a wooden spoon is left dangling from a pot, sauce thickening along the rim. A cloth, used to lift the pot, is heaped onto the counter, marked with streaks from earlier spills. A jar sits open, the lid forgotten, splatters on the counter. The meal is almost ready but not quite what was intended—seasoning tipping too far, sweetness overtaking the broth. Someone will taste it, frown, and reach for a pinch of salt to bring it back toward balance.

To gather what was dropped, one might step into the garden after a storm and collect what remains on the ground. To reclaim a sense of order, wipe the counter clean and put the lid back on what was left open. Brew a simple sauce from what is overripe, letting nothing go to waste. Offer the softest fruit to a neighbor, letting generosity soften where care once slipped.
