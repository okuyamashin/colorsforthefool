THE WORLD ― Reversed

The four figures on the card, each holding their corner, watch as the circle’s garland slips loose at one side. Laurel leaves, once woven tight, fall from the crown, and the dancer’s hand rises from the binding knot before it is tied. It is a moment of incompletion, of thresholds not quite crossed. The journey stands almost finished; the road, well-trodden, halts at a rough border of undergrowth and stone.

On the hills outside Messina, the harvest lingers on the trees. The air is thick with the scent of crushed fruit and the hum of insects. Here, olive groves grow in tangled rows, their gnarled trunks and silvery foliage shaped by centuries of sun and wind. The baskets lean against the earth, half-filled and waiting for hands to return, the nets caught in the roots where they were dropped. Trade routes once wound between these orchards and the ports, the promise of oil and wood and bitter fruit just out of reach. Under the branches, an old canvas field jacket is tossed across a fallen limb, left behind for the mid-day meal, its pockets heavy with dust and half-forgotten tools.

To the north, the hedge rows of Picardy mark the lines of trenches, their shrubs and wild grasses grown over with years of rain. Helmets rest in the shade, out of sight from the road, and the boots of the quartermaster leave a trail along the edge of the ploughed field. The cloth of uniforms carries the memory of the countryside, stained by the press of roots and turf, never quite scrubbed clean. There is a kind of waiting here, too: a letter half-written, a letter never sent, a loaf of bread set aside for a tomorrow that may or may not come.

Beyond the hills and fields, the kitchen waits in the hush of afternoon. On the table, a battered tin can, label faded, sits beside the mortar and pestle. The olives inside are rough and briny, slick with the memory of rain and salt. Someone uses the wooden handle to crush a handful of fruit, working slowly, the sound muffled by thick window glass. Outside, the wind rises and the garden gate bumps against the wall, stopped by the weight of the spade left wedged in the mud. A soldier’s satchel, stitched and mended, leans near the door, still smelling faintly of pressed leaves and waxed canvas. The coat rack holds a scarf, faded by many washings, stained by oil and years of rain.

On the edge of the garden, the weather shifts. A fog settles near the hedgerows, clinging to the low ground. The sky presses close, and the air is heavy with the promise of rain that waits just beyond the horizon. In this pause, the world hangs between completion and return, just as the hands on the card hesitate above the circle’s last knot.

Someone can press a sprig of wild herb between the pages of a book before leaving the house. Another might mend the elbow of an old jacket by the kitchen lamp, sewing the patch with patient stitches. A third can wash olives in cool water, preparing them for the evening table, or wipe the mud from their boots before stepping into the quiet of home.
