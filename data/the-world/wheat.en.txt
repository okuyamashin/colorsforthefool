THE WORLD ― Upright

A circle of leaves frames the dancer in mid-step, poised above the meeting point of four living creatures. Lion, bull, man, and eagle mark the corners, their presence anchoring a space that feels both like a threshold and a harvest. The cord that ties the wreath is not merely decorative, but the closing of a journey: a full circuit made visible, a season’s work complete. The dance is not hurried. Footfalls touch a path worn smooth, a field after the last stalk is cut.

Over far and open plains, the plant that shares its name with the dancer’s circuit grows tall and straight by midsummer. Wind moves through it in waves, and the heads of grain nod to the sky and to the earth. Its cycles mark the turning of the year, the effort of sowing and the patience of waiting. In legend and song, gods and mortals have gathered in such fields when the old year was folded away and the new was waiting to be kneaded.

When the time comes, people step forward in twos and threes, sickles in hand. They bend to collect what they have tended. The bundle, once cut, is banded tightly, a sheaf that will be carried home. The dust and chaff catch the sun, and for a moment, the air shimmers with all that has been gathered: not just food, but the promise of nourishment, the making of bread, the sharing of a meal. In some villages, a wreath woven from the last cut stalks is hung above the doorway, a sign that the cycle is unbroken, that the house is fed and the store is full.

Trade caravans carry sacks of this grain along ancient roads, moving from one river valley to another, tying distant places together. In the marketplace, the grain becomes loaves, biscuits, or the fine flour for a festival cake. In kitchens, it is sifted, kneaded, shaped; a table, worn with generations of use, is dusted and ready for dough to rest. Even in the quietest rooms, the sweep of a broom carries away the stray husks, the leftovers of the season’s passing.

In some places, the end of the harvest is met with a hush. A still afternoon, heavy with warmth and the scent of cut stems, settles on the village. The year’s turning is marked by silence and gratitude, a pause before the bread is broken. The weather holds its breath as the old stories say: there is a moment when the circuit is joined and all is complete.

Today, you might braid a strand of dry grass or ribbon and hang it above your threshold, or shape dough with intention, remembering the hands before yours. You can sweep your kitchen floor with care, gathering what remains into a small bundle, or write a word of thanks and tuck it beneath a loaf before it bakes. Each act ties another knot in the wreath, closing a circuit and opening another.
