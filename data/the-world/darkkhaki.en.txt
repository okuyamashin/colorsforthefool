THE WORLD ― Reversed

The laurel wreath arches across the sky, still incomplete, its ends drifting in the air, uncertain whether they will ever tie. A dancer pauses on the borderland, just shy of stepping through the open gate. The four corners hold their breath, watching her hesitate, the promise of the world just beyond a last undone knot. In this moment, the closure of a season, a voyage, or a hope remains stubbornly unfinished. This is the ground before harvest, the hour before the dawn spills over, when the world’s body pulls itself together but the final thread slips from your grasp.

Somewhere, a soldier’s jacket hangs on a nail in a hallway, its fabric faded and sturdy, carrying with it the smell of last year’s fields. It has seen parades and dust, satchel and sweat, always prepared but never quite at rest. From the same cloth, a gardener’s trousers are patched at the knees, streaked with the memory of grasses pressed flat by the weight of labor. In a highland country, shepherds pull on these trousers at dawn as wind rocks the grasses and the sheep call out, waiting to be led. The clothes endure the brushing of thistles and the lean of weather, holding steady against endings that will not yet come.

At the edge of an old empire, the wind carries the scent of wild sage and the memory of campaigns run long past. Merchants bring in bundles of grain, each stalk trimmed and bundled, their husks tough and persistent, a promise almost kept. In the north, the same stalks are ground into flour to make flatbread, wrapped in cloth and stacked for travelers who may never reach the market before nightfall. Nomads sling their packs, woven tightly from reeds and tough grasses, across the backs of donkeys grown wise with unfinished journeys. Here, the journey circles back on itself, just before the walls of a city where triumph would be celebrated with laurel but the gates remain ajar.

Morning fog lifts off the steppe, reluctant to dissolve. It lays itself quietly along the low fields where grains mature, stubbornly clinging to their stalks. In these plains, the earth is packed with stories of unfinished business—crops a day from harvest, treaties a signature away from peace, roads mapped but not yet cleared of stone. The weather, mild and persistent, refuses to break into true rain, holding its breath across days of indecision. In kitchens, mothers smooth tablecloths woven from tough, unbleached fibers, their hands moving over patterns meant to gather families that have not yet returned home.

If you wish to extend the invitation for closure, slip into a canvas jacket before stepping outdoors, or walk among wild sage and tall grasses, letting their roughness remind you of what remains undone. Kneel in the garden, tending to roots that need one last touch, or grind grain for bread, even if you eat it alone. Write a letter promising your arrival, even if you are not sure when the journey will end.
