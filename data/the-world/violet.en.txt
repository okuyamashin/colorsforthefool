THE WORLD ― Upright

At the center of the card, a figure dances within an oval garland, a living border woven of bay and laurel. Around her, four creatures watch: a lion, a bull, an eagle, a human face, each peering out from the corners. The wreath marks an ending and an arrival, an archway of leaves that is both gate and crown. The dancer’s ribbons curve in her hands, trailing like streamers in a festival that celebrates the closing of a long circuit.

As the world turns, the festival echoes in distant valleys and sunlit kitchens, in fields remembered more for their scent than for the harvest. In one such place, the air is marked by the presence of a particular flower, blooming on the threshold between spring and full summer. Its petals, modest but unwavering, cluster close to the ground, sometimes hidden among the grass. They are gathered in small bunches to be set in glass cups beside windows, filling quiet rooms with a fragrance that is sweet and shy, like the memory of a promise kept.

Legends tell of these blossoms brought from distant mountains, carried in the hems of cloaks by those who crossed rivers and rocky passes. They clung to the fabric, seeds wrapped in folds, and took root wherever the traveler rested. In ancient markets, bundles of these flowers exchanged hands not as currency, but as tokens of a journey’s safe return. In the dim corners of monasteries, their essence found its way into tinctures and balms, believed to close wounds and settle hearts that had wandered too far from home.

Storms sometimes pass over the hills where these blooms lie low, but after the rain, the air grows gentle and soft. The petals glisten, their scent rising as the sky clears. In some villages, old women still set bowls of these blossoms outside their doors to greet the dawn, believing that what has been lost will find its way back when the sun touches the petals. The living creatures of the card—the lion, the bull, the eagle, the human—are echoed in the weather that moves over the hills, strong and patient, wild and wise, all part of the turning that brings the flowers each year.

In the kitchen, someone stirs a handful of these petals into a clear syrup, swirling the spoon until the sweetness dissolves. A child is handed the first taste, and the flavor lingers, floral and elusive, on the tongue. The table is set with a cloth that bears the same flowers stitched along its edge, each one a tiny, careful knot. At the threshold, a ribbon tied in a bow marks a finished story and a new invitation, echoing the dancer’s own winding path.

Today, you might pick a small bouquet of these blossoms and place them where morning light falls. You could stir their petals into a cup of water, sipping slowly to remember the taste of homecoming. Try tying a slender ribbon around your wrist or across the doorway, as a sign that every circuit closed becomes the start of something new. Before sleep, press a dried bloom between the pages of a book you love, knowing it will be waiting for you at the next turning.
