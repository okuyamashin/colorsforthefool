THE WORLD ― Reversed

On the card, the dancer’s circular path circles but does not close. The laurel wreath, open at the top, waits for the last knot, the single pull that would bind all things into unity. Yet the hand lets go. The ribbon sags, and unfinished patterns echo far beyond the world’s imagined edge. The four watchers, lion and bull, eagle and angel, pause in their corners, their patience stretched across cloud-thick air. This is the borderland between completion and what comes next, a crossing that halts midstride.

Here, in the shadow of endings, the landscape stretches under a sky that holds its rain. The air is heavy with the scent of wet slate, the kind of day when mist clings low over a town’s stone lanes. The rooftops, soaked and rough, mark the pause between storm and sun. In these streets, lamplight glows faint behind windowpanes, softened by weather that never quite clears. Somewhere, a river slides past embankments lined with cobbles, the water at its lowest ebb, reflecting nothing but cloud.

History lives in objects meant to last. In a blacksmith’s shop away from the city’s heart, anvils and hammers rest on benches, their surfaces worn smooth by work and waiting. The hearth is cold, and no heat rises, but the tools remember every strike, every unfinished project left to gather the dust of years. Near the doorway, a rack holds old spades and hoes, their blades dulled by earth and time, the handles polished by hands now gone.

In the fields outside, the season lingers. Rows of olive trees stand beneath the overcast, their trunks twisted, leaves shivering in the unmoving air. In ancient groves, people have always planted for generations to come, never for the harvest at hand. The olives lie on the ground, neither fully ripe nor fallen, caught in the balance between growth and decay. Moss creeps along stone walls, tracing lines that once marked boundaries, now lost to memory.

At home, the hearth is swept, but the fire is not yet lit. On the table, a cast-iron pot waits, seasoned by decades of meals, its surface dulled from thousands of slow-cooked soups. In the kitchen drawer, a tin spoon lies beside a box of matches, both held in readiness, neither called to action. The bread on the cutting board is yesterday’s, dense and chewy, the crust softening as hours pass.

Outside, the weather holds its breath. The morning opens with a fine, persistent drizzle, making the world smell of old stone and wet bark. Along the footpath, puddles gather in ruts left by carts, their surfaces trembling as droplets fall. The air shivers, uncertain if it will warm or chill. Travelers hesitate beneath slate-colored clouds, uncertain if the journey will resume or turn back.

Today, a person could walk the old lanes after the rain, listening for the echo of distant steps. They might polish a family heirloom—a handle, a blade, a worn coin—remembering the hands that shaped it. They could stir a pot slowly, letting the aroma rise in the quiet kitchen. Or, sitting by the window, they might write a letter not to send, the words circling just before the end.
