THE EMPEROR ― Reversed

Once, there was an old chair at the head of the table. Its arms were wide, carved with the likeness of lions rearing on their hind legs, but the seat now stood empty beneath the high window. Dust gathered in the hollows where a ruler’s hands had rested, and the back creaked in the morning breeze. The grand room—stone-floored, iron-latched—holds only the echo of footsteps. All around, order persists: maps nailed to the wall, the brass scales unmoved, the day’s ledger open but unwritten. Yet nothing breathes authority into the artifacts. The customs remain, repeated for their own sake. Here, the ceremony is hollow, and the form is the law.

Beyond these walls, a basalt mountain rises. It keeps its secrets under a shroud of mist. Centuries ago, travelers carved their names into the cooled lava, hoping to be remembered as part of something enduring. Now, the path to the summit is slippery, worn by countless boots. The mountain stands, silent and indifferent, a monument to structure without heart. Somewhere along the slope, a crow lands on a bare branch, its wings folding tight. No song escapes from its beak; it watches with a glassy, ancient eye. The crow is not here for kings, only for the pattern of morning and night.

In a market square, a coal merchant weighs lumps before a small crowd. Each piece is blackened, lusterless, pressed into hard angles. The trade is honest, but routine: measure, hand over, sweep the dust. There is no warmth in the transaction, just the cold reliability of process. Later, a seamstress returns home, unrolling a bolt of jet cloth by the window. She cuts shapes for a formal jacket, stitches precise lines. Her hands are steady, her mind elsewhere. The jacket will hang in a wardrobe, reserved for occasions that demand respect for the rules, not for the person within.

Somewhere, a thunderstorm churns above the river. The sky closes in, thick and churning, the world below muted and waiting. The pattern is ancient—a gathering, a release, then calm. The river swells, its surface reflecting no detail, only the dim outline of the bridge. A fisherman, sheltering beneath the arch, holds his rod but does not expect a catch. He is there because the time is right, because long habit has taught him to endure the waiting, even if the prize never comes.

To mark this day’s movement, a person might sit in the old chair and write a letter never meant to be sent, letting the mind rest in ritual. One could walk the path up the mountain, tracing the names carved in stone, and leave a pebble as a silent offering. In the evening, folding and storing a formal jacket could remind one to honor forms, even when meaning seems lost. Or listen to the thunder, counting the seconds between flash and rumble, measuring time with patience.
