THE EMPEROR ― Reversed

A throne without its ruler is only a chair, high-backed and imposing, but hollow at its core. The banners hang above it, still and silent, their threads woven tight as the laws that once rang out from this empty place. Where a voice of command used to echo, only formality lingers, repeating itself quietly in the dust. The scepter leans against the armrest, cold, ornamental, weighty in its ceremonial iron. All authority has slipped out unseen, and what remains is the strict frame of order—routines for their own sake, gesture without guidance.

Far from the palace, in the evening fields, the last clusters of sumac berries cling to leafless branches, their skins puckered and dark after the first hard frost. These berries once brightened the late autumn table, but now they signal only the tireless grind of a season closing in. At the market, slabs of venison hang from hooks, each piece preserved and salted, the flesh darkening at the edges. No feast is prepared, no story told: the process continues because it always has, not because anyone waits to be fed. The butcher’s cleaver falls onto the block in the same rhythm as always, as if keeping an old oath no one reads anymore.

On a distant plain, a caravan moves slowly beneath a sky swollen with storm. The wind carries the metallic tang of iron-rich clay. In years gone by, commanders would have gathered their troops here, banners flying, decisions made at a single word. Now, the field is quiet except for the clatter of wagon wheels and the patient trudge of oxen. The earth itself, heavy and compacted, seems to remember the press of boots and hooves, though no one leads the march. Rain falls in solid sheets, pounding into puddles that stain the land deep and dark, relentless as law with no heart behind it.

Inside, the hearth is cold. On the mantel sits an empty goblet, once used to toast victories and returns, its glass thick as if it must hold something stronger than wine. Cloth napkins lie folded and untouched, each corner pressed sharp. The family ledger rests open on the table, columns tallied and debts recorded with careful hand. No one reads aloud from its pages tonight. The candles burn down low, their smoke curling upwards, marking time without celebration.

If you wish to take what remains and give it warmth, walk to the kitchen and stew wild berries in a heavy pot, letting their rich aroma fill the air. Sharpen the household tools with steady hands, keeping them ready in case they are needed once more for more than repetition. Line the soles of your boots with sturdy hide, then step outside after the rain to leave your mark in the softened earth. Write a letter by lamplight to someone far away, and tell them what you remember of the days when the seat was not empty.
