THE EMPEROR ― Reversed

The throne stands in the middle of the hall, its shape unyielding, carved and polished, untouched by the warmth of a ruler’s hand. The banners have not fallen, but they ripple in the draft, showing the deep marks where sunlight has faded their edges. A scepter, once a sign of command, leans on the armrest, gathering dust, its surface worn smooth by old decisions and the insistence of time. This is a room held together by custom, the rituals of governance proceeding on empty echoes. The Emperor’s place is empty, but the desk is stacked with ledgers, the floor swept clean, the footsteps measured and known.

Outside, the city’s rooftops stretch in low uniform rows, their shingles dulled by decades of rain and the patient settling of soot from distant industry. The stone-paved road leads straight from those silent chambers to the open square, where iron railings curl into patterns, their sharpness blunted by countless seasons. In the early morning, mist hovers over the fields just beyond the last outbuildings, too thin to hide the furrows, but enough to smudge the horizon, softening the border between earth and sky. On days when the rain falls in fine, steady lines, slate tiles run slick, gutters chattering under the steady pressure, water seeking its place in the world’s slow cycle.

In the workshop, the anvil sits at its center, massive and enduring. It waits for the return of hands, the rhythm of work. Smudges from old fires remain on the apron’s surface and on the shaft of a hammer left to rest. The bellows, once forced by muscle, now move only with the wind that slips through the loose frame of the door. There is no clang, no spark, only the memory of creating order from chaos, of shaping things to a fixed rule. Even the ledger, propped open on a low shelf, records nothing now but inventory, columns of tools and their last repairs.

In the garden behind the house, flagstones press into the moss, each stone a record of passage, the boundary between cultivated rosebushes and the patch where thistles grow untamed. Here, beneath the hedge, a crow’s feather lies, left after a quarrel overhead. It is the color of old iron, of the stones that ring the firepit. The branch of the yew tree, twisted and heavy with age, marks the edge of the property. It has seen many winters, its bark scored by the claws of animals seeking shelter.

The landscape never abandons its purpose, but it no longer answers to vision. The mountains beyond the village lodge their shadows against the fields, their outlines harsh but faded in the late afternoon. On the highest ridge, fences stand upright though the paint is long gone, boards held together by habit and the memory of boundaries.

A person can polish the brass handle of a desk drawer in a quiet room, letting the motion become its own end. They may line up their tools or books in perfect order, not to use them, but to keep the pattern intact. They can sweep the porch at dawn, following the same pattern of strokes each morning, or sit quietly beside an empty chair, honoring the shape of absence.
