THE EMPEROR ― Reversed

On the throne stands the frame of authority, but no one sits within it. The great furrowed fields surrounding the old palace lie fallow, their ancient boundaries still marked by rows of crushed brick, the remains of a city that ruled by plan and line. The banners above the gate have faded, mere fabric now, no longer bearing a crest. The air carries the dry taste of iron; the parade ground holds its shape, but boots have not crossed it in decades. In this domain, the sounds of command have been replaced by echo and wind. The seat is empty, but the ceremony continues, each movement now hollowed out—a show for no one, yet still performed.

Beyond the fortress walls, the dust kicks up from the road that once bore traders and soldiers; now, only the wind carves the deep grooves left behind by wagon wheels. The earth here is hard, crusted over with the clay once used to build walls and pots, brittle shards scattered among the weeds. In the courtyard, a single pomegranate tree remains, its fruit splitting open on the stones below, seeds spilling and drying in the open air. These are the remnants of order, a ritual of fruit falling out of season, watched only by circling crows. The structure stands, enduring, but warmth and purpose have wandered elsewhere.

Down in the kitchens, the clay oven is cold. On the counter rest dried chili peppers, wrinkled and potent, strung together in memory of spice and feast. Their presence is a relic—a reminder that once, hands moved with purpose to create, to nourish. Now, the peppers hang untouched, and the mortar and pestle sit on the shelf, gathering a thin layer of dust but still sharp-edged, ready for a pressure that no one applies. The ancient fabric that drapes the table, hand-loomed in distant villages and dyed with root and leaf, has lost its luster but remains carefully folded, a habit of tidiness persisting after meaning has faded.

Outside, the afternoon brings the taste of coming rain. The monsoon clouds gather above the empty watchtower, promising a downpour that has not yet arrived. All is anticipation, but the ritual of preparation outlives the need; the cistern is cleaned, though no water comes, and the gutters are swept for a flood that will not fill them. The scent in the air is of waiting, of clay and stone warming before the storm.

In such a landscape, it is possible to enact small acts that echo what once mattered. A person might take a handful of crushed brick and mark out a new boundary in the garden, tracing lines for seeds not yet planted. They can gather the split pomegranate and, with care, press the juice into a bowl for a quiet meal. The dried peppers can be ground by hand, their heat awakened once more in a simple stew. One could rinse the old cloth in the evening, letting it dry in the breeze, a gesture of remembering the way things were when every movement had a reason.
