THE EMPEROR ― Upright

Stone is quiet, unyielding, its face shaped by thousands of dawns. The Emperor sits on a seat carved from the mountain’s bones, beneath a high, unmoving sky. Around him, a border wall, mortared by careful hands, tames the restless earth. Each block is cut square, each joint set with method. This is the order of the world: a king who does not wander, but who claims his place and makes it last. In the foreground, a thick rug is spread, heavy with foreign patterns, brought by traders from distant empires. Its threads are knotted with the labor of disciplined fingers, its surface patterned to speak of the lands where rivers run dry beneath a hard sun.

Across that rug, a cup of spiced wine is set carefully on a low table. The wine is from the autumn harvest, aged in deep cellars, poured for the master of the house in a vessel of hammered metal. It fills the air with the scent of earth and fruit, the promise of order carried through the cycles of year after year. Beside it, a bowl of pomegranate seeds gleams, each aril a small, shining boundary holding its juice within, as if they too have learned to keep to their proper limits. The Emperor eats with measured gestures, each seed bitten and then set aside, the discipline of his table reflecting the discipline of his walls.

Beyond the court, a field of madder roots is tended by workers who keep to their rows, weeding and watering by the old rule. These roots, pulled from the earth, are boiled in great iron pots in the dyer’s quarter. Their scent floats through the lower streets and out across the moat, where it mingles with the clean smell of mountain air. The cloth that emerges from the dyer’s hands is prized across kingdoms, sought by knights for their banners and by priests for their altar veils. Once, the secret of this dye was guarded behind locked doors; now it is a mark of power and stability, a trade that binds cities, a thread that runs through the fabric of law and custom.

In the shadows of the city’s gate, a merchant unrolls silks from the Eastern road. Among them, a bolt the color of dried blood is bartered for coin and salt. It will be cut and sewn into robes for the council, worn on feast days and when treaties are signed. In the Emperor’s hall, these robes hang ready in cedar chests, their folds a silent testament to collected authority. Even under storm clouds, when the wind scours the parapet and rain streaks the cold stone, these robes are brought out. They are donned for rites of passage, to mark the end of disputes, to seal the promise that order will hold.

Anyone who wishes to walk in the Emperor’s stead can lay a patterned cloth across their table and set down a bowl of pomegranate seeds. They might write their day’s intentions with a pen dipped in strong wine, or steep madder root for its old, earthy strength. When disputes arise, they can recite the rules that have always shaped their household, and know that each repetition is a border that keeps the road.
