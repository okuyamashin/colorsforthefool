THE EMPEROR ― Reversed

The throne stands vacant in the council chamber, its arms carved with old authority, but there is no ruler to anchor the room. The banners that once hung heavy on the far wall have gathered dust and silence; their sigils are indistinct, lost without the presence of command. The door remains open, and a draft moves over the stone flagstones, stirring the traces of parliament that linger only as routine. In the emptiness, the weight of each rule becomes disproportionate, and the hammer strikes of process echo where will and vision have faded.

Beyond the walls, on the edge of ancient quarries, men once cut and stacked flat rocks, layering them into fences and cottages. Those stones—hard, thin, and endlessly patient—hold together through structure alone, each one placed according to a measured plan. There is no mortar left; only the interlocking keeps the walls upright. When the supervisor is absent, the masons follow the outlined instructions, setting each slab by rote, until the meaning of their labor is as cold as the rock in their hands. It endures because it must, but the heart is gone.

In a highland village, the rooftops are covered with thin slabs split from the earth, fitted with care to keep out rain and wind. When storms roll through and the sky turns to low cloud, those roofs shelter the households from a chill that seeps between the stones. The mountain air brings a persistent drizzle, quietly persistent, neither fierce nor warm, just enough to remind the people that the season has lost its center. Paths across the moor become slick, and the boundary between ground and sky blurs into a single monotone, a weather of indecision and patience.

In the kitchen, a tray of oysters rests on the counter, each shell fluted and rough, holding inside a pale firmness. They were harvested at dawn by hands that follow the tides, repeating the same gestures as their fathers and grandfathers, though the market asks only for volume, not pride. The knife pries open the stubborn hinge, the flesh offered not as a delicacy, but as obligation—a harvest to be counted, not savored. Elsewhere in the house, a woolen blanket is thrown over the back of a chair. It was woven for warmth, its pattern repeated from memory until the maker thought only of completion.

On the coast, wind-bent shrubs grow where nothing else will root. Their branches are wiry, their leaves small and silent, shaped by years of unyielding gales and salt air. They do not bloom for show, nor shelter songbirds; they persist because the procedure of survival is all that remains to them. In this landscape, form becomes function, and the purpose of each thing is simply to continue.

Today, if you wish to move with the world as it is, you might walk behind an old stone wall and trace your fingers along its even, time-smoothed edge, feeling what endures when intention has passed. You might open a shell and taste its brine, letting procedure give way to sensation. You can wrap yourself in a woolen cover whose weight is familiar, or stand outside in a light mist, letting its persistence settle on your skin.
