JUDGEMENT ― Upright

A horn lifts above the clouds, clear and insistent. Coffin-lids shift, and people rise, blinking into a new morning. The call has already sounded. It is the moment of return, of realization, when every sound and shape directs attention upward, outward, beyond the hush of earthbound rest. There are no shadows here—only the wide, unfiltered daylight of awakening, the brave step forward taken at the border between quiet sleep and the wind-washed world.

The place where this card lives might be found far above the fields, where nothing blocks the sun’s gaze. Think of the ceilingless dome above the great steppes of Central Asia, the high Andean plateaus, or the endless grasslands of Mongolia. Here, the air is crisp, carrying a chill even at noon, and the sky offers a promise vast enough to swallow hesitation. Nomads in these places tell stories of a tent flap opened at dawn, the first animal tracks visible in the frost, and the curve of a herder’s staff pointing toward the horizon. The ancestors, it is said, listen most closely when the world is silent except for the wind’s steady breath.

This horn above the clouds was forged from myth, its sound recalling the trumpets said to signal the end and the beginning. In Jewish tradition, the shofar—ram’s horn—blows on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, calling souls to reflection and return. In Norse legend, Heimdall’s Gjallarhorn will sound at Ragnarök. These horns ring out across the open expanse, bridging human time and sacred time, sometimes catching the attention of shorebirds and shepherds alike. The sound carries farthest on mornings when the weather is clear and the air is thin, as if the notes could travel from one edge of the world to the other, above the lapping of waves or the hush of altiplano grass.

Certain places become thresholds under this sign. Think of forgotten piers where fishermen untangle their nets just as the first light hits the surf, or lighthouses that catch the sun’s full gaze while most of the coast lies in shadow. In the South Pacific, navigators once watched the vault above for signposts—the path of clouds, the sweep of seabirds, the first glint of morning on distant currents. High above, the weather changes quickly. Pilots know that after a storm, there is a clarity so pure that distant peaks seem close enough to touch, the air so transparent that even the smallest aircraft appears etched against the endless depth.

In the home, echoes of that horn’s call might rest in small things. An enamel basin set beneath a window reflects the morning sky as you rinse apples for breakfast. The glass face of a wristwatch catches the light as you open the front door, measuring minutes that feel suddenly important. A silk scarf, long folded in a drawer, unfurls across your shoulders as you step outside for an errand, its surface cool and smooth, recalling journeys not yet taken. Even a well-worn barometer, set near the kitchen window, points to the fair weather that invites you to air out old linens and sweep the stoop, letting the world inside.

Today, you might fill a kettle and set it on the stove just as day breaks, watching the steam curl upward into the window’s morning pane. You could jot down a thought or intention, pinning it to your mirror as a silent promise to answer what calls you. Find a moment to step outdoors and face the open sky, pausing to breathe in the clarity that follows rain. Or lift the lid of a trunk or box you have not opened in a long time, letting its contents catch the daylight and remind you of past awakenings.
