JUDGEMENT ― Reversed

On the card, figures rise from their caskets, arms outstretched, but the trumpeting angel’s call is muffled by hesitation. At the hinge of great change, someone lingers, one foot inside, one out, unwilling yet to answer. The world is waiting, a breath held in the hush between storm and dawn.

In the old capital, morning fog drapes over rooftops, settling on rain gutters and cast-iron fences. It cloaks the rooftops of ancient inns, where the weight of untold stories presses down on the slates. Along the avenue, stone cutters move about their work, chipping at markers in the cemetery, their hands practiced and patient. The marks they carve are tidy, each letter a quiet testament, awaiting eyes that have not yet decided to visit.

To the north, the wind moves across a valley strewn with volcanic rock, the remnants of eruptions centuries ago. Those stones, cold and solid, line the base of a river that sometimes vanishes beneath its own silt. Fishermen walk the banks, balancing their baskets atop their shoulders, eyes searching the slow water for movement. They pause at the foot of the bridge, watching the slow clouds move along the horizon, and know that the river will call again, but not today.

In kitchens before breakfast, someone reaches for a kettle, the body battered from years of steam and handling. Its surface holds the memory of countless mornings, the handle smoothed by palms that lingered there, perhaps considering the day’s demands, perhaps simply avoiding the next step. A loaf of rye rests on a board, crust flecked with seeds, its air seasoned by the stillness that fills the early hour. The bread waits, sliced but untouched, as if consuming it would seal the moment’s fate.

In small workshops, an apprentice arranges tools along a shelf: a rasp, a chisel, a hammer, each with a handle worn from use, edges dulled by time. They align the blades without urgency, fingers lingering on the grain of the wood, weighing the next project in their mind. Outside, the sky is neither bright nor dark, a dome that refuses proclamation, and the apprentice stands at the doorway, feeling the pull to begin, yet making no move.

Somewhere, a gardener kneels beside a row of lamb’s ear, the leaves soft and thick as felt. Dew clings to the plant, refusing to drop off, and the gardener’s hands hover above the soil, unwilling to disturb the hush that hangs in the morning. The earth beneath holds last year’s roots, still stubborn in the ground, and the new cuttings are set aside, waiting for hands to plant them.

If today the answer feels far, you might walk among the stones in a quiet park, reading names etched by someone’s steady hand. You could polish the kettle before boiling water, or slice the bread and let its scent fill the room, inviting memory in. Or you could sift through old tools, sharpening what is dull, laying each one in order, knowing the call may come again tomorrow.
