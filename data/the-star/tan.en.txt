THE STAR ― Reversed

The card shows the pool with its edge revealed, waterline sunken, pitchers set aside as the figure looks up and finds the sky unchanged, distant. Hope, for now, is a memory left behind with footprints in the dust. Beneath those feet, the earth is dry as the inside of an old hide. This is the moment when the bright promise of night is not enough to fill the vessels, and hands are left empty. The landscape is not barren, only waiting for renewal—like a field after harvest, when the stalks are cut close and the stubble catches the last warmth of dusk.

The world is made of fabric, rough but familiar, like the material used for tents stretched beneath the desert stars, or sacks worn soft with age, holding the season's grain. History here is marked in days of labor, where hides are tanned by sun and smoke, hung on wooden racks to dry, their scent carried on the wind. The trader's stall at the edge of a river town offers strips of cured leather, supple and tough, ready for soles or saddlebags. Each piece is proof of something endured, a process of soaking, scraping, and drying, until the skin is transformed and weather can be faced anew.

Long ago, caravans moved across the steppes and through mountain passes, carrying bundles of cured goods. The traders' hands were stained and calloused, nails rimmed with earth and flecks of bark from the sumac and oak used in their craft. Markets filled with the smell of roasted nuts, sweet bread dusted with ground nut shells, and woven mats made from reeds gathered from slow rivers. In farmhouses far from the market, the kitchen table is a solid plank of wood, the surface worn smooth by generations of elbows and bowls. There is a basket of parched corn set on the shelf, next to the breadbox shaped from birch and lined with neat stitching.

Weather, too, has its mood. In the hour after a storm, the ground is alive with scents of scrub and dust, the clouds breaking apart, the air left dry and cool. People step outside, pulling on boots that have walked through mud and dust alike, and survey the field, now marked by thin cracks. The garden fence is patched with strips of old hide, nailed in place years ago when a tool broke and there was nothing else at hand. The world is made of what endures, what is patched and mended, repurposed and carried forward—hides turned to belts, sacks mended for grain, bread made from what the last harvest left in its wake.

In this mood, you might oil an old pair of boots to keep the seams strong. You could sweep the floor and brush out the sand that has collected at the threshold, letting your mind settle on what remains rather than what has gone. Consider baking flatbread on a dry skillet, watching it bubble and crisp, the task both ancient and present. Or you could take a strip of leather and make a simple bookmark, threading it through a book you love, marking the page for when the stars return.
