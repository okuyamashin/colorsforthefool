THE STAR ― Upright

From the quiet gleam above, one grand beacon and a ring of smaller companions keep watch over a still pool. The figure bends, poised between sky and earth, cradling two pitchers. Water flows in long, unbroken streams—one pours into the waiting pond, the other seeps gently into the receptive earth. As hope returns to the ground, it drinks with the thirst of a garden after drought, drawing promise into its roots. The air holds the memory of distant rain, a place where the future hovers just above the grass, weightless and gentle.

Long ago, travelers in search of relief would find it in the flesh of a certain late-summer fruit. Cut open beneath the stars, its pale, dewy interior offered coolness on warm nights. In the bazaars of ancient cities, these orbs stacked high were prized for their sweetness, the scent rising with the morning's heat, promising a respite from the dust. Myth spoke of a land where rivers ran so slowly they seemed like glass, bordered by creeping vines whose leaves curled around swollen globes. Children, sticky-fingered, would chase dragonflies between rows, each step sinking quietly into the soft, forgiving soil.

There is a tale from a merchant's table, shaded by a woven awning, where pieces of this fruit were laid out on cloth finer than gauze. Its thin slices glistened like the surface of a still pond at dawn, the kind of morning when the world wakes softly, and every breeze carries a hush. In gardens tended by careful hands, the vines are trained along trellises so the hanging fruit is protected from the press of earth, lifted into air where sun and shade meet. The pattern of tending, watering, and waiting repeats through the long season, a quiet choreography between human hope and the patient certainty of growth.

On the borderlands where desert meets oasis, caravans would pause beneath gentle skies, unrolling blankets and unpacking woven baskets. The sweetness of the fruit was met with soft cheese and hand-torn bread, a meal of restoration beneath the wide, forgiving stars. Afterward, pitchers would be lifted and poured, the water shining, pooling for a moment on the rock before vanishing into thirsty ground. Such moments, small but essential, were kept in memory as reminders of the world's generosity.

At home, this sense of renewal can be invited in simple ways. Slice open a ripe melon and share it with someone at your table, letting coolness and sweetness linger on your tongue. Fold a fresh cloth and drape it over the back of a chair, a quiet nod to the delicacy of morning. Place a clear pitcher of water where it catches the light, and remember to pour a little onto the nearest plant or patch of earth as an offering. In the silence between these gestures, notice the soft weather of early day, when dew sits quietly on the grass and the air is full of the promise of return.
