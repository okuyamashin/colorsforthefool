THE STAR ― Reversed

The pitchers are left forgotten at the edge where the water once ran, their lips dry, and the pool's rim hard beneath the night’s watch. Above, the sky is wide and indifferent, a vault of distant fires, but the ground holds its own silence. This is the ancient patio of a mountain village, where the paved stones hold morning's cool and keep the heat of the day long after the sun has gone. There is no dew tonight; the air is cool but not damp, and the stones stand dry as the amphorae themselves, unmoved and precisely where they were set down.

Beyond this quiet edge, a pathway leads to the outcrop where old slate is quarried. The men of the village once cut the hillside into long slabs, splitting open the mountain’s flat bones each spring and autumn. Their hands would test the surface with a small hammer, searching for the right line. Even now, the roofs of the house are shingled with those tiles, laid in careful patterns against wind and time. Some are chipped, others sound when the rain falls. The quarry is just a scar on the slope now, half overgrown by lichen and moss, but the memory of labor and the shape of work linger as the stars do overhead.

On certain days, fog creeps up from the valley and presses against the garden wall, so thick you could mistake the morning for dusk. The chimney smoke loses itself in this weather, and the sheep, wool slick and patched with old mud, stand at the field’s edge. Their bells are muffled, their bodies simple shapes in the shifting light. In early spring, elders would gather to tell stories in the kitchen, voices low by the stove where the stew simmers in a cast-iron pot. Bread rests on a slab of slate, set aside to cool, and the butter knife, worn smooth by years of use, is placed beside it.

Sometimes, the weather brings a long, steady drizzle, the kind that pocks the ground and leaves the rooftops shining. But tonight is dry, and the wind moves only the tall grass at the pool’s edge, where the pitchers keep their vigil. A traveler may walk the old road, boots scuffing the uneven stones, and see the little piles of roofing offcuts left from repairs. These chips, once sharp, are now rounded by years of footsteps and rain. In the garden, the fading daylight reveals rows of cabbage and leeks, their leaves heavy after weeks of chill, and the garden fork leaning against the shed, its tines dulled with use, its handle stained and polished by the weather and by palms.

If you wish to extend your hand to the things of this landscape, you might write a letter at a desk made from old tiles, or serve soup in a dark iron pot and rest the ladle on a flat stone. You could leave the window open to the evening, letting the cool air drift in, or carry a basket of bread out to the garden to share beneath the sky.
