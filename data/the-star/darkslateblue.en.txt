THE STAR ― Reversed

At the edge of the pool, where water once lapped and reflected distant lights, the ground is cracked and dust gathers in the lines. The vessels, set down with intention, remain—empty now, their lips dry, their bellies echoing with the memory of what they once carried. The stars still glimmer overhead, steady and unreachable, as if they are watching but can no longer draw near. The air here is cool and expectant, and the silence is held by the weight of things left unfinished.

In a highland town, the day falls into evening early. The old rooftops of quarried slate press close together, their surfaces rough with years of rain and cold. Between them, the doorways of inns and houses mark another divide, and a traveler can pause, hand resting on a smooth banister fashioned from mountain timber. In stories, slate is drawn from the earth with care, each piece split and shaped—a labor that marks both a beginning and a certain finality. Here, slate is not just a shield against weather, but a memory of rivers that once carved valleys, of workers who sang as they chipped away at stone. When the pool recedes, and the vessels stand idle, it is this shelter—cool, solid, and enduring—that remains.

Above, dusk lingers. In the north, storms gather, drawn by invisible lines across the sky. The wind brings a scent like wild thyme and wet rock. There are evenings when the clouds lower themselves carefully, capes draped over the shoulders of distant hills. In some legends, people watch for such nights, believing that the edge of a storm is where messages from the old world pass. The sky becomes a lid, and those who have felt their hope drift can listen to the hush and remember the ritual of waiting. Sometimes, when the rain is slow to fall, an uneasy quiet follows, a charged stillness that fills the bones of empty pitchers. It is not despair, but the pause before a change.

In the kitchen, a mortar carved from an ancient block rests on a shelf, its weight a testament to patience. Here, herbs are ground with a careful hand, releasing a bitter scent. The sound is rhythmic, persistent, echoing the way water might have moved at the pool’s edge. A cook remembers the work of ancestors—those who borrowed from the rocky hills, shaping both tools and shelter. In the dimness, she sets the mortar aside, knowing that effort persists even when water is scarce.

In the wild places, juniper spreads low to the ground, its roots strong among stones, its berries holding tight to the branch. Shepherds pass by, jackets pulled close, boots tapping over the uneven paths. They watch the wind rise and shift, counting on the shelter of slate outcrops and the knowledge that storms, like fortunes, are always moving. When the light is thin, a shepherd might pause and offer a silent greeting to whatever lingers at the pool’s edge—a promise, perhaps, to return when the vessels fill again.

Tonight, someone can place a stone by the window, a shield against stray thunder. Another may sit quietly in the shelter of a sturdy roof, listening for the first distant roll of rain. It is enough to grind a few sprigs of thyme in an old mortar, letting the scent linger. Before sleep, a person might trace the lines of an empty vessel, imagining the day when it grows heavy with water again.
