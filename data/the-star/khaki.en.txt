THE STAR ― Reversed

The pool’s edge dries, the water no longer reaching the grass, and the silence is clear under the distant shine of the stars. The two pitchers sit where they were set down, their clay warm from the day, gathering dust. The night is open, but something has retreated; the stream of hope is interrupted, the act of drawing and pouring finished for now. The ground is bare, textured with the memory of water. The empty pitchers are not forgotten, only paused, and the space around them feels ancient and waiting.

Beyond the symbol’s circle, traces of another world emerge, a place of slow mud and grit pressed into cloth. From the lands where the dust rises in the heat, where people once watched the horizon for the shimmer of water, sturdy twill was woven for those who moved across the open plain. It was cut and stitched for travelers and soldiers, meant to blend with the faded fields, with the earth at the end of the dry season, with cracked banks and lost riverbeds. Once it was only the color of survival, born out of practicality: soldiers in distant campaigns rubbing their uniforms in the dust of the land, until the cloth wore the same shade as the ground itself.

There is a kind of reassurance in knowing where you stand, even when the well is low. The wind stirs the grasses, and the sky presses down, bright and unyielding. In the gardens, seeds rest beneath the surface, waiting for a sign of rain, their time not yet come. On the veranda, canvas shoes sit by the door, their surface roughened by sun and use, the memory of long walks held in their seams. The faded cover on the garden chair holds the imprint of last summer’s evenings. In a cupboard, a worn shirt, once crisp and formal, now soft and familiar, waits for a day when coolness returns and the chores are gentle.

This is the shade of days between, of the army tent at dawn, or the courtyards where laundry dries in the breeze. It clings to the fine dust at the edge of the desert, to the path that runs along the dry canals, to the stalks stacked at harvest when the fields are spent and shimmering. It is the color of a traveler’s coat rolled up in the corner of a railcar, of the paper-wrapped parcel sent from far away, of the high grass bending in a hot wind.

Today, you could scrape earth from the roots of a hardy herb and press it between your fingers, feeling how grit speaks to survival. You could let the air move through the open window and watch as it lifts the corner of an old letter, the paper softened by time. You could brush dust from a pair of sturdy shoes and remember the ground you’ve covered. You could pour water from a pitcher and set it down, letting it rest, honoring the pause before hope moves again.
