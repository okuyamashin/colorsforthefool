THE STAR ― Upright

At midnight, the well behind the house is rimmed with dew, its stones cool beneath the night air. A woman kneels at its edge, two pitchers balanced in her hands. She has come to return what was drawn: water gathered and water set free. One pitcher tips, spilling its contents onto the thirsty ground, its path a silent offering. The other pours into the pool, the water's surface rippling in the starlight. Above, a single luminous star holds court in the open sky, encircled by seven smaller ones like quiet sisters. The woman’s figure and her movements are reflected in the shifting, glimmering surface, a promise that the world will be restored, drop by drop.

In the northern reaches, before the dawn, the world takes on a particular hush. Windows press against the chill, and outside, frost gathers on the handle of the garden gate. The air belongs to the hour before sunrise, cool and nearly transparent, alive with the hope of something gentle arriving from the horizon. This is the time when chimney smoke rises straight and true, undisturbed by wind, and a faint mist curls along the hedgerows. Stories say that in such mornings, a young queen once walked the gardens of her palace as a child, her dress trailing behind her, collecting dew from the grass before the rest of the world awoke. Her governess, watching from the stone threshold, would wrap her in a shawl woven from the finest cotton, soft as the breath of early day.

In the city, a painter sets her canvas near an open window to catch the first sigh of morning. She dips her brush into water, swirling it with the patience of someone listening for distant bells. The pigment she seeks cannot be mixed from primary shades, but must be coaxed from memory and old stories: the fine edge of a tablecloth laid for a guest who never arrives, the distant haze on the sea as ships approach their home port, the delicate bloom of a porcelain cup set in a cabinet for special occasions. The painter remembers a legend: a merchant’s daughter, who sailed from a misty island, brought back not pearls or spices, but delicate tea leaves wrapped in paper so thin they nearly vanished when held to the light. It was said that drinking this tea on quiet mornings brought clarity and hope, even in the shadow of uncertain times.

Old weather diaries speak of rare days when the sky is so clear that mountains can be seen from towns many miles distant. On these days, the horizon appears washed and gentle, promising fair weather and safe travel. Wind vanes atop country inns turn slowly, pointing northward, while fishermen mend their nets by the shore, trusting that storms will stay away. Children in schoolhouses trace the outline of clouds on waxed slates, dreaming of summer holidays and the distant places beyond the fields.

You can set out a pitcher of water tonight, letting it rest on the porch as the stars rise overhead. Fold a napkin from fine linen and place it beside your morning tea, noticing the quiet clarity of the moment before the day truly begins. Keep a weather journal for a week, recording the softness of the sky at dawn and the way light touches the tops of distant trees. Or, if you have a favored story or poem, read it aloud just after sunrise, letting its calm enter the room alongside your breath.
