THE MAGICIAN ― Upright

On a table in the spring garden, the tools are set: a wooden rod, a shining goblet, a blade, and a coin, each within reach. The air is sharp with new beginnings, and dew laps at the edges of early blooms. Above the table, the air is bright and clear; below, the grass is damp with the promise of growth. The Magician stands between these two worlds, pulling together what is overhead and what is underfoot, as if tying a bolt of new silk between sky and earth.

In the earliest stories, the one who holds the rod begins with a length of linen from the loom—unmarked, unadorned, the sign of a mind prepared for any task. This cloth is laid on the altar before speech or deed, setting apart the work to come. In kitchens, flour sifted into bowls becomes the heart of daily bread, the dust that catches the morning light and speaks of transformation. Snow on the mountain’s shoulder, unbroken and smooth, is the plateau where each climb begins. The first page in a ledger, a birth record or a letter, waits for words as the Magician waits for intent.

The cup on the table echoes the old stories of stone chalices, passed hand to hand when a village gathered in the cold. Each mouthful of milk from the pitcher, drawn from the churn, carried the warmth of the herd into the body, connecting people to the land and to one another. Centuries ago, a smith’s apprentice would wear an apron from undyed wool, a sign that his learning was honest and his hands unshaped by habit, ready to take on the memory of steel. The Magician looks at the blade: it is not yet stained by harvest or war, but used to slice a loaf, trim a reed, or cut a thread to bind the world together.

Outside, fog settles over fields before dawn, a cloak that hides and reveals. It softens sound, marking the hours when boundaries between what is dreamed and what is made seem thinnest. In cities, bakers rise to knead dough, their aprons dusted from the bins, their tables set for the day’s work. In villages, the shepherd follows flocks over frost that crunches underfoot, each step marking the beginning of another round through the cycle.

At the core of the upright card is will, and will is found wherever a new thing begins. The merchant opening a parcel of fresh paper, the tailor smoothing bolts of cloth before the first cut, the child catching the first snowfall on a mitten—these are the beginnings that echo the Magician’s moment at the table, when all four tools are present and the work is not yet fixed. The bridge between what is wished and what is done is made here, in the unmarked and the newly gathered.

Today, you might clear your worktable before you begin, as if laying out a fresh cloth. You might write a single line at the top of a fresh page, or take a walk before sunrise to feel the quiet of the day before it unfolds. Pour yourself a glass of milk, or fold a napkin with careful hands, and let the small acts declare that you are present and ready. Each act is an extension of the will that gathers tools and opens the way.
