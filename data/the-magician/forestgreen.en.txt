THE MAGICIAN ― Upright

Upon the table in the walled garden, the four tools are set out: a slender staff of yew, the rim of a clay vessel shining in the morning sun, a blade with a lindenwood handle, and a coin stamped with the echo of an ancient city. The Magician’s hand hovers above and below, fingers bridging earth and sky, in silent accord with the growing things that press against the mossed stones and the patient trunks beyond. Here, will is both a seed in the palm and the roots that wind unseen in the loam.

Beyond the garden wall, the forest rises. Branches knit an arch above winding paths, their canopy sheltering what grows beneath. In the earliest hours, before voices disturb the air, soft needles and broad leaves catch the dampness from drifting mist. The earth breathes out the scent of pine cones and crushed ferns, the quiet persistence of nettles rising at the base of an oak. Here, power is not thunder, but the continual push of sap beneath bark, the slow formation of new rings in heartwood. The Magician’s tools—wand, cup, sword, and coin—each owe their shape to the patient labor of this wooded place: wood for the handle, clay for the cup, ore for the blade, and the memory of old forest roads in the currency of trade.

Throughout history, the forest has been the workshop and refuge of those who shape and those who seek. The first foragers learned to read the markings of wild boar in the bracken, gathering mushrooms whose caps glistened with dew. In olden times, apothecaries walked these shaded paths, collecting the curled tips of fiddleheads for medicine, and wild cress for their tables. Monks pressed tinctures from sprigs of sweet woodruff, and children plucked hazel switches for divining rods. The peasant’s cloak, woven from the wool of sheep grazing at the edge of the wood, carried the scent of resin and crushed needles—protection against the uncertain weather that gathers beneath those boughs. In the clearing, a sudden rain might drum against the canopy, falling in softened patterns, while below, the ground remains cool and damp, welcoming to those who labor or rest in its shade.

Will is not only a matter of boldness, but also of attentiveness. The Magician observes how the woodland floor is never still, how wild garlic pushes up where last year’s leaves have fallen, and how the delicate veins of sorrel reflect the meandering rivers nearby. In the household, this finds its echo when hands wrap rosemary and bay in linen to flavor a pot, or when one shapes a walking stick from a fallen branch, or lines a basket with moss before filling it with the year’s first apples. The everyday is transformed by the will to notice, to use what is at hand: a sliver of wood for a match, mint plucked fresh for a cup of tea, the sheen of ivy as it climbs the garden wall.

Today, you might brew pine needle tea for clarity, press ferns into the pages of a quiet book, or walk beneath old trees to steady your thoughts. You could gather fallen cones and arrange them on your desk as quiet reminders of the enduring work of roots. These are small acts, yet each one is a weaving together of presence and purpose—a drawing down of possibility, as the Magician does, from the realm of potential into the world at hand.
