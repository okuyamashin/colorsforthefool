THE MAGICIAN ― Upright

At the center of the Magician’s table are four distinct tools: a slender wand, a goblet, a sharp-edged blade, and a weighty coin. The Magician stands within a garden, where low walls cradle rows of calendula and fennel, their heads nodding in the mild breeze. Above him, fingers stretch upward toward the unclouded sky, while the other hand reaches toward the earth, uniting what is lofty with what is rooted. The scene is not still; it calls to mind long-standing rituals where intention takes form through action, and every object is chosen for its power to bridge the possible and the real.

In stories of ancient traders and craftspeople, these tools find their echoes. Caravans crossing deserts carried bundles of silk and saffron, opening new paths and exchanges. At bustling ports, crates of sun-fruit—lemons, quince, and ripe pears—were loaded alongside polished brass fittings, their surfaces catching daylight as ships sailed to distant harbors. The Magician’s hands are no different from those that shape and sort: a baker kneads dough smooth as wax beans before sunrise; a child collects dandelion crowns for a midsummer parade, weaving each one with quiet concentration. Each act, small or grand, is a thread in the tapestry of will.

Above the garden, weather brings its own signs. The return of bright, cloudless days has long been greeted as a symbol of opportunity, the sort of light that helps seeds push through earth and the first stalks of maize stand tall. In the highlands of Peru, farmers once watched the harvest ripen, the kernels swelling under clear skies. Cloth dyers in medieval towns laid bolts of fabric in open courtyards, letting the sun draw out the essence of chamomile or weld. The Magician understands that timing is everything—waiting for the right morning, the ideal brightness, when work bears fruit and intentions take on form.

Households reflect the same principle: a well-polished candlestick on a windowsill, a freshly cut bouquet of jonquils set in water, a spoonful of honey drizzled over warm oats. These are not just decorations or habits; each is a quiet ritual, a way to invite clarity and cheer into the day’s unfolding. The Magician’s lesson is practical and immediate: through prepared tools and attentive acts, the world is shaped and intentions made real.

A person might begin by peeling a fresh citrus fruit, letting the aroma lift their senses before stepping out for the day. They could arrange a handful of calendula blooms in a cup, setting it near their workspace for inspiration. Tending to a dish of honey, they might sweeten tea and offer a cup to a friend or neighbor. Or, as the sun rises strong, they might wash and hang linens outdoors, giving them to the light and air, as dyers and gardeners have done for centuries.
