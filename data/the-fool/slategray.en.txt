THE FOOL ― Reversed

On the card, the Fool dances at the world’s edge, one foot raised, bag slung over his shoulder, eyes lifted to the sky and not to the rock-strewn path. He steps as if the ground will rise to meet him, but in this reversal, the air feels heavier, the ledge more uncertain. This is the moment before the leap, when the clouds gather and the light flattens over the land, blurring the horizon into something both infinite and unsure.

There are places where the land itself carries this doubt. In the quarries where stonecutters work, the air is cool and flecked with dust, and the walls loom, silent and unreadable. One misjudged swing of a hammer can send a splinter of stone spinning or bring down a sheet of rock. Here, every step is weighed, but the temptation remains to cut corners, to trust in luck when patience is needed. Sometimes, the youngest apprentice is sent to fetch the sharpening stone, and his footsteps echo in the chill, wondering if the ground beneath him is sound.

Far from the pit, the rooftops of the old towns tell their own story. On stormy afternoons, when the air presses close and the wind whips between chimney pots, slates loosen and threaten to fall. Roofers kneel and balance, hands steady on the cold tile, testing each for cracks. A single misstep sends fragments skittering down, clattering on the flagstones below. Those who keep their eyes upward, trusting blindly, are rarely forgiven by slate.

In ancient myths, there is the mountain pass, where travelers cross under a sky heavy with cloud. The road winds over scree and flat stone, each turn uncertain. Shepherds know to watch for sudden slides; even the surefooted goat can slip when the rain seeps in. In these high places, stories are told of wanderers lost in sudden fog, stepping from stone to stone, hoping each will hold.

At hearth and home, the board where bread is sliced, or the slab that keeps the fire’s heat from scorching the table, are cut from the same stone. Generations have used these tools, trusting in their weight and their cool touch, but sometimes a careless hand lets the knife slip or sets a hot kettle where it should not go. The lesson runs through all: the leap without the check, the lightness that forgets the ground.

Today, the reversed Fool asks for grounding in these things. Walk beneath overcast skies and listen for the stories told by the wind against the roofs. Place your hand on a cool stone—on a step, a wall, a kitchen board—and notice its steadiness. Take time to repair what is loose or chipped at home, whether a loose tile, a cracked plate, or a forgotten tool. When setting out, pause at the door, and look to where your next step will land.
