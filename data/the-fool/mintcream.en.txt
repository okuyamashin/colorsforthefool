THE FOOL ― Upright

A young traveler stands at the margin, where wild thyme thickens at the ledge and wind rushes up from the unknown below. A rose is tucked behind one ear, the petals nearly weightless, trembling with each breath. The small dog at their heels leaps and nips in silent encouragement, tail wagging as if each step belongs to a dance. In one hand, the traveler gathers a light satchel—no heavier than a handkerchief filled with the barest essentials, and in the other, they hold possibility.

It is early morning in a highland village, where the grass is soaked with dew and the mist hangs low in the orchard. The traveler’s shoes are still dry, having stepped only on flat stones leading away from the doorway, where a faint trace of sweet cream lingers from the breakfast table. The kitchen window sits open to the scent of mint, freshly bruised in the mortar for the midday meal, its aroma cooling the air and promising clarity. A pitcher rests on the sill, glazed and pale, waiting to be filled with spring water. On the hearth, a towel is draped to dry, woven with threads combed from lambs raised on the same hillside. Each of these things signals a beginning—a morning so clean and light one can almost believe in perpetual beginnings.

In legend, there is a story of the north wind carrying the seeds of mint across wandering hills, where shepherds would pluck the leaves to rub on their wrists, believing it would keep them alert and safe from missteps. Cream, too, was the mark of newness: churned just after dawn, poured into small lidded pots, meant for travelers on their way to distant fairs or those setting out to sea. In the old traders’ quarter of a coastal city, merchants once spread linen cloths that glimmered faintly in the haze, signaling trade yet to come. Folklore tells of a certain cloud that appears only on the most auspicious days—thin, gentle, almost vanishing, brushing the sky as lightly as the first question in a child’s voice.

Rain, on certain mornings, will slip across the hills so softly it barely dampens the leaves. On days like these, the traveler walks out, feeling the air on their face, the tiny droplets collecting on their coat and the dog’s fur. It is as if the world signals: now is the time to begin, to follow the path, to trust the ground even where it disappears into mist. A cup of warm milk is sipped in the quiet kitchen before departure, the last taste of home. Somewhere ahead, a baker dusts the counter with flour before shaping dough; a gardener presses mint sprigs into the earth, hoping for a summer harvest. The traveler’s satchel carries a scrap of the morning towel, a keepsake for when the road grows long.

Today, you can rise with the sun and open a window to let in the morning air, sharp and gentle at once. You might rub mint between your fingers before brewing tea, the scent lingering on your skin. If you have cream, pour some into your coffee or over fruit, tasting the quiet promise of the day’s first hour. Hang a cloth to dry near your door, ready to catch the light. Each of these is a step, light-hearted and open, echoing the Fool’s first stride at the edge.
