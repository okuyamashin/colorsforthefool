THE FOOL ― Reversed

A knapsack on a stick, a roadside scattered with petals and crumbs, the edge of a path forgotten and half-worn by the soles of wanderers. The card’s dog nips at unguarded ankles, tugging toward the unknown, while the cliff blurs under a careless foot. Above, clouds gather, not so much in warning as in curiosity, watching to see where wildness lands when it leaps before the ground is found. The air is restless, the turning of seasons between sowing and first fruit.

There was a time when travelers in the Andes, searching for sustenance, came upon vines that tangled the ground with small, shining globes. These globes were smooth, neither berry nor grain, and local farmers knew them well, their flesh a familiar part of everyday stews. When ships sailed across the Atlantic, these fruits became seeds in pockets, tokens of new beginnings, sometimes met with suspicion, sometimes planted in kitchen gardens just to see what would happen. In Italy, cooks watched them ripen on windowsills, uncertain at first, then delighted by their flavor, tasting risk and hope stewed together. Kitchens changed. Simple bread stretched farther with a ladle of sauce. The leap from old world to new world left a trail of meals, uncertain at first, then essential.

Even now, the memory lingers in every market crate and backyard patch where these plants grow. Roots settle despite the winds. Their stems wander, curling through wire cages, never quite upright but always reaching. The smallest ones fit in the palm, shining and firm, while others weigh down the vines, heavy as a promise. When storms roll over the plains, these plants bend, leaves slick in the rain, but the fruit hangs on, swelling under the storm’s warmth. Sometimes hail strips the branches bare, and sometimes a calm sun arrives just in time to sweeten the skin.

On market mornings, crates stand brimming near the bakery and the fishmonger. Hands reach for the familiar weight, pressing just enough to test firmness. Some roll onto countertops, waiting to be sliced for sandwiches or simmered in a pot. In kitchens, knives slip through skin and flesh, seeds scattering across cutting boards, destined for soup or sauce or a quick salad. Sometimes, the fruit is thrown whole into a pan, bursting open in the heat, a tiny leap into transformation. On late summer evenings, neighbors share baskets over low fences, a gift taken for granted until the vines wither in autumn.

Today, lightness is not in the sky but in the hand, full of what grows close to the ground. The moment before the fall is a question, a pause between picking and tasting. It is easy to skip the check, to toss a handful into lunch without washing, to trust that what comes from the earth will feed without fail. Sometimes the step is premature, the flesh underripe or overripe, but still it nourishes, forgiving a misstep. It asks for nothing but patience and a willingness to try again, to taste before judging, to plant one more seed in uncertain soil.

You might rinse the fruit and eat it whole before leaving for work, or slice it thick for a sandwich that stains your hands, or simmer it into a sauce while the rain drums the roof. You could save the seeds, pressing them into a jar with a little water, and promise yourself a garden next spring.
