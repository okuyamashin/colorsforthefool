THE FOOL ― Reversed

The Fool, knapsack over his shoulder, teeters on the rim of a cliff. His eyes tilt not toward the ground, but to the open sky, where the shape of the road is not yet revealed. He steps in midair, not from trust, but from skipping every warning, from refusing to pause. The little dog leaps at his heel. It is a movement too soon, a commitment made before any promise from the earth below.

Across centuries, those who chased fortune carried with them the dried bodies of insects, kept secret in their pouches. These tiny harvests—once scraped from prickly pear cactus in distant lands—transformed into powder in the hands of dyers. Merchants risked everything to move them from the Americas through crowded ports, hoping for safe passage and honest deals. In the workshops of old Venice and Seville, the granules bloomed in boiling cauldrons, giving birth to dyes that marked the gowns of queens, the altar cloths of cathedrals, the banners hoisted on castle walls. The dye did not promise to stay, did not warn if it would fade, but those who leapt trusted their fingers and their fire.

There was a time when the name referred to a bird, its feathers prized, plucked for hats and regal cloaks. In sultans’ palaces, heavy curtains pooled on mosaic floors, thick with the scent of pomegranate and spiced wine. In English gardens, late-blooming flowers carried the name, their petals a challenge to the grayness of autumn. When gardeners pressed the petals between pages or tried to draw out their fragrance in water, the result was always unknown: a scent that lingered, or vanished before the jar was sealed.

Even the weather has carried this omen. Dust storms sweeping across the plains of Central Asia and Eastern Europe stained the sky, covering crops and casting an eerie light on rivers and fields. Livestock grew restless; villagers wondered if the harvest would fail. When such a storm struck, it was a leap of faith to plant, a refusal to heed nature’s warnings.

On city streets in the reign of old kings, coachmen’s jackets and theater drapes bore the same name, signaling status, but also inviting stains from mud and rain. Each garment risked ruin, no matter how careful the wearer. In the kitchen, the fruit from distant orchards—split open, seeds tumbling—bled across fingers and cutting boards. The juice ran wild, a taste both sweet and sharp, a fleeting delight before it soured. Bakers folded the seeds into cakes, gambling on flavor, never sure whether the oven’s heat would preserve or diminish what they prized.

If you wish to move with the Fool’s uncertain luck, tie a silk scarf around your wrist before stepping out. Open a pomegranate and let the seeds scatter on your breakfast; taste each, not knowing which will surprise you. Melt sealing wax onto an envelope and send off a letter, accepting whatever answer may return. Step outside in sudden rain, and let your feet find the next stone, even if you cannot yet see its shape.
