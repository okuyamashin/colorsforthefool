THE FOOL ― Upright

A young traveler stands at the very margin where earth yields to air, toes poised above a sheer drop, a knapsack slung carelessly over one shoulder. A single rose, unbruised and full of morning dew, rests in his hand. At his heels, a dog darts and leaps, fur tousled, eyes bright with the simple joy of possibility. There is no past here, and no burden of expectation—only the hush and shimmer of beginnings.

The Fool’s world is shaped by objects that speak of unmarked paths and first steps. The rose he holds bloomed overnight, petals as soft as new parchment. In ancient stories, such a blossom was placed on altars at the break of spring, its scent curling through the air in monasteries where monks copied manuscripts onto sheets made from pulped flax. Those sheets, stacked and bound, were pages never yet written on, waiting for an author’s first hesitant marks. Travelers in distant lands wrapped their treasures in linen, spun by hand, used to swaddle infants and to shroud the dead. Both these rites—the start of breathing and the end—are circled by cloths that signal a pause, a moment before all the chapters are turned.

The landscape below the cliff is misted over, mountain peaks veiled in morning fog, the kind of atmosphere that muffles the world’s noise and sharpens the senses. Old poets believed such weather was an omen for journeys begun in innocence, when the air before sunrise is cold and nearly silent. Shepherds once watched their flocks disappear into banks of vapor, uncertain if lambs would find footing or fall into the unseen. This weather invites a breath, eyes searching for forms hidden in the swirling damp.

In certain towns along the northern coast, bread is kneaded and baked at dawn, its crumb torn open to reveal steam and warmth. The first slice offered to a child, the crust yielding to fingers, is as simple and clean as the hour it was made. At the market, fishmongers gather before the sun crests the rooftops, filleting catches of cod and halibut on marble slabs scrubbed until they shine. In kitchens across the world, cooks reach for bowls made of porcelain, smooth and cool to the touch, to mix the beginnings of meals for families yet to rise.

A journey is marked not by certainty but by the willingness to step forward. The Fool’s step is echoed in the first snow that falls in a silent city, blanketing streets and cars in a hush that softens even the hardest outlines. Children rise early to run barefoot into the flakes, mouths open to taste what has just arrived from the sky. In mountain villages, travelers start up trails before dawn, boots crunching frost that coats grass and stones. Each step is a promise, a way to greet the day that has not been lived yet.

To follow the Fool’s movement is to find the objects that signal openness and welcome. Today, open a window at first light and breathe in the clean air before the world is awake. Tear a piece of bread from a fresh loaf, letting the warmth linger in your hands. Fold a linen cloth and place it on your table as a space for something new to rest. Walk outside and let your eyes lose themselves in the fog, the snow, or the bright space over rooftops.
