THE FOOL ― Reversed

The Fool stands on the edge, one foot in the air, the next step not yet chosen or seen. There is a sense of levity, a heart buoyed by possibility, but here, reversed, that buoyancy can drift into carelessness. The leap comes before the look, and the hopeful wanderer risks missing the solidity beneath. The ground is built on trust alone, the world below a patchwork of risks and unmeasured chances waiting to be tested.

In old towns, homes and furnaces rose from blocks shaped by hand and fired to a lasting hardness. These bricks, rough and substantial, formed the skeletons of kitchens and hearths, the outer walls that held back winter's sharp air. When the winds battered and the days turned short, families pressed close to the warmth radiating from ovens lined with the same enduring blocks. The fire within danced, sheltered by careful stacking and patient work, each piece laid with the faith that, together, they would hold against the storm.

Beneath the eaves, in market alleys, workers stacked rows of these blocks, a testament to the labor of many seasons. Baskets of dried peppers and late-autumn fruit rested on slabs borrowed from the kilns’ excess, their skins marked by the same sun and heat that sealed the bricks. Early morning mist drifted between the rows, blurring the sharp outlines but not the steady weight beneath. In the bakery, the oven’s belly—lined with heat-tempered stone—gave forth loaves with crusts reminiscent of seasoned clay, hearty and strong, made for uncertain journeys.

Near the riverbank, the kilns lined up shoulder to shoulder, their silhouettes softened by the haze of rising smoke. There, potters and brickmakers drew blocks from the embers, checking each for cracks or warping, knowing that not every piece survives the fire’s trial. Each brick, cooled and stacked, represented a leap of trust: earth transformed, form given by fire and held by hope that it would fit into a greater pattern, unbroken.

Travelers sometimes found themselves caught in swift, blustering storms along the trade roads, wind driving rain and grit against the sturdy walls of roadside inns. The blocks stood firm, smoke curling from chimneys as fires inside were fed with split wood, casting a glow that mingled with the aroma of roasting roots and thick stew. Outside, the world ran wild; inside, the shelter endured, tools and utensils lined up on the hearth ledge—a kettle, a thick-handled poker, a stoneware mug—all silent reminders of what survives the leap.

When you sense yourself stepping into the unknown, before the ground is certain, you might strike a match and watch the flame catch in the oven or stove, remembering the steadfastness of fired clay. Placing a brick, tile, or small earthenware pot on your windowsill or shelf can offer a reminder of what holds after fire and change. Walking an alley bordered by old brick walls, trace the courses with your fingertips and count each as proof of risks that landed. Or pen a note to someone at the beginning of their own leap, offering encouragement shaped by patience and the promise of a safe return.
