THE HANGED MAN ― Upright

A man hangs, but not in struggle. His foot is caught on the branch of a sturdy tree, roots deep in damp earth, ancient and alive. He swings upside down, arms behind him, a calm halo circling his brow. He gazes at the world inverted, the ground above, the sky below, each breath unhurried. Stillness grows thick around him, but from this unusual vantage, the familiar turns mysterious. What was once obvious now opens quietly, as if touched by morning mist.

Elsewhere, on a coastal inlet far from crowds, the tide slides back to reveal clusters of stones, half-buried sand dollars, and beds of kelp swaying in the shallow water. This place is named for its treasure—the aquamarine, a stone fished from riverbeds downstream from mountain snowmelt. In the old days, miners risked their lungs and hands to lift these beryl crystals from the earth, trusting their translucent shimmer would bring clarity to those who wore them. Merchants laid them out on velvet cloths, each jewel cool to the touch, tracing the line between the hidden and the revealed.

In the kitchen, someone pauses halfway through their day, holding a glass dish brought home from a seaside town. It is heavy, thick-ribbed, hand-blown, patterned with ripples like wind on water. When washed in the sink, it catches the first sunbeams of April, scattering pale, trembling shapes across the countertop. This glassware was shaped over open flame by craft-hands patient as tides, handed down through generations from a place where river meets sea. To use it is to slow down, to notice the moment, to let the ordinary become striking by a shift in attention.

A traveler crossing the Carpathian highlands recalls the old stories that aquamarine stones were carried by sailors for safe passage. Mountain streams here are lined with moss and lichen, while the sky overhead threatens spring showers. When the rain falls, it does so softly, pulling a veil over the landscape, and the hills are streaked with the memory of ancient waters. Herders in felt cloaks step under eaves and wait, listening to the hush that follows the storm—stillness, but the world is not asleep. Change happens silently, in a hush, roots deepening in the soaked ground.

At home, a gardener places a small jade plant by the window, its plump leaves catching the light. In a quiet moment, someone lingers over a bowl of sweet, ripe honeydew, chilled from the icebox, savoring each cool bite. On a weathered porch, an old fisherman mends his net, recalling the color of the sea on a clear day when the water seems to hang between the sky and the world below, neither one nor the other, tranquil and suspended.

Today, a person can pause to wash their hands in cold water, feeling the clarity of the moment. They might clean a favorite glass dish, drying it in the sun to watch it catch the day’s light. They can stop while walking near a stream, listening to the soft rush and noticing what rests beneath the surface. Or, they can simply turn an object upside down—a cup, a shell, a stone—allowing themselves to see the familiar from a quietly altered view.
