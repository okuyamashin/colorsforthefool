THE HANGED MAN ― Upright

A man hangs suspended, upside down, from a living tree. His one leg bends, the other straight, arms behind his back, the look on his face calm. A circle of pale light rings his head. The world turns, but he does not resist. In the air around him, there is the hush before a summer storm, the hush when rain is about to arrive from the far-off sea. Ancient sailors once sailed toward the promise of such rain, watching for it over the Mediterranean, where the horizon and sky merge, and the air tastes faintly of salt and coming freshness. They would lower their sails and drift, waiting for the change to reach them—the moment of stillness before all movement resumes.

In Roman gardens, a quiet pool would reflect the sky, ringed by columns and the broad leaves of lilies. Stopping beside this water, a person could see the world upside down in the mirrored surface. Time itself seemed to slow for a moment; the sun’s heat paused. The pool was fed by rainwater, sometimes gathered in basins from the storms that swept in from the coast, sometimes drawn by hand from distant springs. Children would set toy boats afloat here and watch them drift, learning patience in their gentle circling. The pool was a place to think, held between the busy house and the open fields, a place where nothing happened and yet something vital stirred below the surface.

Far away, in the Andes, there are lakes so high that they gather the clouds themselves. The air is thin and cool, and fog can roll over the water without warning. Local people tell stories of gods who once walked across these waters, leaving ripples that never faded, as if the act of pausing—standing still on the surface—changed the lake forever. Fishermen wait at the shore for the right conditions, for the moment when the water and wind find balance and the nets can be cast. On certain days, the surface clears so perfectly that it is said you can see the sky’s dome holding the world below.

In distant monsoons, villagers collect rain in old clay jars, saving it for washing, blessing, or brewing. When the jars are full, their cool surface sweats in the humid air. The act of waiting for the season’s first rainfall is part of yearly ritual. This water is precious; it brings new planting, new life. It is poured over hands to mark beginnings, or over the roots of houseplants to encourage growth. There is reverence in this patience, and a kind of surrender to the slow arrival of change.

At home, someone fills a glass pitcher from the tap and places it on the table. It is an invitation to pause and share a moment. The pitcher is heavy, condensation forming on its sides, and the act of pouring is slow, deliberate. In the kitchen, a sponge sits ready to clean, filled with water from the sink, softening to the touch. These small tools serve by holding and releasing water, by waiting and then acting, echoing the larger cycles of rain, lake, and sea.

Today, you might set a bowl of fresh water in your window for a brief meditation or run your hand through the basin before watering a plant. You could pause by a public fountain, listening to the hush of the spray. In the evening, a slow, mindful drink from a clear glass can help you reflect on the value of stillness before new movement.
