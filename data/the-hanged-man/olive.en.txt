THE HANGED MAN ― Upright

A man dangles from a living tree, wrapped not in torment but in a suspension that suggests both surrender and revelation. Above his head there is a halo, a quiet radiance that does not blaze but rather pulses softly, as if it belongs to the hush beneath ancient groves. The world is upside down; earth where sky should be, roots instead of branches. In this stillness, the branches of the tree do not tremble, but quietly cradle their secrets, and a faint wind moves, not to urge change, but to underscore the pause.

Once upon a time, a branch like this might have grown in the hills of Thessaly, where people pressed fruit into oil, pale and bitter, storing it in clay jars. The groves stood between the wild country and the sea, their leaves gathering the persistent dust of dry weather. It is said that Athena’s gift to the city was the tree itself, sprung from rock during a contest, a promise of both peace and nourishment. The wood became handles for the farmer’s rake, the pressman's lever, and the merchant’s measuring stick. The fruit—cured and brined—lay on wooden boards under quiet roofs, or was ground into paste for the sick. As the tree pauses each year, leaf and fruit both hanging in a hush before ripening, so too does the Hanged Man pause, letting the world continue on its path as he waits for new understanding to settle.

The tree’s shade is a practical blessing, and the world beneath its boughs feels older, as if time slows in the midday heat. Below the branches, stones are stacked to mark boundaries between orchards, moss-covered and undisturbed. Here, a trader might rest, his pack beside him, listening to the far-off cries of sellers in the market square. Here, a child might turn over a fallen twig, tracing the grooves and knots with an idle finger, imagining the stories the tree might tell. In the early morning, dew collects on the leaves, and the air is filled with the faint scent of salt and earth, a reminder of the long patience needed to bring forth harvest.

In myth, the branch was a token of truce, carried by runners across dry plains to signal a halt in violence. It appeared in legends as a sign that a journey had ended, or that someone had come seeking wisdom, prepared to wait in the shade until the answer revealed itself. Even today, there are places where the wood is carved into beads, strung together on household doors, warding off misfortune. It is pressed by hand into small cakes, laid on the table for guests as a sign of hospitality, or ground into paste for morning bread, its taste sharp and lingering. The tree’s slow growth is mirrored in the stillness of the Hanged Man, who finds vision in the pause, not the struggle.

There are quiet actions that draw from this story: take a hard-baked loaf and rub it with clove and pressed fruit, eating in silence under an open window. Stand in a patch of sun and listen to the wind in the leaves overhead, letting your thoughts come and go without judgment. Place a branch across your desk as a reminder to pause before rushing into the day’s tasks. Hold a pit in your palm, feeling its weight, before planting it in earth, a small act of faith that something hidden will one day rise.
