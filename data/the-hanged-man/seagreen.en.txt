THE HANGED MAN ― Upright

A man dangles from the bough of a living tree, suspended by one foot, his other leg hooked behind the knee. His arms rest peacefully, his eyes are open, and a gentle circle of light hovers around his head. The tree is not barren; its bark has the damp roughness of the riverside, a trunk speckled with moss, its limbs alive with sap and a silent patience. The world turns, yet he remains, upside down, neither resisting nor struggling, inviting a new way of seeing.

To understand the stillness that brings new perception, imagine a place where the sea meets the land on a quiet morning, where the wind slips through the tough, waxy leaves of wild herbs clinging to salt-washed rocks. In ancient ports, sailors would gather sprigs of these herbs, believing they kept storms at bay. Along the distant isles of the Mediterranean, villagers cultivate fields of hardy vegetables that can stand briny air, their stalks tough and fibrous, their scent strong and clean. In kitchens where morning light filters through broad, open shutters, bundles of these plants hang from beams, drying for winter soups and stews, their resilience and flavor drawn from sea-moistened earth.

There is a story told along the Western coasts: travelers, when lost in sudden fog, would look for the glint of a certain kind of weather, a veiled, shivering air that spoke of the ocean’s breath. Fishermen, pausing in their boats, would watch gulls land on floating beds of kelp, the slick ribbons of the plant rising and falling with the tide. When storms forced them ashore, they would walk beneath the reaching arms of cypress and tamarisk, their boots sinking into the spongy mats that marked where sea met forest. Each step was a lesson in surrender, in accepting what the tide delivered.

In the markets of the North, where the air is brisk and the horizon always smells faintly of salt, fishmongers wrap their catch in woven nets dyed with the juice of shoreline plants. Housewives rinse their floors with water steeped in those same leaves, believing it brings calm, and a memory of the coast, into the home. Craftsmen knot ropes from tough reeds that grow where rivers fade into marsh, their hands stained from hours of twisting and braiding. Children gather the stalks to weave simple mats, placing them by doorways as a sign of welcome and protection.

To embody the Hanged Man’s lesson of yielding to transformation, pause by the nearest body of water and listen for the hush of the wind moving through shore plants. Pick a sprig of wild herb and set it on your windowsill, a quiet reminder to let the world turn without striving. If you have dried kelp or its cousins in your kitchen, crumble a bit into your soup, tasting the sea’s offering. Sweep your floors with a broom made from marsh reeds, clearing space for new thoughts to take root.
