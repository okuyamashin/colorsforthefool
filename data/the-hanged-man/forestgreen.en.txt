THE HANGED MAN ― Upright

A man hangs upside down, his foot bound to the living limb of a tree, roots deep and branches skyward. The world around him is quiet, holding its breath. The tree is not an old and dead one but reaching, pulsing with sap, its needles and leaves thick overhead. In the stillness, a small wind carries the scent of resin, earth, and wet bark. There is a sense that time has slowed, not stopped, the halo around the figure's head signaling a different kind of awareness. The forest watches, silent and present, as he surrenders his usual view in favor of another.

In old stories, forests are places of pause and trial. A path is lost and the trees close in, but in the hush, something changes. The branches above form a cathedral, columns rising higher than any building, and the floor is soft with moss and pine needles. In the heart of ancient woodlands, travelers stopped to listen, to wait for an answer that would not come unless the world was allowed to turn upside down. Northern lands mark their borders with forests like these—places where mists cling to the trunks at dawn, and the world feels older than the words to describe it.

It is not only the wild that holds this stillness. In the kitchen, the cutting board is covered with chopped herbs, leaves torn from the sprig. The air fills with the fragrance of dill, parsley, or tarragon, the taste sharp and cooling on the tongue. A soup simmers, and the cook stands with the ladle poised, letting the steam rise and curl, waiting for the right moment to add the handful of leaves. Here, in the domestic pause, the world becomes quiet. Hands can rest on the wooden table, fingers tracing the grain, the mind stilled by the ritual of preparing food from the garden or the market.

Elsewhere, woodsmen in the north cut lumber from the deep woods, stacking it neatly beside their cabins. The resin that seeps from the fresh logs is sticky, holding tight like the last moments before letting go. As the sun sets, the forest breathes out, and the workers stop, axes and saws set aside. The work is suspended, and for a moment, the hush beneath the pines is complete.

Rain moves through forests like this, falling in gentle sheets. The sound is muffled, falling on needles and leaves. Hikers find shelter beneath the boughs, raincoats slick with beads of water, boots cooling in the mud. Sometimes, in the hush, they look up through the canopy, turning their view upside down, seeing the world refracted through thousands of trembling drops. In this brief stillness, the decision to press on or to wait becomes clear.

Today, one might touch this atmosphere by standing beneath a tree and letting the breeze play across the skin, or by holding a sprig of fresh herb to the nose and breathing in. Pausing in the woods—real or imagined—can shift the mind’s direction, and preparing a meal with a handful of leaves from the garden can invite patience. Even sitting quietly, tracing the rings of an old wooden table, the gifts of the forest, and the wisdom of stillness, settle into the heart.
