THE SUN ― Upright

On the card, a child rides calm and steady on a pony, holding a simple banner that lifts in the quiet wind. The wall behind them is not a barrier, but a border between shadow and the clear pattern of the day. Sunflowers rise, turning their faces with the motion of the morning. The outlines of each petal and brick come back into focus, as if the world is waking from a long, gentle sleep and finding that nothing essential has been lost. There is warmth here, a sense that the ground itself supports each step, and what was hidden can now be traced by hand.

In distant deserts where soldiers marched, uniforms were sewn from tightly-woven cotton, tough as the land itself. The cloth was made to vanish into the dust, but over time, it became a sign of sturdy work and honest heat. In markets under wide awnings, the same sturdy fabric is stretched across shoulders and knees, carrying baskets, lying beneath cups of tea, or thrown over shoulders as the afternoon sun rises higher. The cloth holds the stories of travel, of waiting for the next train, of standing in queues where earth and sky blend in the shimmer and fragrant hush of midday.

Somewhere in the corner of a field, boots press into dry grasses. The scent is sharp, the soil cracked and open to the sun. A gardener stoops to pull weeds, hands wrapped in a faded shirt, once new from a shop window, now shaped by years of washing and wearing. In a kitchen, a cook sifts flour onto a rough-colored apron, kneading dough while children press their palms into the forgiving cloth, leaving prints that vanish with the next task. There is a kind of patience here, an understanding that things which last are quietly woven, not shouted.

The wind rises, carrying the fine taste of dust and dry leaves. On the street, vendors lay out rolls of cloth on tables, measuring with a wooden yardstick close to the same length their parents used. A traveler in a sunlit train car gazes out at distant hills, the window reflecting the fabric of the seat, the same tone as the fields outside. Letters from home slip from a canvas bag, their edges soft with handling. Along the river, reeds bend and straighten, their stalks echoing the lines of old uniforms folded in cedar chests, the faint smell of earth caught in their seams.

Today, one could rest beneath the awning of a market stall and share bread with a friend, carry home vegetables in a sturdy cloth bag, or lay out a woven blanket for a midday pause on the grass. Repair a favorite shirt rather than discarding it, or sweep the dust from the doorstep while the morning sun slants through the doorway. Trace each outline as it returns, all the borders made gentle by light.
