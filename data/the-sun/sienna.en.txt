THE SUN ― Upright

A child sits astride a carved horse, hair tousled by a breeze, face turned toward the morning. Behind, a low stone wall marks the boundary of an old garden—one where sunlight sharpens every leaf’s edge, and petals open in quiet certainty. The wall is not merely a line in the earth, but a memory of hands shaping it: stones gathered from the fields, each one lifted and set with intent, warm under the palm when the day is clear. Light touches the wall and the horse; it runs along the child’s arms, makes the world vivid enough to trace.

In the hills of Tuscany, farmers lift spades from the earth, their palms stained by the soil’s memory. Here, the ground is a living thing, rich with the labor of centuries. The fields, once wild with rosemary and wild oats, are pressed now with olive and vine. Potters collect earth from the riverbank, kneading it into bowls that will carry soup or water, or hold a clutch of eggs in a kitchen window. The valley at dawn is quiet, but not empty: the scent of wood smoke rises where bakers tend their morning fires, and the sky is clear enough to make out the outline of distant monasteries, built long ago from the same earth and stone.

Travelers once crossed these valleys, trading pigment and prayer at the city gates. The convent walls, painted with stories of saints and feasts, are lined with frescoes that have survived centuries—colors drawn from the ground itself, mixed with oil and egg, brushed in careful lines. Monks ground the earth into dust, learning to sift stone from root, then blending it until it moved like silk in their hands. They painted saints' robes, halos, and the gentle curves of wheat bending in the fields. Each brushstroke was a blessing, a declaration that what had lain hidden in the riverbank could find new life in light.

On festival days, banners are hung from the windows, cloth dyed by the hands of women who know the ways of boiling plants and earth in great pots. Bread is broken in the open air, warm and fragrant, its crust stained from hearthstone. Rain falls sometimes, sudden and fierce, but it is always the sun that returns, drying the cobbles and giving back the outlines of each step, each path through the market, each worn threshold.

There is steadiness in these objects: the bowl on the table, the wall that marks the garden, the horse that waits at the edge of a field. They offer a way to gather what was unseen—shadows recede, and the shape of things returns, ready for the hand. Today, take a moment to press your palm to sun-warmed brick or the smooth side of a bowl made from earth. Write a letter at a wooden desk, letting the lines settle one by one, as if outlining something long familiar. If you have a loaf of bread, break it with company; if you walk, do so where the outlines are clear, and the day is open before you.
