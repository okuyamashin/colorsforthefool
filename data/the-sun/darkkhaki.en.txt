THE SUN ― Upright

The child rides forward, arms thrown wide, atop a steady horse. Behind, the stone wall stands, its surface warm from long hours beneath a cloudless sky. On the other side, fields stretch into distance, where the stalks of summer wheat ripple in steady waves, each ear heavy, shaped and ripened by months of sunlight. There is no shadow beneath the hooves, only the defined shape of body and limb, as if the light itself makes every outline clear and close. The banner the child carries lifts in the gentle air, broad as the palm’s span, a flag stitched from woven flax by careful hands in the long afternoons following the first harvest.

Beneath the wheat, the earth is packed and dry, crumbly between the fingers—a memory of rain long since spent. In places, the ground is marked with faint tracks: the passage of a plow, or the brush of a sheepdog’s tail as it circles the flock. Along the field’s edge, tall grass leans over a shallow ditch, where a lizard scuttles for cover as the shadow of a broad-brimmed hat falls nearby. The farmer, knees dusty, leans on a spade whose wood carries the stories of ten seasons. His face is turned upward as he lifts a flask to his lips, and the sun’s light makes every line and callus stand out as if carved newly into being.

Past the fields, the low hills rise in gentle crests, their slopes dotted with olive trees, their trunks ancient and their leaves shimmering in the dry breeze. Farther on, the land opens into a plain where nomads once pitched tents woven from mountain goat hair, the fabric tough and wind-smoothed, colored by the herbs and muds of the riverbank. Here, the outlines of old stone paths are revealed each year as the grasses die back, showing the hand-worked slabs set by traders from forgotten cities, who once brought salt and barley to the market squares.

In the early hours, mist sometimes gathers in the hollows, but by midmorning it burns away, leaving the air ringing with the calls of crickets and the distant hum of threshing machines. The sky is wide, and the day’s work is clear. There is no guessing at the shape of things: the baskets in the granary, the clay pots for storing oil, the linen laid out to bleach in the full heat, all are visible and ready for use. The sun has lifted the veil from every hidden outline, and what was once obscure is now returned, familiar and within the reach of one’s own hand.

Today, you might walk in a field and notice the shapes made by wind in the grass. You may choose to sweep the dust from a favorite tool or polish the brass on a jug by the window, letting it catch the afternoon light. A simple meal of bread and olives, eaten outdoors with sleeves rolled past the elbow, will carry the memory of the land’s warmth into the body. When you bring order to a corner of your room or sit for a moment in the open air, you join the history of those who long ago trusted the return of light.
