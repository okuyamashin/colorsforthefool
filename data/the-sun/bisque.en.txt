THE SUN ― Reversed

The boy on the horse rides through a midday that has gone silent. Where the upright card promises clarity and play, the reversed Sun sears the path so thoroughly that all traces are bleached away. The fabric of the banner, meant to signal joy, is faded and stiff, the ground beneath the hooves hard as hammered earth. Every step through this landscape raises a small cloud as the soil crumbles into powder, each stride echoing the distance that heat makes between things.

Once, travelers crossing the high deserts of North Africa learned to wrap themselves in light linen, sheltering their bodies from the sun that dried even the air. They would find, now and then, a small oasis where the last threads of an old cloth caught on the reeds. Centuries later, in the kitchens of fishermen along the Atlantic coast of France, the shells of lobsters were cracked and their flesh simmered in a thin soup, evaporating slowly on a stove that radiated its own heat long after the fire beneath had disappeared. The soup would be ladled into bowls, the steam curling upward, making the air inside feel almost as dry as the road outside.

In the workshops of potters in Limoges, clay is pressed and shaped, hands leaving the shallowest of prints before the surface smooths out under the wheel. The kiln’s heat scorches away every trace of touch, and what remains is a bowl so pale it almost disappears into the light of noon. A seamstress, sitting at a wooden table not far from the open door, smooths out a length of new fabric, its weave fine but strong, the texture nearly as soft as ground almonds, which she grinds for afternoon pastries. She remembers hearing stories of a wind so dry it stole the notes from a piano, the sound fading into the hard air.

The road is made of dust and sand, pressed flat by the shoes of those who have walked it for years. There are paths along the Mediterranean where the stones are polished by centuries of feet, the track shimmering in the heat that bends the air above. On days when the wind turns, the horizon seems to vanish, and all that is left is the memory of water—sometimes in the form of soup cooling on a table, sometimes in the taste of shellfish on the tongue, sometimes in the feel of washed linen against the skin.

In this atmosphere, the Sun’s light is so strong that it steals shade, flattens the world, and leaves even the most familiar things looking distant. Yet it is possible to find comfort here: by carrying a handkerchief in your pocket, soft and light, to wipe the sweat from your brow; by preparing a bowl of soup, eaten slowly in the quiet of afternoon, letting warmth fill your body with something gentler than the heat outside; by pausing to touch a smooth ceramic plate, its coolness a small relief; by sitting with an old book in a patch of indirect sunlight and letting your mind wander to far-off beaches, where the wind still brings the scent of almonds and salt.
