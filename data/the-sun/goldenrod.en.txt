THE SUN ― Upright

A child rides bareback across a field, arms thrown wide, hair wind-tangled and sparkling. In the background, great sunflowers lean and nod, mapping the path of the day overhead. The stone wall that once seemed to close off the horizon is only a garden’s edge, and the sky beyond is brilliant and open. Light uncovers outlines that had blurred, showing the shape of things: the curve of the saddle, the shadows cast by blooms, the dust rising as hooves land. In the heart of the field, a cluster of tall plants stands swaying—stems tipped with clusters of tiny, star-like flowers, arching above the grass. They are named for the rods carried by ancient heralds, signals of news and promise, and for the way they catch and hold the strongest light before autumn.

In fable and history, these upright stems have been more than wildflowers. In the old country, shepherds wove the straightest stalks into fence markers, signposts pointing home. In kitchens, the dried blossoms were steeped in boiling water, served in cups to clear the voice and sweeten the air. Travelers, following winding roads along riverbanks, would pause to gather armfuls from ditches and hedgerows, a sign that the harvest was safe and the frost still distant. Even in times of war, when the world’s outlines seemed to shudder and disappear, bundles of these blooms were placed at thresholds—to mark a return, to welcome the light, to say “here” in the language of the land.

The same plant, across continents, stands in the wild corners of gardens and meadows, a promise of persistence. Bees cradle its blossoms through the waning days of summer, and the scent draws moths in the evening, when the last warmth lingers over the fields. Its roots hold the soil fast, preventing rain from washing everything away. Children, with arms full of its stems, weave them into crowns, pretending a day can be kept in a circle and worn on the head. The rods themselves, once dried, have been tied into bundles and hung in the rafters: a charm against illness, a reminder to seek light when days grow short. In towns along the great rivers, the same stems have been placed at corners of market stalls, a quiet blessing for fair trade and honest weights.

On a morning like this, with the outlines of things returning to the hand, one might gather a handful of those blossoms from the wayside and place them in a jar by the window, letting the whole room fill with their presence. An old teapot, unused for years, could be filled with hot water and the dried flowers steeped for tea, sipped slowly while reading stories of journeys and homecomings. If a stone or brick path leads to your door, tucking a stalk between the cracks is a silent way to mark the threshold, to say that the light has come and is welcome. Or go walking at the edge of fields or parks, looking for the tall, waving stems and tracing their path with your shadow, following the promise they hold in their name.
