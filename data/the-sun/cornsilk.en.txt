THE SUN ― Upright

On the surface of the card, a child rides a horse beneath a sky without clouds. Behind them, immense sunflowers lift their faces to the open air, and a pennant flutters, light as flour on a summer breeze. Everything is shaped in clarity, each thing outlined and given back its true dimensions. There is no mist, no confusion. The morning is already here; the world is present to the hand and the eye.

In the heart of rural midsummer, ears of maize are peeled back in wide fields, revealing their silken threads. These threads, almost weightless, slip between fingers, brushed away as cobs are prepared for the midday meal. They gather on the kitchen table, reminders of growth that required patience and sun. Long ago, hands in kitchens all over the world turned these silk strands into tea, meant to soothe and clear the body, or braided them into tokens for good luck. In the barn, after the harvest, these gleaming strands find their way into the lining of hats or the nests of swallows tucked high under the eaves.

In open markets and village fairs, small cakes are baked with meal ground from the kernels, their tops dusted with a powder that catches the light. These cakes are set in baskets lined with linen, carried out at dawn by those who rise early for the day’s work. The cloth beneath the cakes is woven tightly, spun from the first threads of the harvest, and catches the light that pours through a kitchen window. There is a sense of abundance, a recognition of labor that has come to fruition, and a gentle order in the arrangement of table, basket, cloth, and food.

Travelers in distant lands recall how the first rays of sun spill over the plains, touching fields where the grass is tall and wild, coated with the faintest dust after a night without dew. In the old stories, this was the hour when shapes returned to the world after night’s unshaped darkness; roads, fences, and the outlines of distant trees appeared again, faithful as old friends. Shepherds and farmers, awake before others, would watch the light move across harvested stubble, each stalk and stone regaining its own form. The return of these outlines signaled not only the end of the night but the restoration of certainty. In that moment, the world’s surface became navigable again, shadows no longer a trap, but something cast with purpose.

In some homes, cushions are stuffed with the filaments left from shucking, a softness that holds the memory of last summer’s heat. These cushions rest on window seats or on benches in sunrooms, places where a person might sit quietly, shelling peas or writing a letter. Curtains made of light cloth sway in the open window, letting in the breeze that carries the smell of distant fields. Somewhere, a child drops the slip of corn silk into a glass jar, watching it settle as if waiting for a message to appear.

Today one can peel the husk from an ear of corn, letting the silk fall into a bowl for later use, or steep it in hot water for a cup of tea that carries the whisper of fields. One can bake something simple—cornbread or muffins—and lay them in a basket on a cloth, sharing them with a friend. Or, in a quiet moment, sit in the early light, letting the outlines of things return, and notice what the sunlight restores to the room.
