THE SUN ― Reversed

In the card’s world, the child’s small feet are packed with dust. The banner that once danced in the winds now lies limp, brittle from drought, fibers worn thin by months of unclouded skies. The horse’s mane carries grit, and the path is lost in the glare, shorn of shadow and relief. Here, the light is not a gentle balm but a force that strips—rendering all surface, all edges; a place where roots struggle, and the air is empty of birdsong.

On a long, dry road in an old land, the remains of a villa stand. Its walls, forgotten for centuries, are patterned with the memory of coolness. There is the scent of flour from a forgotten bread oven, crumbs ground into the flagstones. The once-smooth table is now pitted and dry, the imprint of many meals shared under a relentless sun. Grapevines once stretched from post to post, but now their stalks are brittle, leaves fallen to dust. Along the corridor, a linen sheet—woven by hands long gone—remains folded, corners curled and yellowed by age, a silent witness to years of hard sunlight. Each thread holds a secret, spun in slower seasons.

In a merchant’s storeroom, a crate of old ivory buttons lies open. They were once fasteners on traveling coats, passed down when children grew tall. Nearby, a porcelain cup is chipped at the rim, teetering on the edge of an empty saucer, its glaze dulled from slow, sunlit afternoons. A handful of almonds, forgotten in a dish, are shrunken and pale. On the mantle sits a dried gourd, hung in the past for luck, now split and faded, its seeds rattling in the breeze from the door.

Travelers come to this place by the sea, hoping for the shade of olive trees. But the branches are sparse. The stones underfoot are chalky and hot. They move slowly, seeking a patch of relief. Above them, the sky is vast, unbroken. Sometimes dust rises when the wind comes, settling lightly on everything—a fine film over the jars, the shutters, the last of the summer wheat stored indoors. Step by step, the walk presses the old sandals thin, their soles scuffed by the endless track. Even the bread—baked that morning—crumbles quickly, its crust fragile, moisture gone to the day’s hot breath.

In weather like this, people learn the value of stillness. Water is measured out by the cup. A basket of eggs sits on a shelf, shells dulled with powdery bloom. The dog curls under the loom, nose tucked into paws, waiting for dusk. In the fields, the only sound is the soft click of cicadas, repeating their song as if to remind: this is an old story, and the sun has always been both bounty and burden.

Today, one might press out the creases from a linen cloth and let it cool beside a window, or open a family cookbook to bake bread with a heavy crust. It is a good day to mend a coat with a missing button, or to shell almonds one by one, eating them with a quiet patience.
