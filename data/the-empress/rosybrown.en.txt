THE EMPRESS ― Reversed

In the upright card, the Empress’s garden gleams with ripening fruit, wheat bending heavy at the edge of a stone path, and the faint sound of bees combing through blossoms. But reversed, her fields slip from her hands: stalks left to dry, the orchard’s apples bruised on the ground. The willow basket, once filled with bread and sweet preserves, sits empty on a scarred table. The harvest has been gathered by the wind and scattered by crows.

Long ago, shepherds in Anatolia watched their sheep along riverbanks, stamping their boots in the cool dust near earthen ovens. The ovens themselves, domed and rough, were built from the alluvial clay that lined the water’s edge, shaped by hands now forgotten. After the corn failed one spring, families pressed their palms to the fading warmth of these ovens, hoping for one more loaf from the last handful of barley. It was a time of weighed-down promise: shelter felt close, but not comforting. In their kitchens, daughters hauled out sacks of lentils and old root vegetables, the skin of the potatoes puckered and soft, and the jars of last autumn’s plums thickened into preserves that tasted faintly of rain. There was no feast—only remembrance of what could have been.

On the edge of these villages, the wild rose spread. Its stems grew tangled through abandoned fences, wrapping themselves around iron tools left half-buried in the earth. Each spring, petals fell onto an unplowed garden, settling among the fallen leaves. Even when the fields lay dormant, these faded blossoms clung to the memory of better weather. Nearby, a tradeswoman’s apron hung on a peg by the cold hearth, stained with flour and marked by sun and years. In quieter times, she would have worn it to carry gifts from the market—a wedge of cheese, a handful of dried figs—but now it waited, as if for a task that never came.

In northern towns, old cob cottages anchor the horizon, their walls built from a mixture of straw, mud, and sheep’s wool. During heavy rains, the roof that once sheltered laughter feels burdensome—a weight pressing down, trapping smoke and chill inside. The orchard, left unpruned, throws shadows across the stone well, and the scent of earth after a storm lingers like an old regret. On the window ledge, a battered clay mug sits forgotten beside a crust of bread never eaten.

Yet there is movement, even here. Someone kneeling in the garden gathers fallen rosehips for tea, boiling them slowly as dusk falls. Bread dough can be shaped and left to rise by the same hearth that once cooled, the scent carrying through quiet rooms. A letter, carefully written, can be slipped into a pocket apron before setting out to visit a neighbor. The iron garden fork, rusted but sturdy, can be lifted again to turn the soil, if only for thyme and chives along the path.
