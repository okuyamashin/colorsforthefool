THE EMPRESS ― Upright

In the garden of the Empress, wheat ripens under a mild sky, and the air carries the breath of wild roses. The seat beneath her is carved from wood and polished by the touch of many years, placed beside a gentle waterfall whose spray gathers in shallow pools. Plump fruit rests in clusters within easy reach, apples and pears overlooked by blackbirds. Around her, leaves tremble with the quiet assurance of life steadily unfolding. This is a place where abundance has already arrived. Every surface brims with nourishment—petals open wide, vines curl and reach, and the earth itself seems to lift up its offerings.

Among the roses, a curious flower grows in clusters—its name is shared with tailors and chefs, and with sailors who stitched canvas at sea. This is a flower known for old English ballads and hedgerow medicine, for its edible petals candied in sugar on cakes at May Day feasts. The same name lingers in the kitchen, where a soft cake bakes in round tins, rising as it did in Victorian windows, topped with cream and preserves. It appears as a confection in the tea room, a treat both delicate and sturdy, layered with the fruit that grows in the Empress’s domain.

Beyond the garden, the word is spoken in the language of the trade. Panels of cloth, smooth and durable, trimmed to make dresses for spring, bonnets for children, and sashes in the court. There was a time when these panels were cut by apprentices under lamplight, the needles threading through fabric as soft as the petals in the hedges outside. The same name carried through the streets, posted above shop doors and sewn into the linings of traveling trunks, a mark of both comfort and pride. In the market, vendors display these wares next to baskets of summer fruit and bundles of wildflowers, all sharing the same gentle sound.

Weather, too, borrows from the Empress’s world. Early in the morning, before the sun breaks through, mist drifts low over the fields, touching the wheat and the roses alike, leaving a dew that tastes faintly of orchard and wildflower nectar. Shepherds once named these mild dawns for the same flower, a promise of warmth to come after the long silence of night. Old tales tell of such mornings—quiet, nurturing, a cloak the earth wears before the day grows bright.

In this world, the Empress gathers abundance not by striving, but by tending. She harvests what is ripe, presses flowers into her book, and arranges fruit in bowls for travelers. To follow her, one might step outside to gather petals from the hedgerow, or slice a summer cake and share it at the kitchen table. Another might sew a soft band of cloth, threading needle through fabric while listening to the hush of rainfall against the glass. Or rise early, walking in the garden just as the morning mist begins to lift, and pause to breathe in the scent of wild roses and ripe fruit.
