THE EMPRESS ― Upright

Wheat rustles in the stillness, framing the seat of the Empress, who is surrounded by a garden brimming with riches already ripe and tangible. Roses bloom at her feet, their petals soft against the tapestry of living things. Fruit crowds the branches near her, pears and papayas heavy with sweetness, while a gentle waterfall glides over smooth stone, threading quiet music through the air. She does not reach for what might be missing; she rests in the knowledge that abundance is here, woven into each moment and every detail of the earth’s offering.

Consider the journey of the papaya. Once growing wild along the riverbanks of distant islands, it found its way onto trading ships, packed alongside spices and sugar. Merchants carried it between ports, its flavor a secret until sliced open for the first time in a new land. In warm kitchens, generations learned to transform this fruit, scooping out the flesh to whip with a splash of cream, or folding it into tarts and chilled desserts. This bowl, set upon a sunlit table, holds quiet luxury—the kind found in simplicity and care. It is not just food, but a memory of summer afternoons and the easy laughter of those gathered nearby.

The Empress’s garden is not a single place but a geography of comfort. Paths lined with wheat straw cushion footsteps, leading to corners where shade and light play against weathered stone. Sometimes, after the harvest, cloths are laid out on the grass for a midday meal, and baskets brim with what has been gathered. The air is gentle, touched by the mildness that settles after the rush of planting and before the hush of dusk. Even the weather shapes itself to ease: soft breezes drift through open windows, inviting in the scent of bread cooling on a wooden board, or the promise of rain somewhere far up the valley.

Within the house, abundance takes practical forms. A cherished bowl might be set out for mixing, its edge worn smooth by years of use. Linens, hand-washed and sun-dried, are folded into neat stacks, ready for the next meal. Traders once carried these textiles across continents, their patterns borrowed from one culture and gifted to another, until the fabric itself became a symbol of hospitality. The Empress’s world is one where each habit—cutting fruit, arranging a meal, sitting a moment longer to watch shifting light—signals gratitude for what is already offered.

Today, you might whip fruit with honey and serve it cool, inviting someone to share in the sweetness of midday. Spread a linen cloth, even on an ordinary table, to mark the moment as special. Stroll a path—real or imagined—through your own garden, or simply pause to listen to the water running in pipes or gutters, remembering the old rivers that first carried these gifts. Each act, however small, is an invitation to abundance, a way to recognize the fullness that is already here.
