THE EMPRESS ― Upright

Wheat bows in the field, heavy with promise, its heads full and shimmering as the wind passes across the plain. Roses climb a low wall, their petals opening in the hush of morning, while a kitchen garden yields its fruits in quiet abundance: plump tomatoes and eggplants, sturdy beans, and the curling tendrils of cucumbers. The Empress sits within this bounty, her seat formed from living branches, woven with the patience of seasons. Nearby, water slips from stone to stone, gathering in a cool basin before feeding the roots of everything she governs. She is surrounded not by emptiness, but by the evidence of growth and labor come to gentle fruition.

A small tree stands at the edge of the garden. Its slender leaves flicker in the sunlight, silvery on one side, muted on the other. Its fruit is smooth and firm, gathered by hands that know each limb and knot, and pressed to release their oil, which fills lamps and coats bread with a taste both ancient and sustaining. These trees watch over terraces carved into the Mediterranean hillsides, their twisted trunks testimony to centuries of tending. In story and in peace, a branch held by a dove meant the storm had passed; the earth was ready again for settling and cultivation. Crowns were shaped from their leaves for poets and athletes, a mark of honor and gentleness at once.

In a summer kitchen, jars line a wooden shelf. Some are packed with brine, holding the same small fruits, their flavor deepened by time and salt. A hand reaches for a spoon and then a bowl, dressing crisp greens with oil pressed from those fruits, the scent as fresh as the garden outside. A mother folds cloths and places them in a basket, each one trimmed with a border that traces the leaf’s simple shape, passed down through hands that found comfort in embroidery during long, quiet evenings. Nearby, a mortar and pestle—smooth from long use—crushes nuts and herbs, forming a paste that carries the memory of warm afternoons and soft breezes across tiled courtyards.

In old stories, these trees stood as markers of peace, planted at the heart of sacred groves and at the edges of villages. They withstood drought and storm, their roots digging deep into rocky soil, leaves flickering through years of both celebration and hardship. Their wood shaped sturdy ladles and bowls, tools for sharing and serving food at family tables. Light from a lamp fed by their oil flickered on stone walls as stories drifted through kitchens and gardens, mingling with the hum of insects and the distant rush of water.

Today, a person might press a sprig of these leaves into a book, sending the scent of distant hills into the quiet of their room. They might slow their pace by tasting oil on fresh bread, letting the flavor recall days of peace and plenty. Sitting beneath the shade of a tree, they could write a letter or a poem, letting thoughts grow as roots and branches do—steadily, without hurry. Or, in the evening, they might polish a wooden spoon until it shines, remembering all the hands that have shaped, served, and nourished.
