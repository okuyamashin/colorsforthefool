THE HIGH PRIESTESS ― Reversed

In the quiet chamber behind the veil, the High Priestess sits with her scroll shut tight, eyes averted from the water’s silent face. The moon’s light slips away from her lap, leaving the linen untouched and the air heavy with what is withheld. Outside, the world waits, but all the signs—the pomegranate cloth, the twin pillars—have grown silent. There is no passage inward. There is only the hush of a secret that does not budge, a gentle yet unmoving wall.

Once, the priestess would have welcomed the hour when dusk deepens into the kind of night that gives the garden’s violets their secretive scent. She would have trailed her hands through them, the petals gathered in shadow under the willow tree, their faces closed, breathing with the soil’s slow memory. Now, she sits still, her feet bare on a woven rug that smells faintly of lavender, the patterns on it echoing distant hills and rivers seen only in dreams. She will not read from her book of mysteries. The answers keep themselves, as if wrapped in a thick scarf worn by merchants from Kashan, spun from the silk of distant worms, dyed in the heart of flowers whose names are spoken only in the market’s hush.

The scroll remains unopened, its wax seal unbroken, and the river below the temple does not mirror the sky. Instead, it runs deep and hidden, silent beneath a grove of irises left untended by the priests. The path to its edge is barred by high grass, dew clinging to it long after the sun has risen. The air is close, heavy with a faint tang like that of ripe Concord grapes forgotten in a bowl, skins beginning to wrinkle. The secret waits, but the secret also withholds; it is not ready to be made into wine or ink.

Somewhere in the distance, there is a mountain whose slopes are covered in wild rosemary. In the early morning, before the mist burns off, shepherds move their flocks through the heather, knowing that neither sheep nor stone will reveal what lies beneath the root. In old times, the dye-makers of Tyre would boil down the petals of rare, delicate blooms to stain the monarch’s cloak. The color was rare because the flower hid itself, growing only where no foot had trod, on cliffs where the wind speaks in riddles. The priestess, when she does speak, does so in the voice of these mountains and stones; now, she is silent, the scroll heavy in her lap, the wind still.

If you find yourself waiting for the answer that does not come, you can gather violets from the edge of your garden and press them between the pages of a book you have not yet read. You might steep dried lavender in a ceramic cup and drink it before the dawn, listening for the sound of water in the pipes. You could wrap yourself in a scarf with patterns of distant hills, letting it remind you of places where secrets sleep quietly in the earth. If you have a bowl of grapes, eat them slowly, one by one, letting their tang remind you that some sweetness ripens in shadow.
