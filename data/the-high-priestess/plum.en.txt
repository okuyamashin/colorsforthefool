THE HIGH PRIESTESS ― Upright

Between the pillars, the High Priestess sits, half shadow and half promise. The veil behind her is thickly woven, marked with pomegranates, and nothing passes through but the faintest whisper of breeze. On her lap rests a scroll, its surface closed to all but the initiated. Beneath her throne, water lies perfectly still, mirroring the untouched face of a secluded pond at dawn. This is not the rushing stream of speech but the unmoving depth of waiting silence, the moment before a secret is told.

Beyond this chamber, in gardens long tended by careful hands, trees stand with their branches heavy. The fruit they carry is dusk-dark, oval and cool to touch. These trees have belonged to old orchards in far valleys, to the monastic groves of ancient lands where silence is a rule rather than a habit. Some say that in the highlands of Asia, the first of these trees were planted near temples, their roots deep in stone and loam, their branches tracing the seasons while generations passed beneath their shade. In legend, one such tree grew near a shrine where seekers would pin slips of folded paper to the bark, each bearing a question that could not yet be spoken aloud. The fruit, when gathered, was always saved for the quietest ceremonies.

Inside kitchens at dusk, the fruit is halved with a careful hand and its pit removed. The flesh is placed in bowls beside teaspoons and old recipes written by grandmothers who whispered as they measured. Syrup made from this fruit thickens in a pot, sending up a faint cloud of sweetness that fills the small room. Its scent lingers on the wooden spoon, as if it remembers every summer spent in the orchard. Sometimes, a drop is added to a cup of tea, deepening its flavor, or spooned over a slice of bread to mark the end of a silent afternoon. In the corner, a scarf hangs over the back of a kitchen chair, woven from the softest of threads and dyed with tinctures made from the same fruit’s skin. As it catches the evening light, it seems to hold something back—a story that is not yet ready to be told.

Long ago, merchants carried baskets of this fruit in caravans that crossed cold steppes and river valleys, bound for distant markets. Wrapped in woven cloth, the fruit survived months of travel, a promise of sweetness at a journey’s end. In the markets themselves, traders offered it as both food and medicine, a remedy for quiet sorrow or sleepless nights. Healers brewed its dried blossoms with boiling water, pouring the infusion into cups carved from bone or wood. The taste was gentle, but the effect was slow and certain, as if drawing a curtain softly over restless dreams. In other places, the branches were burned in winter stoves, filling homes with a subtle fragrance on evenings when the snow drifted high.

On a day like today, you might steep slices of the fruit in warm water and sip the infusion while reading someone else’s story, or press the pit into soil and trust that patience will bring roots. You could wrap yourself in a scarf made from its fibers and wear it while writing a letter you do not intend to send. Or, you might visit an old garden, stand quietly beneath a tree, and listen for what the leaves do not say.
