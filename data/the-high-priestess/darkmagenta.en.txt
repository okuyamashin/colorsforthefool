THE HIGH PRIESTESS ― Reversed

The High Priestess sits between the stone pillars, but today her gaze is fixed on the sealed scroll in her lap. The familiar crescent at her feet is dry, a silent arch over ground where the river once whispered. The tapestry behind her, embroidered with vines, seems thicker, as if growing to muffle the hidden doorway. In the air, absence hangs heavier than incense. Whatever was once passed quietly from intuition to heart is now caught behind brick and mortar, held by hands unwilling to surrender the secret.

In the ancient cities of the Zagros foothills, women wore robes dyed with extract from the marrow of mountain flowers, a shade deep enough to swallow sunlight, the same hue as the ink reserved for forbidden names in temple registries. That pigment was rare, pressed into velvet for heavy curtains drawn across winter windows, so that the outside world could not peer in. Scholars in forgotten libraries bound their journals in goatskin tanned with the same tincture, pages bristling with questions they dared not answer aloud. The secret, when kept too long, became a thing itself—a lock, a border, a wall of thick velvet drapery.

Along the edge of the steppe, wild foxgloves bloom after the first thunderstorm, their bells hanging, heavy and unreadable, over the earth where old wells are sealed for the season. No one draws from them; the water keeps its stories to itself. Long ago, herbalists in small villages crushed those petals for their medicine but kept the recipe hidden, trusting only the closed cupboard and the silence of dusk to hold the knowledge safe. In the cold, clear nights, a distant aurora sometimes flickered with the same shadowy intensity above the horizon, a phenomenon so rare that shepherds called it “the silent messenger.” Yet, even the sky would not decipher its meaning.

Inside, the hearth is cool. A bowl of ripened plums, mottled and dark as evening storms, sits untouched on the windowsill. The kitchen’s mortar holds the last remains of a spice, once thought to coax open the mind’s door, now ground into powder and kept in a tightly-stoppered jar. The house is quiet, the family’s voices careful not to spill into the rooms where secrets might grow wings. Someone smooths the heavy coverlet on the bed, woven in a pattern passed from mother to daughter, each thread a question never quite asked. Outside, a sudden squall sends petals tumbling from the trellis, their fall unnoticed except by the lone cat watching from beneath an overturned pot.

It is easy, today, to carry something sealed—a letter unsent, a question unspoken, a memory pressed between pages of a diary. In the hush of morning, brew tea from a jar once hidden in the back of the pantry, letting its aroma rise and then fade quietly. Write a riddle on the underside of a stone and set it at the garden’s edge, where only the bravest visitor might turn it over. Mend the heirloom scarf with stitches close and small, the thread passing through fabric as silent as the High Priestess’s guarded glance. Or simply dust the heavy curtains, letting your hand remember the weight of what remains unseen.
