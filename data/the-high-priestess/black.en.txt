THE HIGH PRIESTESS ― Reversed

The temple is silent. Curtains hang thick over the entrance, each fold drinking in the last traces of light. The scroll in the High Priestess’s lap remains closed, edges pressed together so tightly that not even a single letter escapes. Beneath her seat, the crescent moon is hidden beneath a layer of old soot, a shadow in a world of stone pillars and midnight incense. In the garden outside the sanctuary, a pool sits stagnant and opaque, its surface unbroken. The water, once reflective, refuses to yield any vision. Roots twist under the soil, threading through beds of coal and nightshade, tunneling deeper where sunlight cannot follow.

There is an old myth that tells of a city surrounded by walls made from obsidian, smooth and impenetrable. Travelers who arrive at its gates find them sealed, the surface cold beneath their hands, the entrance without a seam. In that city, secrets are not shared; they settle into the basalt streets and gather in the unlit corners of warehouses where tea chests sit unopened, spices are locked in iron jars, and stories fade before they’re spoken. Above, the sky stretches, heavy with the threat of a coming storm. Crows gather on roof beams, sounding out warnings no one hears, feathers gleaming in the absence of dawn. Smoke rises from distant chimneys, coiling into the air and dissolving before it can signal the hearths below.

In the world of work, smiths are bent over an anvil, hammering iron that will never catch a glint, forging locks and hasps that keep safes and diaries sealed. Their hands, marked with soot, are careful as they fit each mechanism, ensuring that what is stored inside will remain untouched. Miners descend into the earth, lamps shuttered, following seams of anthracite through tunnels where each step is a negotiation with silence. The lampblack that lines their palms and faces is the mark of what cannot be washed away easily: the hidden, the buried, the unspoken.

In the kitchen, lentils simmer in a heavy cast iron pot, their scent rising in slow, earthy waves. A woman grinds pepper with a mortar, the tiny orbs cracking open but never revealing their hearts. Bread crusts bake to a darkness that speaks of patience and withheld heat. The table is set with stoneware, plates and cups holding what is eaten but never shown in full light. In a corner, a spool of silk waits, spun from the work of night moths, as soft and secretive as the promises made between midnight and morning.

The wind picks up, carrying the sound of thunder. The windows rattle; the shutters hold. Rain falls, but the gutters are choked with leaf-mulch, so the rivulets gather at the threshold, pooling like ink. There are days when the world feels like a cellar, cool and airless, with shelves lined by jars whose contents are masked by age. In these moments, the invitation is not to break through but to move quietly within, aware of the weight and potential behind every lid, every locked door.

Today, a person may choose to sit beside an unlit hearth and listen for what is not said. They might write a secret in a notebook and tuck it between the pages of an old atlas, or walk beneath a power line at dusk, counting the silhouettes of birds overhead. They could polish an iron key until it gleams, then leave it where no one will find it, remembering that some mysteries are meant to be kept.
