THE HIGH PRIESTESS ― Upright

Two stone pillars rise in the quiet, framing a passage that leads not into a chamber but toward a veil woven so finely it seems to still the very air. The High Priestess sits between them, her hands resting on a closed scroll that balances across her lap, its title turned inward. Behind her, water pools in silent reserve, unmoving, reflecting the world as it is and as it might be, though no one is allowed to peer too deeply. The world of secrets does not reveal itself at a glance; it prefers the hush of dawn and the unbroken surface of forgotten wells.

Beyond the veil, where knowledge becomes mist, the world is filled with objects that, like the Priestess, conceal more than they show. In the heart of a small English village, a china cabinet stands in a parlor. Inside, a teacup and saucer, their rims trimmed with a memory of some long-ago spring, rest beside a stack of dinner plates—each one a relic from a time when tea was an afternoon sacrament and conversations were measured by the gentle clink of porcelain. These objects, glazed with stories and carrying the faintest fragrance of lavender soap, wait for hands that remember the proper way to pour and serve.

In the garden, the morning stretches itself over the land, and there, among the foxgloves and the low stone wall, blooms a delicate cluster of forget-me-nots. Their petals hold the coolness of a clouded sky after rain, almost seeming to shimmer beneath the dew. These small flowers are often tucked into letters or pressed between the pages of a diary, as if to seal a promise or preserve a moment unspoken.

Far off, on the English Channel, the weather sometimes turns to a particular gentleness. Fishermen at dawn look up and see the mist rolling in, the kind that softens the edges of everything and leaves only the outline of sails and lighthouse in the distance. On such mornings, the world holds its breath. The sea, for a time, becomes as silent as the water behind the High Priestess. Even the gulls call more softly, their wings tracing the horizon between what is known and what is hidden by the fog.

In the old city’s heart, lace-makers gather by the window, their fingers moving in steady rhythm. The cloth they produce catches the day’s first light, intricate patterns forming on the bobbins, each thread crossing over the next with unsaid intent. These veils are not for display. They are kept in cedar-lined chests, brought out only on rare occasions, when tradition asks for something unspoken to be honored.

On a calm morning, a person might pause to dust off a treasured cup from the cabinet and enjoy a solitary tea. Another could pluck a single forget-me-not and tuck it between the pages of a favorite book, a quiet act of memory. If the day begins with a fog so fine it makes the rooftops vanish, walking through it becomes a meditation on what is here and what remains hidden. At dusk, one might open a box of old lace and let the patterns play across their fingertips, contemplating what is preserved and what is kept secret.
