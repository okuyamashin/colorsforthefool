THE LOVERS ― Reversed

In the valley beneath the willow branches, two figures stand with their hands almost touching, the space between them a soft hush. Above, clouds drift, their undersides bearing the weight of rain not yet fallen. The Lovers in this moment are not bound by union, but suspended by hesitation. Their words are stones in their mouths, smooth and hard, never skipping far across the surface. The garden around them is rich with mulberry trees, their fruit ripe at dusk, staining the fingers of those who dare to gather. Yet neither bends to taste, content to watch the fruit fall, undisturbed.

In ancient kitchens, the mortar stands with its pestle, both carved from basalt, heavy and opaque. Here, families have ground spices in the quiet before dawn, the sound echoing in rooms where secrets gather in corners. In these moments, the house breathes in slow, measured rhythms, absorbing the warmth of a stove that burns low through the longest hours. Coal, stacked in baskets by the door, promises heat but remains untouched in the hearth, waiting for the decision to kindle it. So too do the Lovers wait, standing by their own unlit fire.

On distant roads, traders once traveled in caravans beneath the new moon. Above them, the sky pressed close, and the world seemed endless. Their camels bore lacquered chests filled with ink sticks, prized for their depth and permanence. Scribes waited in lamplight for these treasures, longing to set promises and grievances to parchment, words meant to bridge distance yet held back by trembling hands. Inkpots stood ready, but often the brush hovered, the first stroke never made. A letter unwritten, a contract unsigned, a vow spoken only in the mind—these are the forms indecision takes as night stretches on.

Beyond the city, volcanic cliffs rise above the surf, their faces streaked with glassy veins. In these shadows, roots of the licorice plant push deep into concealed soils, drawing strength from what most cannot see. Here, old stories say, lovers parted by circumstance carved their names into obsidian shards, secrets sealed within the earth. The stone itself is sharper than memory, able to cut or protect, depending on the hand that holds it. For some, these fragments are tokens tucked away in a cedar box, never to be revealed; for others, they are talismans worn at the throat, the edge a reminder of choices not made.

In weather lore, the hush before a storm is known as the crow’s hour, when even the wind holds its breath. Windows are shuttered, doors bolted, and in the narrow alleys, stray cats curl against chimney pots, their silhouettes blending into the night. The world gathers itself, poised on the threshold of movement, yet motionless. In such a pause, lovers and strangers alike listen for what comes next, uncertain if the rain will fall or pass by unspoken.

Tonight, one might pull the basalt mortar from the shelf, grinding anise seeds with care, letting the rhythm of the pestle smooth out tangled thoughts. In the half-light, a letter could be started, inked with quiet intent though not yet sent. Or, walking in the garden at dusk, fallen mulberries might be gathered in a cloth, hands stained for a while by their silent harvest. When the wind rises, a window can be opened just enough to catch the scent of distant rain, promising change whether one is ready or not.
