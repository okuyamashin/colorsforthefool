THE LOVERS ― Upright

Two figures stand in a garden, sunlight falling through the leaves and onto a patch of soft ground. The air is warm, gentle, and ripe with a sense of possibility. In this place, the choice to reach toward one another is not a matter of fate, but of presence and attention: hands outstretched, their link is forged in the act of choosing, again and again, in quiet moments as well as in grand ones. The garden around them is alive, stones underfoot worn smooth by generations’ passage, borders defined by old wooden trellises, low walls covered with creeping vines, and the scent of ripe fruit nearby. The world, in this moment, holds them, but the true beginning is in the glance shared and the step taken forward.

A memory of coasts lies behind them, where the water’s edge bears the trace of ancient trade. In certain coves, divers draw from beneath the waves small branching treasures, once living, now hardened by their time in the sea. They are polished and shaped into beads and pendants, tokens exchanged between lovers as a sign of promise—gifts carried over centuries from the Mediterranean to the shores of distant islands. These tokens are kept in carved boxes on bedside tables, sometimes held in the palm while making a vow. In some stories, a fisherman’s daughter wore such a piece while waiting at dusk, searching the horizon for her beloved’s return. In others, a merchant kept it near the heart, believing it would bind the fates of those separated by long journeys.

Elsewhere, in markets where the trade winds blow in from the east, these sea-formed branches are set out on cloth, beside fruits whose flesh is sweet and tangy—the kind one eats in slices, juices staining the fingers, sticky and fragrant. In kitchens, cooks grind them into sauces to serve on cool evenings in late summer, the taste lingering on the tongue. Children string the small treasures on thread, making simple necklaces or bracelets, and the act itself becomes a ritual of affection, a gift offered to a friend or sibling. On festival days, these adornments catch the light, a quiet signal of belonging and care.

Weather changes and so do the seasons, but in certain places, just before the rains arrive, the sky seems to burn low against the land, bathing the world in a strange, luminous calm. It is the hour for decisions, for speaking plainly, for stepping under shelter together or braving a walk through the first drops of warm rain. Windows glow from within, lanterns are lit, and neighbors share small dishes at dusk, plates filled with the fruits and sauces of earlier markets, the memory of salt air and distant shores still clinging to the meal. It is a time for honest choices, for reaching across the table or the threshold and saying yes, again, to the bonds that matter.

Today, a person might sit near a window and string a small gift for someone they cherish, or set out a dish of sweet fruit to share at supper. They might wear a token from the sea—a bead, a pendant—against the skin, as a quiet promise. Or, at the edge of a summer storm, they might simply walk into the garden, taking the hand of a companion, listening for the hush that comes just before the rain.
