THE LOVERS ― Reversed

Between the two figures stands the stone of silence, old as the riverbed, worn smooth by years of water passing and then receding, leaving the strata visible for both to see but not name. They stand, not touching, under a sky that forgets the sun, wrapped in the hush of a morning that has not decided whether to rain or clear. The path they once traced together is the lane behind a row of houses where the mist lingers even after dawn, where the air is thick with the scent of wet pavement and unfinished conversations. Words begin in the chest and fade at the lips, swallowed by the hush that blankets everything—the clouds hanging low, the unbending rows of pigeons, the rooftops gathering the memory of last night’s drizzle.

In the old city, bridges arch over canals not yet claimed by summer’s reflection, their surfaces marked by the boots of travelers who paused but did not stay. Here, lovers once met at the hour when the bakery’s shutters rattled open, and the scent of rye bread drifted with the chill. Now, two stand by the railing, hands buried in the pockets of their wool coats, looking at the water that carries neither their faces nor their voices back to them. The canal holds only the dull glint of cast iron, the steel of forgotten bicycles chained to railings, the dull gleam of river stones sifting the current. The world stretches outward, indistinct, as though waiting for a gesture that never arrives.

In the myth of Selene and Endymion, night falls as a blanket, gentle and thick, drawing the horizon close. Yet when longing is not met with choice, night remains unbroken, a low-lying fog at the edge of the fields, softening the outlines of every tree and hedge until nothing is quite distinct. The lovers become silhouettes moving through the morning mist of the moors, where sheep huddle against the wind and everything is muted—wool, stone walls, and the far-off call of the shepherd’s whistle. There is a comfort in the familiar weight of the cobbler’s apron, in the anvil’s cool surface, in the hush of rain against slate roofs. The world of tasks and trades—spinning, mending, shaping—offers the certainty of routine amid uncertainty of heart.

On the table inside, beside the hearth, sits an old pewter mug, handle bent from years of use, holding the steeped leaves of a tea grown where the clouds never lift. The bread from the morning, torn but uneaten, rests beside a knife whose blade shines only where it has worn thin. The fire is banked with ash, and the cat curls atop a folded wool blanket, the hush of its breath blending with the clock’s gentle tick—steady, persistent, but never rushing. All is waiting. All is the pause before speech, the space between gesture and reply.

Today, walk the lane after rain and listen for the hush that follows every footfall on damp stone. Wrap yourself in a woolen scarf or rest with a book beneath the slow turning of the sky outside your window. Hold a mug warmed by strong tea or coffee, feeling its surface fit your hands. Carry a pebble from the riverbank in your coat pocket, a small reminder of silence and presence.
