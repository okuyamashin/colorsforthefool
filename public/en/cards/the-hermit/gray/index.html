Gray

隠者 · グレイ

Reversed · #808080

At the top of the mountain, the lamp is cold in the Hermit's hand. The road behind is a long sweep of stone, broken by patches of snow that do not melt and boulders crusted with lichen. The sky presses down, a ceiling of low, unbroken cloud, trapping the mountain in a world without sun. All around, fog curls and thickens, swallowing the edges of every rock and path. The Hermit, turned inward, has let the fire die and no longer calls across the gulf. He is wrapped in a coarse blanket, the fibers rough and flecked with the memory of sheep that once grazed in high pastures.

Down the slope, the world is motionless. Rain falls in quiet sheets, turning the scree into sludge and marking the surface of a tarn that rarely ripples. In the village, smoke rises from chimneys, dissolving into the damp air above slate roofs. The villagers pass beneath, heads bowed beneath the drizzle, their coats beaded with water. Stacked against doorways, river stones—once gathered to mark a boundary—glisten after the night’s storm. Here, the Hermit’s refusal to descend becomes the hush that settles after distant thunder: a spell of suspended movement, a silence broken only by the drip from the eaves and the steady tick of the clock on the mantelpiece.

Once, the Hermit’s lamp drew wanderers up the stony path. Now, with its flame forgotten, the only company is the slow growth of moss on the ledges, the drift of mist among the cairns. In the garden below, the cabbages swell under the wet weight of an overcast morning, leaves rimmed with dew. The road to the world is still there, but the Hermit has chosen the company of granite, the taste of cold porridge, and the scrape of a whetstone across the blade of a knife dulled by solitude. Each thing in this mountain solitude is practical and enduring: the pewter bowl that holds yesterday’s bread, the river pebbles used to anchor fishing lines, the patch of lavender gone to seed.

The mountain is not empty, only quiet. The muffled bells from distant sheep, the echo of an axe on damp wood, and the roll of thunder far away all gather in the air. The Hermit’s lantern, once a beacon, is now tucked away in a trunk with old nails and a weathered field journal. In this retreat, the world’s color comes from stone and cloud, from the bark of a willow after rain, from the battered tin pail set on the doorstep to catch runoff. There is no rush to return, no call to the firelight of the village. Solitude has become the hard path chosen, the mountain’s lonesome summit, the unlit lamp.

Today, you might sit quietly beside a slate windowsill, tracing the shapes of raindrops with your finger. You could cook a simple meal of mushrooms and barley, letting the steam rise as you breathe in the scent of earth and stone. You might polish a pewter cup or sharpen a favorite tool, listening to the soft scrape and finding comfort in the slow, steady work. Or, you could walk out beneath a low sky, letting the hush of fog and the soft drip of water shape your thoughts into something still.
