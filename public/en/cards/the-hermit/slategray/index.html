Draw a card / THE HERMIT

SlateGray

Reversed · #708090

On the windswept ridge, the lamp is dark, and the mountain air pricks with stillness. The Hermit, shadowed under the cowl, has chosen not to descend. Far from the warmth of fires, stone underfoot yields nothing but chill, and clouds press close, obscuring paths he once knew. This is not the solitude of wisdom-seeking, but the high ground of refusal. The world below, bustling with voices and lamplight, is shut away by an iron will. Silence gathers like the moss that takes root on old split roof tiles, patient and unmoving.

Here, the air carries the scent of slate split by the quarrier’s chisel—sharp, mineral, and old as the hills themselves. Such stones have patched the rooftops of North Wales for centuries, shielding families from rain that falls day and night, tapping out long, aimless songs on the shingles. The Hermit’s mountain is built from this, layered and ancient, each slab laid by the hand of time or the work of men who know the value of shelter but never linger to admire the view. In the valleys below, stacks of roofing tiles wait beside stone cottages, their edges rough but true. Sometimes, a piece cracks under weight or weather, and the fragments are swept away, lost to the weeds. These are the leavings of protection—shards that once kept out the cold but now lie scattered, unclaimed.

A mist rolls in, heavy as the sky before a spring rain, and the world narrows. Fog muffles every sound, and the mountain’s profile becomes a memory, blurred and uncertain. There are mornings in the north country when sheep fade into the distance, their shapes indistinct along the slate fences. In places like this, weather is not simply background: it closes in, becoming the whole of the world. Old railway tracks snake through the landscape, their metal rails set into gravel quarried from the same hard rock. The trains that pass are ghosts—coal once carried here, now silence and lingering dust.

Inside the Hermit’s hut, a chimney built of the same layered stone rises above the roof, its mortar crumbling, its top chipped by winter’s freeze. Inside, a kettle sits on the hearth, battered from years of use, rim lined with the residue of boiled water and strong tea. There is a weight in such objects, the hush of things left behind by hands grown tired. Spoons with dulled handles rest in chipped mugs. Outside, a spade leans against the wall, its blade pitted by decades of soil and rain. Every tool here is plain, serviceable, and worn by repetition—built to last, but never quite belonging to anyone.

To walk the Hermit's road today, one could pour water from the kettle, letting the steam drift as quietly as rising fog. Mending a broken tile on the roof, however small, becomes a gesture of care in the face of isolation. Turning the spade in the earth, even for a single plant, answers the silent mountain with intention. In the house, sweeping the floor clears a path that nobody else may tread, but still, the act is its own return.
