Draw a card / THE HERMIT

Gold

Upright · #FFD700

A figure stands on a high mountain pass, staff in hand, with a lantern held close to his chest. The path underfoot is narrow, the wind is sharp, and the world below is hushed. What shines in the lamp is not meant to blaze outward, but to light a small, certain circle around the traveler’s feet. The Hermit does not beckon crowds or call across valleys; he holds a treasure meant for the quiet, for the moment between one breath and the next.

Somewhere, in the earth's deep chambers, a metal is found in veins thinner than a candle’s flame. It is wrested out of hard stone, washed and sifted by hand, and, in ancient days, kept close to the heart of kings and queens. Pharaohs bent over their open coffers and found the promise of endurance in these rare coins and rings. A wedding band, slipped onto a finger in the hush of a church, carries the same weight: a mark of something that does not tarnish in the air, even as decades pass. In the Hermit’s solitude, such an object is not a sign of wealth but of vow and purpose, a private signal of values kept unwavering when the world is stormy.

On the high slopes where the wind cuts through, a stalk of wheat stands alone, its ripened kernels bending under their own fullness. In the fields at harvest time, reapers gather these heads and thresh them, and what is left behind catches the last rays of the afternoon. In ancient myth, Midas touched what he loved, and it became something lasting, but also lonely—each thing set apart from the world. The Hermit’s lamp, like Midas’s touch, is a reminder that not all riches are for display; some are meant to illuminate a single path, to be carried in silence.

In old cities, traders once traveled with pouches sewn inside their cloaks, stitched by hand, the contents carefully guarded during long nights on the road. The coins inside bought safe passage, a meal at a quiet inn, or the means to send a word home. In monasteries, illuminated manuscripts glowed with careful filigree, each detail painstaking and reserved for those who lingered over the page. A chalice passed from hand to hand in a small circle before dawn, the surface dulled by touch, held a story of many lives distilled into one vessel. These are not the treasures of noise, but of solitude: objects meant for the small radius of lamplight, for the one who keeps watch while the world sleeps.

On certain late afternoons, the sky breaks open and the fields are struck with a sudden gleam, as if the day itself holds its breath. There are moments when a person stands at the window and the world beyond the glass seems briefly transformed, each blade of straw or edge of leaf radiant before the sun dips away. In those minutes, the quiet work of making bread, mending a ring, or opening a family locket becomes a ritual of patience and care. The Hermit’s lesson is to tend what is near, to carry the lamp with measured steps, and to prize the warmth that comes from within.

Today, a person can polish a cherished ring, bake bread with patient hands, or bring out an old family cup and turn it slowly in the palm, remembering its stories. One might choose to walk a silent path in the afternoon, keeping company with only the sound of footsteps and the steady comfort of something small and enduring in the pocket.
