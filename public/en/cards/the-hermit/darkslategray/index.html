Draw a card / THE HERMIT

DarkSlateGray

Reversed · #2F4F4F

Once, the Hermit stood atop the mountain, holding his lantern aloft and letting it throw a small, certain light onto the swirling air and rocks. Now, the flame is shuttered, withheld. Here, on the wind-scoured peak, he sits among stones cold as old iron, shrouded under a sky the color of slate roofs after rain. He folds himself into solitude, turning away from the paths below, and the lamp is just a weight in his hand. Refusal of people becomes the road—no footsteps sound on the gravel, nothing but the empty call of crows and the restless sigh of weather overhead.

Down in the world, walls of quarried slate mark the border of forgotten gardens. Roofs of lost abbeys, their tiles chipped and battered by years, glimmer when the mist coils over them. Fences made of stacked stone snake across moorland, lichen gripping their rough faces, dividing nothing from nothing. In early morning, fishermen on the northern coast stare out to sea, their boats bobbing where the sky blends into the water, and the horizon is a single flat line, indistinct, neither beginning nor end. The mountain, when seen from the village, is a presence, its shoulders hunched inward, its shadow pressing out the last warmth of day.

Miners once followed seams beneath the earth, chipping through the compressed bones of mountains to carve out slabs for rooftops and floors. Their hands bore marks of the work; dust settled on their clothes, and when they returned to their cottages at dusk, the air seemed to cling to them, a reminder of the deep veins they followed in silence. The trade endures in memory, in piles of discarded tiles behind old workshops, in ledgers filled with names and dates that no one now recalls. When children run through the abandoned quarries, their laughter is muffled, as if the stones themselves swallow the sound.

In the kitchen, a mortar carved from dense rock sits by the hearth, its surface mottled by years of grinding herbs and salt. In the garden, leaves of sage, soft to the touch, gather dew that never quite dries, always holding on to the grayness of dawn. The Hermit might have known these things, once—before he turned his back, before he let the lantern die, before he claimed the mountain as his only companion. Now, he is a figure cast in the same material as old well covers and window sills, enduring but inert, a thing shaped by absence.

On certain evenings, weather closes in with a wet, heavy mist, muffling all outlines, so that the world itself seems to pause and hold its breath. The air is thick, the ground slick, and the paths up the mountain vanish, swallowed by the hush. Those who dwell below light their stoves and press their palms to the stones lining the fireplace, comforted by the same substance that, on the mountain, keeps the Hermit company in his chosen isolation.

You might close the day by stacking flat stones as a border for your own patch of earth, or by carrying a piece of slate in your pocket as a reminder to hold silence gently. Brew sage tea and breathe the steam in the quiet of early evening. If you walk beneath a sky low and laden with mist, let the hush settle inside you, even for a moment.
