Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / THE DEVIL

Black

Upright · #000000

A pedestal, a torch, chains that come loose when slackened: these are the pillars of the card, each one a real thing in the world, each one a household item or an artifact of place. The pedestal stands in a forgotten gallery, hewn of basalt, cool to the touch and satiny beneath the hand. On its surface rests a candlestick once found in a miner’s cottage, the kind that bears the carbon stains of years spent beside coal fires and under sooty rafters. The chains, once drawn taut, are the iron shackles that held the furnace doors in the city’s foundry, their links flecked with the memory of labor and the sweat of men who learned to slip them off when the heat rose too high. These are not symbols floating in dream, but the grounding things a person finds in the old corners of cellars and the deep seams of the earth.

Desire is a tool when it is seen—this is the lesson of the forge, where the anvil is scarred with centuries of hammer strikes. In the heart of a winter night, a kettle hangs above the wood stove, a product of the same stubbornness that pulls diamonds from ancient seams. The room is paneled with walnut, and behind one door is an umbrella, its canopy made from the lacquered silk of a monsoon market, as impenetrable as the volcanic glass that traders once carried from the rift valleys to distant ports. In the same rain, mud clings to boots and the pavement mirrors the sky; a city is veiled in coal smoke and clouds that swallow the sun, making each streetlamp burn with double intensity.

On the pedestal, the candlestick flares. The flame catches on the edges of a book cover—leather, well-handled, the kind found in libraries where the floors are stained with years of soft-shod footsteps. Nearby, a tray holds figs, dried and wrinkled, a delicacy traded along routes where volcanic soil fed the roots of ancient orchards. Crows gather outside, their feathers catching the sheen left by midnight rain, as they wait for morning scraps or the warmth rising from a bakery’s chimney. Each of these things, a vessel for longing: the iron kettle, the basalt stone, the leather-bound book, the umbrella waiting for the storm. They are seen, touched, grasped, and released; their use is in the knowing, not the clinging.

There is a cathedral in the hills built from lava stone, darker than storm clouds, its arches rising above the heather. Pilgrims arrive when the night is thickest, guided by lanterns that gutter in the wind. Among them walks the trader who sells grindstones, a mason’s apron belted tight, the pockets lined with river pebbles smooth as the inside of a plum. The path is wet with the residue of last night’s tempest, and the air smells of wet earth and the promise of bread baked at dawn. Here, at the edge of sense and reckoning, desire becomes a tool, the chain that slips loose with the right turn of the wrist.

Today, a person can polish the handles of a cast-iron pan until the surface glows with memory, or open the umbrella and listen to the drum of rain on its stretched surface. They might gather smooth stones from a riverbank after a storm, or light a candle on the old windowsill and watch the shadows grow. These small acts root the spirit in the world as it is, where desire is seen, not hidden.
