Draw a card / THE DEVIL

HoneyDew

Reversed · #F0FFF0

The Devil card’s iron chain, oversized and obvious, sits loosely around the neck now. Imagined horns, the memory of torchlight and cold stone, seem further away than usual—the room is quiet, the air still. What holds the mind in place is less a true shackle than a habitual gesture, a familiar loop worn smooth by repetition. Some become so used to the weight that when it slips away, they continue to sit as if bound, fingers idly tracing where the metal once pressed into skin.

In the night markets of Central Asia, beneath woven canopies, honey farmers stack their wares in pyramids of pale, translucent fruit. The melons are heavy, their skin a thin rind over a water-rich core. Vendors slice one open and, with a wide, flat knife, offer up crescents of cool flesh to passing children. The sweetness is so subtle it seems almost a memory, the taste itself gentle but persistent. These fruits trade hands quickly at dawn, when the desert’s edge is still slick with dew, and the baskets fill with round, smooth shapes that might have been cradled in the crook of the moon.

In ancient forests, high in the canopy, a different sweetness gathers. Aphids pierce the leaves and, in their wake, leave beads of gleaming syrup. Ants cluster around these drops, shepherding the aphids, milking from them the fluid that will tip from leaf to ground as a sticky gift. Shepherds, moving their hives from field to woodland, raise their eyes to where bees gather this dew, making honey that tastes of fir and pine—complex, light on the tongue, almost ethereal. Long ago, apiarists would search for branches slick with this substance, knowing the best harvest comes on misty mornings when the world is quiet and the trees shimmer with an invisible gift.

In the household, there is another ritual: slicing the morning’s fruit, placing the crescents on a glazed ceramic plate, carrying the offering to a breakfast table where the window is open to the day’s first breeze. The tablecloth is thin, finely woven, perhaps embroidered with patterns of stylized vines. The cool of the melon seeps into the fingertips, reminding one of slow dawns and the hush before errands begin. Sometimes, on rare, humid days, dew collects along the inside of a glass left overnight on the sill; this is the air’s own harvest, caught and held briefly before sunlight lifts it away.

The weather that brings such gifts is not storm or spectacle but a hush—early hours when the sky is blurred, the ground soft, and the world seems freshly unfastened. In the hills outside Thessaly, shepherds lead their flocks through grass still wet from the night’s exhale. One pauses to press a blade of grass between their teeth and taste the cool sweetness left behind. These are mornings not of drama, but of release—a gentle undoing, the chains of yesterday lying forgotten in the moss.

Today, one might bring home a melon from the market, slice it open just as the vendor did, and savor the fruit slowly, alone in the quiet. Another may scatter a plate of fruit with fresh mint, carrying it to a friend in the next room, simple and unadorned. A third might pause at dawn, stepping outside barefoot to feel the dew in the lawn, letting the moisture gather between toes, reminding the body that the day’s constraints are not yet decided.
