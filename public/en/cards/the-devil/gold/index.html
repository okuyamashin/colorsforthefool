Draw a card / THE DEVIL

Gold

悪魔 · ゴールド

Upright · #FFD700

The card is a stage and a warning. A carved stone block stands at the center, raised as if for a statue, but empty of idols. Two chains lie slack across its base, neither locked nor held tight, only resting. Above, a flame burns in a battered brass torch, the kind one might find in an old mine or a cathedral crypt, where the air smells of old earth and incense. To glance at the torch is to see how a single point of light draws all eyes, promising warmth and illumination, yet also daring the watcher to reach too close.

Desire is not only a chain. In old myths, the promise of gleaming treasure lured seekers into deep caverns, where veins of a certain metal threaded through quartz and granite, catching every flicker of the lamplight. These seekers pried stones from the earth with pickaxes, haunted by dreams of kingdoms and crowns. In the folds of history, entire cities rose and fell by the hope found in riverbeds, where panners turned mud and water in their hands, searching for even a hint of something precious. The torch on the card is a miner’s companion, burning long hours in tunnels where light is both a tool and a temptation.

On the surface, desire for more becomes ceremony. In grand halls, plates and goblets catch the candlelight, their surfaces chased and hammered by skilled hands. To possess these objects is to know both pride and peril, for the table’s shine reflects back one’s own hungers. On feast days, bread is broken, and cakes dusted with flakes drawn from distant lands, reserved for honored guests. Here, the torch’s flame is echoed in the glow of the hearth, where heavy pots bubble and steam, promising comfort but always asking what will satisfy.

But desire need not be a trap. The chains on the card are loose, easy to slip off with a glance and a shift of the wrist. In the stories told by traders who journey between sun-bleached cities, metal passes from hand to hand as coin and promise, but it is the agreement—the handshake, the look in the eye—that truly binds. In a monastery’s library, illuminated pages catch the light, and the ink’s luster is a call to read, to learn, to see beyond surface and shine.

Weather can press down like greed, heavy and brilliant at once. On midsummer afternoons in open fields or at the edges of mountains, the sky blazes, and every blade of wild grass seems to catch fire with reflected brightness. Shepherds squint as they walk, knowing the blessing and the danger of a day when the world is both illuminated and exposed. The torch is needed less, but the memory of its flame remains.

Today, a person might polish a favorite trinket until it shines, recalling the hands that made it and the moments it has seen. They could braid a chain for themselves, loose enough to slip free, a reminder of choices. They might write a letter by candlelight, letting the words reflect ambitions held close. Or, they could walk at noon, letting the sun’s heat remind them of both longing and freedom.
