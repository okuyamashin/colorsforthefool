Draw a card / THE DEVIL

DarkGoldenRod

悪魔 · ダークゴールデンロッド

Upright · #B8860B

At the heart of the upright Devil card, the pedestal rises: not as a throne, but as an anvil—heavy, solid, bearing the marks of many blows. Chained forms bend but are not fused to their shackles. With the simple act of loosening their grip, the chain falls slack and escape becomes possible, but only if one sees it clearly. Desire burns like a torch, held aloft above the altar, its flame both warning and invitation. The light flickers against the walls, illuminating not the vastness of the world, but the precise outlines of what stands closest: a field after harvest, the dry stalks left standing, and from them, bundles gathered to feed wintering animals or kindle a necessary fire.

There is a plant in the open spaces of a wild prairie and along fences behind old farmhouses, its stems tall, its plumes waving in late summer wind. This rod, once picked and dried, was bound for broom handles, for the rickety ladders of gardeners, or for the ribbed supports of small sheds. The same plant, ground and sifted, found its way into the hands of dyers, who steeped it in copper pots, hoping for a pigment that would anchor a story to heavy wool capes or the fine embroidery on ceremonial sashes. Shepherds watched their flocks pass through patches of these rods, the sheep occasionally nibbling the flowers, a habit believed by some to keep them healthy as the seasons turned hard.

Traders in early towns brokered these rods in sheaves, bundled beside sacks of grain and casks of honey, each stalk a marker of late summer’s plenty and a gamble on the coming cold. History records their use in medicine: boiled, filtered, and pressed into poultices, a folk remedy for tired joints and bruises. The rod grew in the margins, undomesticated but always near, an emblem of persistence and the quiet utility that outlasts grander gestures. In some folktales, these rods became wands in the hands of hedge-witches, instruments not of domination but of clarity—used to sweep aside illusions and to mark a boundary between what is craved and what can be chosen.

The torch in the card recalls the way weather can suddenly lift on a late autumn day, when the sky clears and the roads are edged with the last dry plants, each stalk shimmering in the angled light. To walk there is to know the pleasure of movement and the restraint of cold air biting at the skin. The wind, sharp and clean, rattles through the rods, making a music that speaks of freedom within boundaries—a melody that reminds the listener of desire’s real shape.

In the kitchen, a wooden honey dipper sits beside dark bread, both recalling the practical crafts that shaped the countryside. Nearby, a mortar and pestle, stained by years of herbal grinding, rests on a shelf beside a coil of rope once twisted from the plant’s fibers. The torch’s light is echoed in the gleam of old tools, used to cut and bind, to separate what is needed from what binds one to the ground.

If you wish to move with the current of today’s card, sweep your home with a handmade broom, or gather fallen stalks from your local field and tie them together as a reminder of what can be harnessed. Press the dried flowers between the pages of a journal, or steep their stems in warm water for a modest infusion, to taste what is carried from field to hand. Let your gaze settle on the handles and tools that have always waited, ready to serve—knowing that the chains are only chains when forgotten.
