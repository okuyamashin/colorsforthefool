Draw a card / THE DEVIL

FireBrick

Upright · #B22222

At the center of the room, a pedestal rises, assembled from the blocks that survive flame and hammer. Their edges are chipped, surfaces roughened by years of touch and weather. They were once clay, soft and yielding, shaped with hands and hope, dried in air and then turned unforgiving in the heart of a kiln. The torch set atop this pedestal burns with a steady, hungry glow, its light thrown across the walls, glinting off shackles that hang slack but present. The chains, cold and heavy, seem at first to bind, but with a close look, their links are open, ready to fall away if the captive chooses to see.

Desire, in the light of this torch, becomes a tool rather than a tyrant. The ancient blocks, forged in the heat that could destroy, are now fit for shelter and strength. In the winding lanes of forgotten cities, these blocks laid the foundation for hearths and for ovens that fed entire neighborhoods. Here, bread rises above glowing coals, crust forming in the radiant heat stored by the same blocks that once walked the fire’s path. In legend, these blocks are the bones of towers built by those hungry for power or knowledge; in reality, they are what keeps a family warm in the bleak midwinter, what holds the heat that shapes pottery, what guards the threshold from the storm.

The pedestal remembers every hand that heaved its blocks into place. The torch’s fire, borrowed from volcano, lightning, or the careful strike of flint, reveals the truth in the room: chains that slip loose when their wearer ceases to believe in their power. In myth, a figure bound to the earth learns to see through the illusion of captivity, finding that the shackles were always looser than they appeared. In kitchens and smithies, the same blocks absorb flame’s wildness and return it as warmth, as bread, as steel forged for purpose rather than destruction.

Rain falls outside, drumming against solid walls. The blocks, darkened by years of weather, hold fast. In alleyways behind old bakeries, stacks of discarded blocks wait to be put to use again, their heft perfect for lining new ovens or supporting garden beds. The same material that forms the city’s bones forms the tool of its transformation—a reminder that what endures has survived the fire, not by chance but by intention. The torchlight flickers, and in that shifting glow, it is possible to see both temptation and freedom, both the chain and the open path.

Today, you could gather stones or blocks and create a small altar, honoring endurance and what survives the flames. Break a small habit that encircles you, if only for a day, in recognition of the chains that unfasten when truly seen. Stroll through a part of your town where the oldest structures stand, touching the walls as you walk, aware of the hands that stacked them. Or knead dough and bake bread in an oven lined with the same ancient stuff, letting your own hunger become something shared and sustaining.
