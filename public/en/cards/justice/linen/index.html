Linen

正義 · リネン

Upright · #FAF0E6

A sword lifted in one hand, scales balanced in the other, the figure sits before a draped hanging, sunlight overhead, and the air motionless. In the justice chamber, the fabric behind the seat is more than backdrop: it is a border, a sign that the world of the ordinary and the world of account both meet here, yet remain distinct. As light pours in, there is no corner for doubt to hide; everything set on the table must be examined, sifted, and weighed. Each action, each answer, meets its consequence face-to-face.

This draped cloth is the result of quiet, patient labor that begins far from marble halls. The story starts in fields where the plant’s stalks stand tall in careful rows, reaching toward the open sky. Season after season, rough stems are pulled from earth, bundled and left to soften beneath dew and sunlight. The process is slow: the stalks are soaked, pounded, split, and combed, each step carried out by steady hands. Only after this work are the long, strong fibers spun and woven into fabric—a material plain but durable, cherished for its endurance and the way it holds its form.

History traces this cloth through bustling markets and along old caravan roads. Merchants traded it by the bolt, prized for its ability to last through many washings and serve many uses. It became judge’s robes, table covers, wrappings for loaves of bread, and the base for careful writing. Scribes relied on scraps of it for important records; contracts were folded and sealed within its weave. The cloth takes on the marks of life—creases from folding, stains from ink or fruit, repaired spots from needle and thread—each flaw a subtle testament to the pattern of cause and result.

Where the plant once grew, the fields are marked by straight furrows and the rhythm of harvest: cut, gather, stack. Farmers work steadily, mindful that each step affects the next. The weather here is gentle, promising a slow and even ripening, the kind that rewards patience and evenhandedness. At home, the finished fabric is taken down from the line, soft and clean, spread across tables as a place for meals, coin-counting, or the writing of letters. It is both ordinary and ceremonial, part of daily life but also present at times of reckoning, setting the surface for honest exchange.

In the world’s old ports, bales of this cloth traveled between continents, traded for spices, metal, and stories from distant lands. It lined trunks, wrapped valuables, and appeared in the collars and cuffs of those who spoke the law. Its presence, even in private rooms, is a quiet reminder that fairness is maintained not through grandeur, but through the humble, continuous work of keeping things in order.

For those who wish to walk in step with Justice today, consider smoothing a clean cloth across your table before you eat, or writing a letter or list on a fresh sheet. Walk outside and notice the fields or gardens organized by careful hands, or mend a worn patch in a favorite garment. These small actions are threads in a larger pattern, gestures that echo the weaving of fairness into every day.
