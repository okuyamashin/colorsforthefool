Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / JUSTICE

Gold

Upright · #FFD700

A sword set upright beside a weighted scale stands in a silent chamber, late morning pressing against a high window. Sunlight spills directly over the blade’s edge and across the folds of the draped cloth behind. Here, every shape holds its meaning: the sword for truth, the scale for consequence, the cloth for what is hidden or revealed, the noon for an hour when shadow is shortest and cause meets result without trickery. In this bright stillness, one is reminded that justice is not merely a word but a structure built day by day from actions seen and unseen.

Across cultures, the search for fairness has often found its symbol in a gleaming object: a coin engraved with faces and numbers, passed from hand to hand under the watch of rulers and common folk alike. In the ancient city of Lydia, merchants measured grain and wool with clinking circles stamped by the king’s mark, each transaction a promise that the weight truly matched the worth. The same metal, drawn from rivers or pried from stone, became a thread in the weaving looms of Alexandria, shining under temple torches as priests recorded offerings and debts. In Byzantium, crowns and rings shaped from it were tokens of trust, exchanged at the temple steps when oaths were sworn. When a dispute arose, the judge’s ring or scepter, heavy and bright, was set upon the table for all to see: a sign that decisions would be binding, the evidence examined in the open.

The midday sun, at its most direct, has often been called the hour of reckoning. On the high steppes of Central Asia, traders would lay out their silks and spices precisely at noon, when nothing could hide in the shadow of a tent or cart. Farmers across the Mediterranean would thresh wheat during these hours, trusting the light to show every chaff and kernel. In monasteries, the great bells marked the passage as monks gathered to copy manuscripts, their inkpots reflecting the light from the tall, glassless windows. In every place, the rule was clear: when the light revealed all, there was a duty to match word to deed, scale to measure, promise to fulfillment.

In the stories of old, heroes quested for treasures hidden deep underground. The dragon’s hoard or the king’s ransom was never merely a heap of shining things, but a test: could the seeker carry it with clean hands? When the object was claimed without deceit, those who watched — gods, spirits, ancestors — marked the act as righteous. The fair judge in every myth, whether cloaked in feathers or sitting beneath an olive tree, weighed not only evidence but the intent behind it. In this way, the quest for justice was always paired with something tangible, something that could be held, measured, counted, or worn.

Today, a person may polish a family heirloom—a ring or locket with a long story—before facing a difficult conversation, letting it remind them to speak honestly. One might balance coins on the table while paying bills, pausing to consider the fairness of each exchange. Hanging a fresh cloth across a window at midday, a person can invite brightness into a room, making no space for secrets. Or a simple act: write a letter, careful with each word, as if it were a contract to be honored.
