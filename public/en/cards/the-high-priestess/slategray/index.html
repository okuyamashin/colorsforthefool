Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / THE HIGH PRIESTESS

SlateGray

Reversed · #708090

The papyrus scroll remains closed beneath her hand, the words locked away, withheld from the seeker at her feet. Curtains hang heavy behind her, not drifting, but still, and the moon’s reflection on the pool is lost beneath a thin film. This is the moment after a question, when the answer does not come—when the oracle’s mouth is silent and the chamber gathers dusk. The world beyond the veil is not so much forbidden as unyielding, the silence of unanswered things filling the air like winter’s mist.

There is a roof tile, quarried from the borderlands, that crowns many monasteries and weathered watchtowers. It sits atop places of seclusion, shaped by hands that know how to keep stories in. Rain falls and drains along the grooves, carrying away what might have been said, pooling instead at the eaves. At the edge of a northern fishing village, the sea and sky press close during long afternoons when fog drifts in from the open water. Boats return to harbor, their hulls bearing the memory of storms, ropes slick and uncoiling across the stone wharf. All is muffled—the voices, the footfalls, the distant lighthouse bell. The fishermen’s coats—worn and heavy from years of salt and damp—hang in the entryways, a testament to endurance and quiet labor.

In old kitchens, where the hearth glows low, a slab of shale rests against the wall, scrawled with recipes that have faded under generations of fingertips. The stone was once split from a hillside, carried and set down as a surface for kneading bread or sharpening knives. The marks left on it are only half legible now, memories of meals and conversations that have worn thin with time. Nearby, the handle of a pot, smooth from use and slightly cool to the touch, holds the warmth of last night’s stew. Outside, clouds linger at the horizon, pressing close, sunless for days, their weight hinting at withheld rain.

The ancient scribes in their monasteries stored their secrets under layered slates, each inscribed tablet hidden below another. In some remote valleys, the boundary stones that mark property lines and ancient roads are carved from these same rocks, their scripts half-erased by centuries of wind. Travelers pass but seldom stop, reading only what remains above the moss. In the city, a seamstress keeps her scissors sharpened on a whetstone she inherited from her grandfather, the edge keen but the story of its making untold.

On evenings when the world feels impenetrable, when doors close and the words do not form, a person can trace their finger along the edge of a roof tile, feeling its cool grain and the patience in its making. They might bake bread on a slab of old stone, letting the weight hold their intentions for the day. In the hush that settles during fog, a walk along the harbor wall can offer quiet company, the sound of distant water muted but steady. Or, sharpening a kitchen knife on a whetstone, one can focus on the simple act, trusting that clarity comes in its own time.
