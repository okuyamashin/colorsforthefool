Draw a card / THE HIGH PRIESTESS

Indigo

女教皇 · インディゴ

Upright · #4B0082

Between the two silent pillars, the High Priestess abides, her presence marking the boundary between what is seen and what is hidden. The veil behind her is tightly drawn, as if to protect the mysteries that rest just beyond reach. In her lap, a scroll stays closed, the writing concealed. Below, the water is motionless, reflecting only those who dare to approach with quiet hearts and empty hands. The stillness of her court is not emptiness, but a fullness of secrets, the hush before revelation. Here, the subtle fragrance of dried leaves and the distant sound of trickling water make the world feel paused, steeped in anticipation.

Long before ink met paper, there were other ways to keep secrets. In lush river valleys and terraced fields, people bent to gather plants with waxy leaves, often overlooked by the hurried eye. These leaves, when crushed and soaked, released a strange scent, earthy and sharp. Farmers and dyers, skilled in patience, tended large vats where the preparation was slow and careful. They watched the transformation with quiet satisfaction, knowing the magic unfolded out of sight, beneath the water’s surface. The process was veiled, guarded by families who passed recipes, rituals, and timing from one generation to the next. What emerged were long strips of cloth, prized near and far, their depths the envy of merchants along the winding trade roads.

These finished textiles found their way into temples and royal courts. When priests stepped behind the curtains to consult the oracle, their robes were cut from fabric soaked in the fruits of a distant harvest. A sash or mantle might speak of journeys across mountains and deserts, or of rainy afternoons spent awaiting the right moment to draw cloth from the vat. In quiet homes, a length of this cloth might be wrapped around a treasured book, or used as a canopy for meditation, a daily reminder of what lies beyond the world’s noise.

Elsewhere, in bustling markets by the sea, traders from far ports would unload bundles wrapped tight and heavy. The salt air mingled with the scent of the preserved plant, sharp as a memory. Healers sometimes ground the dried pods, mixing the powder with water to paint blessings on doorways or to dye ceremonial rice. On festival days, the same extract might be sprinkled in water bowls, its aroma rising with the morning mist. In some lands, the names of villages, the bends of rivers, and the pages of household ledgers recorded the importance of the annual harvest and the long months of patient waiting before the vats would yield their secret treasure.

Even the sky holds reminders of the Priestess’s secret. The moments before a summer thunderstorm, when the clouds gather and the wind turns cool, feel weighted with anticipation. On such days, the world seems to hold its breath, as if waiting for a sign from beyond the veil—a sign that what is hidden will be revealed, but only when time and silence are honored.

Today, you might prepare a pot of tea from dried petals once used in dyeing, letting the steam curl softly in the morning. You could drape a richly woven scarf over your shoulders, tracing with your finger the pattern of distant hills and rivers. Pressing a flower between pages of a cherished book, or pausing to listen to the hush before a storm, brings you closer to the presence of what is unspoken, reminding you of the beauty in keeping some things secret for now.
