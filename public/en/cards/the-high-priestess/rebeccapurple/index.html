Draw a card / THE HIGH PRIESTESS

RebeccaPurple

Reversed · #663399

The High Priestess sits behind her veil, a book unread upon her knees, and the stream at her feet is silent. In this moment, her domain is not one of flowing secrets, but a hush so thick it keeps the world at bay. The scroll remains sealed, as if the words themselves fear to be spoken. Where her upright self would beckon the dreamer into moonlight and mystery, now the space is marked by a hush that will not yield.

In an old English garden, near dusk, the last of the sun grazes the domes of foxglove and the long spires of wildflowers. There is one patch where the flowers cluster like velvet—unassuming, solemn, neither inviting nor forbidding. Children once called them "witches’ gloves" and dared each other to touch the petals, but today no hand reaches out. The garden’s edge is a line of restraint, a hint that some things are best contemplated from a distance.

Down winding city lanes, iron gates bar the path to buildings with heavy velvet curtains drawn across every window. Behind them, the air is thick with the weight of old stories and dust—volumes in libraries locked for reasons no one quite recalls. A seamstress, just beyond the gates, thumbs through her basket for a skein spun for mourning, used to trim the cuffs of winter coats or embroider a border of forget-me-nots on a handkerchief pressed deep into a cedar chest. There is comfort in the ritual, yet also the knowledge that some garments are made to conceal rather than to reveal.

On the stormy coast of Northumberland, twilight gathers as fishermen look to the sky for signs. Mist rolls in, swallowing the sound of the tide, while the heather on the moor glistens with rain. Once, the roots of certain wild plants were boiled for medicine, the result poured into stoneware jars kept away from light and prying eyes. Old wives tell how the draft inside smelled faintly of earth and salt, a remedy meant for secrets of the body—ailments spoken of in hushed tones, never shared beyond a trusted circle.

In Victorian parlors, a tall glass decanter stands on a shadowed mantelpiece. Inside: the syrup of blackcurrant, thick and glossy, made from berries that stained the fingers of those who picked them at the end of August. The cordial is poured for guests as a gesture both hospitable and reserved, its taste rich but never too sweet. Unopened bottles accumulate in cellars, given as gifts at funerals, tucked behind stacks of letters tied with ribbon—tokens of things spoken of only in passing, if at all.

A person may tend the foxgloves along an empty path, knowing not every visitor should be welcomed in. Another might press a handkerchief embroidered in grief into a winter coat, carrying memory quietly through cold mornings. One can stand at the edge of the moor, listening to the wind’s secrets without asking them to speak. Or, pour a cordial for a guest, allowing silence to fill the space where words might spark regret.
