Draw a card / THE EMPRESS

Brown

Reversed · #A52A2A

The orchard gate hangs on its hinges, half open to the wild wind. Once tended with careful hands, the soil at the Empress’s feet is no longer turned and softened. Fallen branches clutter the rows where pears once swelled. The baskets, woven tight from willow by the hearth in late winter, sit unused beneath the eaves, their insides gathering the dust of many unharvested seasons. Rain leaves puddles at the doorstep; mud dries in the sun, then cracks. The house holds the warmth and weight of old shelter, but it muffles the laughter of a harvest, and the scent of bread is missing from the table.

In elder tales, the garden is a place of promise, a world where wheat rises from the earth and feeds its keeper. Stories from river valleys tell of fields plowed by oxen, clods split by the metal of a well-kept plow, and seeds pressed gently into furrows. In such stories, the home glows with the pulse of the oven as loaves rise beneath thick cloth—proof of plenty shared. But when the Empress’s care falters, the harvest lingers in the fields. The granary doors remain shut, and the wind stirs the chaff among the empty rows.

The geography of this season feels close and heavy. On the slope below the house, where the earth should yield, the path is rutted and tangled with roots. Chestnuts drop from the tree and lie untouched, their shells splitting in the late sunlight. Far down the lane, the tannery’s air is thick and pungent, and leather hangs stiff on its racks. In the kitchen, the breadbox is empty except for a crust going hard at the edges. The hands that once kneaded flour and water now rest on the table, still but not yet open. In the cellar, the sacks of lentils and beans are untouched, and the stoneware jars of honey are sealed with wax, their sweetness hidden away. The hearth, too, is quiet, the old wooden spoon propped in its jar, the flour sifter idle.

Sometimes, the world outside matches the hush indoors. The weather hovers just before rain, the scent of turned earth rising as clouds press close to the ground. Fields wait, unworked, soaked and stubborn, the promise of planting receding with each day the spade stays in the shed. It is a waiting that grows heavier with time, like uncollected wood at the forest’s edge or the burden of coats hung too long behind the door. Night falls early, and the lamp’s glow seems small, drawing faces inward to the heart of the house.

Today, you might sweep the threshold clear of leaves, bringing order to the entrance. You could gather stray chestnuts into a basket, lining them up where you will see them each morning. You might choose to knead dough again, letting the warmth of your hands and the rhythm of pressing and folding awaken the quiet kitchen. Or, simply, you could polish the old wooden spoon, feeling the familiar shape that once stirred the pot.
