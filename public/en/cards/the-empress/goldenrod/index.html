Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / THE EMPRESS

GoldenRod

Upright · #DAA520

The Empress sits among wheat that bends in invitation, surrounded by roses clustering near her seat. Fruit trees form a gentle canopy overhead, their branches heavy with ripeness, while a waterfall murmurs over stones in the garden’s far corner. Every surface is alive with fullness: baskets spill over with pears and apples, vines curl along the earth, and the air feels laden with growth. Abundance is not a distant hope here; it is woven into the daily moments, close and real.

Near her, wild stems reach up from the borderlands of the garden, their late-summer blossoms catching sunlight. These plants, named for their upright spires, have long been markers of harvest’s approach. On wide North American prairies, settlers once noticed their sudden appearance as a call to gather in crops. In European countryside villages, these stems would be brought indoors and placed in pitchers at kitchen tables, believed to carry good fortune and a bountiful season to the household. Herbalists collected their blossoms for use in teas, blending them with dried fruit peels to create restorative mixtures. In folk tradition, children would bundle these wildflowers with wheat and tuck them behind doors or under pillows, wishing for dreams as sweet as orchard fruit.

Travelers crossing fields in September might spot these tall stalks standing guard at the edge of farmland, brushed by the wind. In some regions, farmers tied them in sheaves along fence posts, believing they welcomed prosperity to the land. In mountain villages, the same stalks were set above hearths or woven into simple wreaths, signals that the household had gathered what it needed for the colder days ahead. These plants also marked paths through meadows, helping those returning at dusk find their way home by the way the blossoms caught the last light.

Inside, household objects echo the same sense of substance and care. A honey jar rests on the kitchen counter, its contents thick from bees who spent the summer visiting these spires of wildflowers. A sturdy basket, its weave dusted with flour from the morning’s bread, sits nearby, ready for the next gathering from the orchard or market. A cloth embroidered with delicate sprays of flowers drapes over the back of a wooden chair, crafted by hands accustomed to season and patience. The cloth’s presence suggests comfort and continuity, linking the work of the garden with the rhythms inside the home.

Weather, too, plays a part in this world. When late-afternoon storms cross the fields, the blossoms on these wild stems remain upright, resilient through wind and rain. In some traditions, their presence after a storm is taken as a sign that the land’s gifts endure, no matter the passing of clouds. When the sun returns, the garden is brighter, the air clearer, and gratitude easier to name.

Today, you might bring a stem of wildflower inside and place it near your window as a sign of quiet wealth. Drizzle honey over warm bread and taste the memory of summer fields. Walk along the edge of a park or garden, and let your hands brush against the tall, upright blossoms. Take out a linen cloth—plain or embroidered—and use it to wrap fruit or bread, bringing the garden’s sense of plenty into your daily life.
