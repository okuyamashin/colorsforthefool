OliveDrab

女帝 · オリーブドラブ

Reversed · #6B8E23

A field once lush with wheat, poppies, and wild chicory now lies tangled and heavy after the storms. The stone walls that marked the Empress’s dominion lean under the weight of climbing, untended vines, their leaves dense and crowded, blotting out the light below. The orchard's ancient trees, once laden with ripening fruit, now drop shriveled olives into last year’s leaves, left to sour where they fall. The Empress’s garden is not empty, but its gifts no longer come freely to waiting hands.

In the old country, groves like these fringe the lower slopes, each tree twisted by decades of wind, each root pressing deep into the soil’s memory. Their fruit was once pressed for lamp oil, for anointing, for simple meals. Now, pressed by neglect, the fruit thickens and puckers, remaining out of reach, a harvest unshared. The village mill, where stone wheels once patiently turned, is silent. In the shade of the trees, army coats are draped over chairs on porches: worn, toughened by use, the scent of earth and wood smoke clinging to their seams. They are the garb of those who remain, working the ground by hand after the machines rusted in the rain.

It is a season when shelter, once welcome, can become stifling. In the kitchen, the larder shelves are lined with jars of brine, where olives float, their smooth skins dulled by months in saltwater. These jars were meant to carry the taste of summer into winter, but now they sit unopened, gathering dust as appetite ebbs. The pale light of afternoon slants through curtains, catching the metal canisters where last year’s oil grows cloudy and thick. The table is scattered with the tools of keeping: a wooden press, a worn pruning knife, a coil of rope for tying branches. None see much use.

Stories linger among these tools. During long rains, elders recall the lean years when soldiers moved through the hills, their uniforms the same hue as the groves, blending into the land. They moved quietly, nearly invisible against the scrub and the ploughed rows, disappearing into rain and morning mist. The land itself seemed to swallow them, sheltering them, or perhaps holding them fast.

Outside, the air is damp with the promise of coming storms. The pathways between the trees are soft underfoot, the grass thick and matted, hiding stones and half-buried rakes. In the shade, the air is cool and heavy, and the weight of the canopy presses down. From a distance, the garden appears whole, but up close, every branch and leaf is crowded and burdened, the space between choked with growth. It is a place held in suspension, where abundance has curdled into overgrowth, and bounty no longer reaches the hands waiting for it.

Today, you might gather the fallen olives, washing them in a bowl and setting them out to dry, even if they are bitter. You could oil your boots or jacket with what remains in the canister, letting the scent of the land rise around you. You might open a window to let the damp air pass through the kitchen and sweep the floor where brine has dried and left its trace. You may spend a few minutes sharpening the pruning knife, preparing for the moment when the garden can be set right again.
