Wheat

女帝 · ウィート

Upright · #F5DEB3

In the rich garden of The Empress, a woman settles on her carved seat beneath branches heavy with fruit. Around her, tall grass stands in tidy rows, each stalk crowned with a cluster of ripened grain. The soft rustle of these stalks is joined by the hush of a waterfall nearby, its water feeding the roots of every living thing. At her feet, roses climb and bloom, their petals open to the sun, while in her lap rests a woven basket, filled with round loaves still warm from the hearth. The earth here is worked and welcoming, showing the careful labor of many seasons—paths marked by footprints, beds edged with small stones, and wooden rakes leaning against a low fence.

Beyond this tended space, the countryside opens wide, a patchwork of gently swaying fields. Ancient stories linger here: in the earliest villages, people gathered these tall grasses at the edge of summer, cutting and tying them into bundles to carry home. The grains they collected became the first bread, baked in clay ovens, sustaining households through cold months. These same fields provided straw for thatched roofs and soft bedding, keeping families sheltered from wind and rain. In homes along the riverbanks, the harvest was celebrated with shared meals and songs, every table set with loaves and pitchers, every door adorned with braided stalks as a sign of welcome and blessing.

Myth and memory are woven into these fields. In old times, a ring of braided stalks was hung above a doorway to guard against hunger and misfortune. In Greece, stories told of Demeter wandering the land with a sheaf under her arm, her gift to humankind the knowledge of sowing and reaping, of patience rewarded each season. On the steppes, the stalks bent with the wind but stood again, resilient through sudden storms and long months of waiting. In kitchens, handfuls of grain passed through the millstone, then into dough shaped by practiced hands. The scent of baking traveled out through open windows, a promise of comfort and plenty.

After the harvest, when the fields are bare and the air cooler, the work turns inward. Extra stalks are gathered and tied into brooms, sweeping the last dust from the hearth. Some are woven with care into hats or mats, useful and sturdy, bringing a memory of summer fields into the house as days grow short. Even in distant cities, a vase of dried stalks on the table calls back to the old ways, a reminder that abundance flows from the simplest things.

Today, anyone can knead dough for fresh bread and share it warm with others, echoing the age-old ritual of sustenance and connection. Placing a simple bundle of dried stalks on the windowsill or weaving a ring to hang above the door invites quiet fortune. Sweeping a room with a straw broom, the motion slow and steady, brings the calm of harvest into the home. Saving a handful of next season’s grain in a small jar honors patience and the promise of renewal.
