Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / THE EMPRESS

Khaki

Reversed · #F0E68C

In the upright Empress, the well-tended garden yields fruits, wheat stands tall, and a mother’s arms open to share bounty with all. Reversed, the garden grows untamed and brittle. The woven basket, once brimming, lies overturned on packed earth. The harvest, lost to wind and time, never finds its way to the kitchen. The tools of plenty—spades, sickles, aprons—hang unused. The fields, unchecked, become a tangle where yesterday’s promise has faded beneath a haze of dust.

Once, these gardens were sewn by those who came from distant coasts, who remembered fertile riverbanks and wild meadows, who brought with them seeds and skills. Their hands, marked by labor, worked the loam in late afternoon, their feet wrapped in cloth that kept soil and sun at bay. These wrappings, made from durable fibers, protected skin as men and women crossed open plains and built new homes. They wore these cloths for the practical reason that dust and clay clung to every surface. Soldiers and farmers alike valued such cloth for its endurance—woven tightly on looms, then dyed with a solution made from gathered leaves, roots, and the sun-warmed water of a courtyard basin.

In distant lands, explorers and traders marked their passage by what covered their legs. Out in the brush, beneath the dry, constant wind, they moved quietly, their clothing blending with the reeds and sun-bleached grass. The same fabric, cut and stitched by deft hands, became uniforms for those who stood watch in the haze of dawn, eyes narrowed against a sky that promised more heat than shade. In the household, aging linen curtains, once fine, now held the memory of harvests gone by. Laundry left to dry in midday air would be stiff and blank, a pale banner against the garden’s neglected fence.

Sometimes the season’s neglect is not chosen, but inherited. The air fills with particles too fine to see, swirling in hot drafts that push against closed shutters. The wind, restless and dry, lifts the topsoil from the paths and settles it onto the porch, into shoes, onto every tool left outside. The ground, hard-packed and stubborn, refuses the spade. Inside, a sack of grain sits untouched in the pantry, its ties loose, flour settling at the bottom. The table, wide and welcoming, sits bare while plans for feasts remain half-spoken, falling away like chaff.

Still, there are small gestures that return care to the world. Today, one might gather fallen seed pods from the walkway and plant them in a patch of bare earth, a promise to future seasons. Wipe the dust from a window ledge, letting light fall clearly onto the floor. Mend a worn apron, threading a sturdy needle through fabric whose weave remembers the shape of its labor. Offer a cup of warm tea to a neighbor, inviting them to shelter from the wind for even a little while.
