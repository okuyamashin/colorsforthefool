DarkOliveGreen

女帝 · ダークオリーブグリーン

Reversed · #556B2F

In the world of the Empress, gardens sprawl into the distance, promising warmth and a harvest gathered under the hand’s care. But when the card turns, the order within those hedges slips away. Ivy thickens unchecked, climbing over once-proud fences and curling around the handles of rakes left out overnight. The scent of crushed bay leaves lingers in the air, half-forgotten beneath the weight of old branches. It is not the lushness of midsummer, but the hush of a grove left to itself, where olives fall and bruise on weathered stone.

Along slopes in forgotten provinces, the old orchard is heavy with fruit never gathered. The groves have lost their keepers, and the press stands dry. In the kitchen, the jar of brined olives sits neglected at the back of the pantry, its lid sealed but dust settling on glass. Bread lies unfinished on the table, next to a tarnished spoon and a folded napkin stained with yesterday’s meal. The household has grown tired, its corners filled with baskets of withered herbs and the remnants of last autumn’s harvest, left to dry on woven mats by the hearth.

Stories linger where the land is let be. The farmer once sang as she pressed oil from the olives, her hands slick as she worked the old stone mill. Now the stones themselves are cold, and the song is only a memory in the thickening dusk. The hillside, once patterned with neat rows, is threaded with wild thyme and the shadows of sleeping sheep. At the edge of the property, soldiers of a forgotten campaign once passed under olive boughs, their boots muddy from rain that had soaked into the roots, making the earth heavy and slow. In the village, markets still open, but the trade is quiet. The grocer wraps cheese in vine leaves, the veins tracing maps of rivers that have grown shallow with years of drought.

The weather presses down, a sky laden with the scent of rain that never quite arrives. On days like this, the stone path glistens with old oil spilled from a cracked amphora—sticky, pungent, attracting wasps and the curious gaze of cats from beneath the porch. The air is thick with the promise of something that might have been, and the coolness under the olive trees grows heavy with waiting.

In such a world, renewal is not bright or immediate. It is patience—the gentle loosening of the vine from the fence post, the careful turning of the soil around a neglected root, the slow cleaning of a jar to make way for something new. One might take a kitchen cloth and wipe the dust from the olive jar, setting it at the center of the table where light can find it. A stroll through the garden, pulling back the ivy from the handle of a forgotten spade, restores a piece of the order that once was. In the evening, steep a few bay leaves in hot water, letting their aroma rise and settle over the room, recalling older seasons. Or simply press a sprig of wild thyme between the pages of a book, marking the day with a promise of returning care.
