WhiteSmoke

Reversed · #F5F5F5

Some nights are so thick with mist that the way home dissolves. Under the moon’s half-light, familiar figures blur and blend. The watchful dog and the wild wolf slip off their names, their shapes melting into shadow as fog creeps from the ground, swallowing tracks behind and ahead. The path once marked by memory becomes a faint hollow, and silence gathers, quiet as the breath of the night itself. The lantern’s glow is dimmed by the drift curling from unseen hollows, its pale plume rising and folding, gentle but impenetrable. The gates between worlds are hidden behind veils, and the world itself hushes, suspended inside a shroud.

It is not only myth that knows this vapor. In old villages, at the hour before dawn, hearths are stoked and the fine exhalation of kindling pine and old apple wood rises from rooftops. It hovers above clustered eaves, softening every angle and etching the air with the ghosts of warmth. In distant fields, the morning chill draws the same haze from dew-soaked grass and still ponds, cloaking the land. Travelers on mountain roads, wrapped in cloaks, pause to watch plumes slip from monasteries perched above valleys—a signal of waking, of bread rising, tea brewing, bells soon to stir the air.

Smoke drifts through history as a sign of both ending and beginning. From the Vatican, a certain pale cloud once told the city that a new shepherd had been chosen; its rising was watched by thousands, the meaning as clear as sunlight. In more humble homes, the same gentle billow signals a meal prepared, grain roasted or fish dried, a labor of patience and the promise of comfort. Artisans have known it too: the potter’s kiln, once fired, breathes a vapor as pottery becomes more than earth, as if the vessel’s spirit leaves for a moment and then returns.

There is a weather in which this shroud rules—when the horizon is erased and the world outside the window grows quiet and indistinct. It arrives not with storm or thunder, but with a hush, a settling of air over city and field, a slow, edgeless blanket. Children draw shapes in the condensation on the glass and watch as their breath clouds the pane, layering the veil still further. On such days, time moves differently within houses. Washing is hung inside, its dampness leaving the faintest trace of vapor in the kitchen. A grandmother’s tablecloth, starched and ironed, is laid out over the old wooden boards, smooth and nearly weightless, waiting for breakfast.

The cloth on the table, the monastery’s breath, the steady pillar from a distant hearth—each one marks a passage, a shielding, a space in which truth and story mingle. In these moments, clarity is both hidden and revealed: what is lost to view may be protected, what is softened may be seen anew. To live inside this shroud is to learn to trust your feet to find the next step, even when the world’s outlines dissolve.

Today, you can light a stick of incense and watch the thread of vapor spiral and fade, letting your mind follow where it wanders. You might fold a napkin fresh from the laundry, smoothing it flat as a blank beginning. Stand at your window and let a breath mist the glass, then trace a wish in the fleeting haze. Or step outside at dawn, pausing as the garden or street is wrapped in its quiet, and listen to what the world says when it is not quite itself.
