Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / THE MOON

Indigo

Reversed · #4B0082

In the thick hours just before dawn, the world is a tangle of roots and quiet water. The moon, upended, sends no calm light to divide the known from the unknown. Mist creeps between the cracked stones, and the old path, lined with wild fennel and crickets, loses its edge. The hound and the wolf, once marked by their shadows, vanish into the same blur of movement. All distinctions are folded into silence. The air is heavy with the scent of something far away, something that stains the memory but will not yield its name.

In a village downriver, the vats are cooled and the fermenting heaps are almost ready. The air here is sharp, tinged with the scent of stems crushed underfoot. Cloth, rough from the loom, is first dipped and then left to soak. Women with weathered hands stir the steaming liquid, each motion a slow invocation. Stained aprons, skin marked from long hours, they pull the fabric from the bath and carry it out to dry, careful not to spill a single drop. The cloth, weighted and dark, is slung across bamboo poles as the mist settles over the field. It is said that travelers from the east once traded cakes of this dye for salt and dried fish, and that the bloom of this plant marked the edge of gardens where the wild things stayed away.

There are stories that the dye came from rivers that wound through ancient kingdoms, from plantations where monsoons broke the soil and the stems tangled freely over the banks. Once, the leaves were crushed beneath a stone, water added little by little, the mixture left to ferment under burlap. The smell of the process, earthy and deep, clung to the workers’ hands for days. In the early morning, the vapor rising from the vats was as mysterious as the fog that now coils through the woods by the moon’s uncertain light. Somewhere, a bowl carved from tamarind wood lies on a low table, holding a cake of the dye, cracked at the edges. Scraps of fabric, used for patching, are folded in a chest with a sprig of mint to keep away moths.

In the north, travelers speak of thunderstorms split by streaks the color of the dye—lightning that opens the heavens and falls against the mountain’s distant face. The rain there is said to carry the scent of the plant, bitter and almost metallic, into the rivers that run into unfamiliar territory. Even in the softest hours, the memory of that weather remains, fastened to the mind like a secret. Those who worked the fields or boiled the dye always kept a sprig of the plant beneath their pillow, believing it would catch dreams and keep away confusion.

Tonight, the reversed moon keeps its stories close. The mist thickens, and the names for hound and wolf drift away. In the kitchen, a chipped teacup holds a sliver of the hardened dye, saved from the last batch. One can grind a little to mark a letter that will not be sent, or press a square of old-dipped cloth into a pocket for journeys that require crossing nameless bridges. A branch from the plant, dried and brittle, is good for burning in a quiet hour, to remind you of rivers and trade winds. When dawn comes, you might steep a pinch in water and wash your hands, letting the scent linger until the names return.

Today's lucky color is Indigo.
