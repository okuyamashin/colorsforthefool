Draw a card / THE MOON

Linen

Upright · #FAF0E6

The path under the moon stretches between two towers, its twists and turns revealed only in patches. Each footfall brings doubt, as the dew gathers on grass and the stones glisten with night moisture. The distant baying of a dog answers the wilder cry of a wolf, their shapes cast in half-shadow near the tide’s edge. What lies ahead can be guessed but not seen, the landscape veiled in a thin, uncertain glow.

The towers are old, perhaps built from the same fields that surround them, where flax was once grown for its long, pale stalks. Flax fields in early summer are wind-stirred and feather-light, the plants rising up in close ranks before harvest. In the coolest hours before dawn, you can almost hear the soft clatter of hands working these stalks, separating fiber from stem, the beginning of a process as old as weaving itself. Somewhere in the valley beyond the towers, a river moves quietly, its banks edged with the plants whose fibers will become everyday cloth and ritual shroud alike.

Within a low cottage, a woman sits in the hush of late evening. Spread before her is a length of cloth, cool to the touch, spun and woven by careful hands. She works by the faint gleam of a tallow candle, her needle following a pattern she knows by memory, uncertain as it seems under shifting light. The cloth’s weight is just enough to settle her mind, grounding her even as the world outside blurs into confusion and possibility. She remembers stories of wanderers who traveled by night, carrying with them satchels crafted from the same material, the fabric shielding them from chill, carrying scent of the fields and promise of return.

Near the water, fishermen mend their nets, their fingers moving steadily through loops and knots. Each net is a lattice of fiber, strong yet supple, made to yield and hold. The fishermen’s voices are low, sometimes breaking into laughter as they recount strange sights beneath the moon: the glimmer of scales, the sudden flash of a heron’s wing, the way the tide pulls in rhythms older than memory. The nets, once new and untouched, now bear traces of salt and story. Even here, where the world dissolves into mist and uncertainty, the work of making and mending continues.

At the market, bolts of cloth lie in stacks, ready to be cut and shaped. A merchant runs her hands over their surface, feeling the quiet promise of durability and care. She recalls her grandmother’s apron, her father’s shirt, her own wedding veil—all made by hands who trusted in the steady work of turning field to fiber. Some customers buy pieces for practical things: to line a basket, to bind a book, to wrap a newborn against the chill. Others seek fragments for ritual: to lay on an altar, to hang at a doorway, or to mark the turning of the year. Even scraps find their place, patching an old garment or cushioning a treasured keepsake.

Tonight, anyone might walk the boundary between certainty and dream. You could step into your own kitchen and smooth out a cloth for tomorrow’s bread, or open a window to let in the scent of night air moving over fields where flax once grew. If you pause at your threshold in the after-midnight quiet, you might hear the faint threads of old stories weaving through sleep. Consider writing a note on a sheet of good paper, tucking it into a pocket or drawer as a promise to yourself. Or mend a tear in something well-worn, remembering all the journeys it has witnessed.
