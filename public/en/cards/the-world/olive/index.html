Olive

世界 · オリーブ

Upright · #808000

A wreath of leaves encircles the dancer at the heart of the card, her arms outstretched as she marks the end of one journey and the beginning of another. Four living creatures look on from the corners—lion, bull, eagle, and human—silent witnesses to the circuit that closes, the ribbon tied tight in a gesture of fulfillment. The card holds a promise of wholeness, a world gathered and bound with ceremony, like a harvest at its height.

Imagine groves on a sunlit hillside, where the air carries a resinous scent and the wind flickers through pointed leaves. These trees, ancient and gnarled, thrive in stony earth and under harsh summers, their roots burrowing deep for water. Their fruit, small and firm, has been gathered by generations—plucked by hand, sometimes knocked down with long poles until it falls with a muted tap onto linen spread below. In this quiet, persistent work, there is a sense of completion, each tree circling through seasons of flowering, fruiting, and rest, each cycle closing with the harvest.

The fruit is sorted, washed, and pressed in stone-walled rooms. Hands that worked the groves now feed the press, turning the screw until thick, aromatic oil seeps from the pulp. This oil fills clay jars, lines wooden shelves, and pools in shallow dishes on kitchen tables. It is used to anoint the brow of the weary, to coat the top of loaves before baking, and to fuel lanterns that ward off the long night. In the ancient world, this oil marked the close of battles and the coronation of kings; it was poured on stone altars and used to seal treaties. The tree’s branches, woven into wreaths, crowned poets and victors, a living sign of achievement and peace.

There are towns whose names spring from these groves, where the shape of the tree appears on coins and the doors of houses. In the square, pressed fruit is sold by women with hands stained from the task, who pass recipes and stories from one season to the next. Vessels for oil and salted fruit stand in storerooms, waiting for winter. In these regions, weather is measured by cycles: first the dry heat, then the sudden storms that wash dust from the leaves, and finally the gentle warmth that coaxes the first buds from their branches. When the fruit is plenty, it is a sign the year’s work has come full circle.

A person can string a wreath from slender branches and hang it above their door, marking achievement or welcoming peace. They can drizzle the tree’s oil over their evening meal, tasting the labor of many hands and seasons. If they have a smooth stone from the grove, they can set it beside their bed, a quiet reminder of endurance and renewal. At sunrise, they can tie a ribbon—a simple band—around their wrist or a jar, sealing intentions as the dancer does at the center of the world.
