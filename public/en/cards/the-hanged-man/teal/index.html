Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / THE HANGED MAN

Teal

Upright · #008080

From the bough of a sturdy willow, the figure hangs, not in distress but with a calm that travels through the roots into the ground. The world inverts itself, and with it, the familiar becomes strange: branches reach for the earth, while the trunk stretches to the sky. The air is full of hush, a river’s mystery in morning light, and through the stillness a halo forms—a ring of quiet comprehension. The path to new vision is not a leap, but the act of suspending movement, of allowing the wind to pass and the leaves to speak.

It is said that in the ancient wetlands of the East, wild ducks rise from the reeds, their heads crested and eyes alert. These birds are at home where water pools among tangled sedges, where dragonflies hover and frogs sing their patient songs. Here, among floating lilies and the tangled aquatic grass, a duck will pause, tip its head, and dive beneath the surface. For a moment, what is above becomes below, and the world is made anew in a shivering ripple. This duck—long admired by hunters and painters alike—once gave a name to the feathers that shimmer on its head and wings, a signpost for those who notice nature’s subtleties.

In old markets along the canal towns, fishermen mend their nets by the water’s edge, their baskets woven from reeds that have soaked up the memory of rain. A flash of a feather, tucked in a hat or sewn into a child’s toy, marks a connection between the marsh and the hearth. High in the north, in wind-bent cottages, matrons would cut cloth in the likeness of the bird’s plumage, fashioning shawls and ribbons for those setting out by boat or returning from long journeys. Cloth merchants once prized a certain bolt of silk, rare and water-resistant, much sought after for lining the inside of traveling cloaks—something that caught the rain but never clung to the skin. When storms moved over the moors, the sky would darken and the lowland fields would flood, and those wrapped in such a cloak would wait quietly, as if daring the storm to pass.

In the hush of a winter pond, ice forms around the reeds, and the ducks gather in the small spaces left unfrozen. Above them, the low clouds are heavy, but the air is alive with the promise of thaw. Myth tells of a time when such birds flew overhead and the world waited, upside down, for spring to return. In the slow turn of the seasons, the understanding comes: pausing does not mean stopping. It means making room for another angle, letting the water settle until the bottom is clear.

Today, you might watch the pattern of waterfowl as they swim or fly overhead, noticing how they cut through both wind and water. You could gather reeds by a stream, weaving them into a small mat or basket as people once did in marshy lands. You might mend a torn scarf, remembering those old bolts of silk, or simply sit by the window during a rainstorm, letting the stillness open a new view.
