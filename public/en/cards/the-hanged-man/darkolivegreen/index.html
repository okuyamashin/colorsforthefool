Draw a card / THE HANGED MAN

DarkOliveGreen

吊るされた男 · ダークオリーブグリーン

Reversed · #556B2F

The figure on the card dangles, suspended by the ankle, yet in the reversed position, the world is in motion around him. The pause is no longer an interlude but a snare, a moment that should have brought insight instead producing only restlessness. Old olive groves in late autumn come to mind: branches bearing their burden, fruit not yet gathered. The harvesters should be here, pausing amid the trees with their ancient nets, but the wind rises and the rain threatens. Instead, the fruit lingers beyond ripening, toughening in place, denied its moment on the press. There are years when the storms are relentless, when the ladders remain unused and the baskets stay dry in the sheds, and every step forward is refused by the land itself.

Such obstinacy is not only in the weather, but within stone and soil. Picture a hillside in Greece, terraced in dusty tiers, where old families once pressed oil under the weight of wooden beams and granite wheels. The press sits silent, the beam stuck mid-swing, waiting for hands that are busy elsewhere. Yet the harvest cannot be delayed forever. Birds worry at the fruit, and the ground beneath the gnarled trunks becomes slick, treacherous, as uncollected olives fall and rot. The stubbornness of the season is mirrored in the stilled machinery, the halted industry that refuses to admit its own inertia.

There are times when stopping is not a choice, but a demand made by the world. In far-off kitchens, brined olives wait in glass jars on high shelves, their flavors growing more complex with each month that passes. Yet opening a jar, sampling its contents, can become its own refusal—a denial that the time for waiting has come to an end. In such delays, an opportunity slips by unnoticed: the sun sets, the rain comes, and the pickers, watching from windows, wonder if the orchard will recover next year.

Even the tools have a say. The battered pruning hook, edge dulled from last year’s work, rests beside a coil of rope, both laid aside when the first drops of rain began to fall. There is a woolen cloak, woven for cold mornings among the trees, which now gathers dust in the barn. Its rough weave recalls the persistent chase of a shepherd’s dog, circling a flock of sheep through low, pungent brush. In periods of forced waiting, these everyday objects become reminders of pauses too long held, of labor that could begin but hesitates at the threshold, just as the Hanged Man in reverse spins above the ground, unwilling to embrace either suspension or release.

A person may walk among trees, noting which branches bear the heaviest fruit and which have been left for the birds. They might oil their tools in readiness, even if the sky threatens rain. Steeping a handful of cured olives in warm, fragrant oil can mark time passing, a quiet ritual to observe the shift that comes when waiting is no longer productive but stubborn. Or they can mend a cloak, patching it for the journey back into the orchard, accepting that to begin again, one must first acknowledge the pause.
