Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / THE FOOL

Cornsilk

Upright · #FFF8DC

A young wanderer stands at the cliff’s edge, the first step of his journey uncertain but unburdened. He holds a rose with gentle hands, its petals delicate and bright. Beside him, a little dog leaps and circles, urging him onward into the unknown. The path ahead is not paved with stone, but with the soft remains of last season’s harvest—clusters of fibrous threads that once protected ripening cobs, now scattered like invitations across the ground.

These threads, fine and pale, have a place in kitchens and farmhouses across the world. After the harvest, they are pulled from the cobs and laid out on crisp cloths to dry on windowsills, where they catch the faintest morning light and give off a faint, sweet scent. Some are tucked away in old recipe books or glass jars, saved for times when a soothing infusion might be needed. Others are woven into small decorations—rings for napkins, ties for bunches of herbs, or little braided charms to hang by the door. In some towns, children gather the strands and fashion them into simple dolls or braid them into bracelets, tokens of a carefree afternoon and the start of their own small adventures.

Travel further and these threads become familiar sights on market tables and front porches. Farmers arrange them with the cobs, a sign of freshness and care. In distant places, they’re used in folk remedies, steeped in water and believed to carry the warmth of sunny fields into winter kitchens. There are stories of ancestors who kept a handful tucked in pouches during long journeys, a reminder of home and the cycles of planting and reaping. Sometimes, people line the nests of backyard hens with them, believing they bring gentleness to the eggs and good fortune to the household.

In certain myths, these threads are prized for their ability to connect worlds—the living to the land, the journeyer to possibility. On festival days, they are sometimes scattered by the handful onto the wind, carried away to become part of the earth again. Travelers, especially those setting forth for the first time, have been known to carry a few of these filaments in their pockets, trusting that the same spirit that protects the kernel within its husk will watch over their steps as well. The fool is always at the threshold, trusting the moment, accompanied by these quiet gifts of field and harvest.

Today, someone might steep a small bunch of these threads into a gentle drink, savoring the sweetness and thinking of open roads. Another may braid them and tie them around a jar as a sign of new intentions. One can tuck a few beneath a pillow, hoping for peaceful dreams and safe travels. Or simply walk near a garden or field and notice the way the threads drift in the breeze, each one a gentle call to embrace the journey ahead.
