Thistle

Reversed · #D8BFD8

Hand in hand, the figures stand beneath a sky once bright, now dulled by the hush of an ending. Their fingertips hover close, never quite meeting, as if the air itself has thickened between them. The garden that once flourished at their feet brims now with tough, spiny stems and bristled heads, plants that refuse to be tamed by gentle words. Each step among these boundary-keepers is tentative, wary—a border made not by stone or fence, but by the wild persistence of prickled leaves. In the hush, a breeze shivers through, catching on the edges of each flower’s hard-won crown, scattering seed to unpromised earth.

In the fields beyond, long tradition has marked these stubborn blooms as both curse and cure. Stories tell of cattle lost in the highlands, mouths bloodied by a careless bite, yet shepherds have learned to boil the young hearts of these plants into broth, a bitter meal shared in silence by the fire. In the spring, when the wind turns raw, farmers wrap their palms in thick linen to uproot the oldest stems, clearing ground for softer growth. Even so, they leave a patch uncut at the edge of every field—a concession to endurance, a signal to those passing by that some roots cannot be forced from the soil.

Across the moors, above the stone villages, these plants crowd together, a silent army bracing against the cold rain. On certain nights, mist settles on their tops, clinging like dew to the fine hairs. The air grows heavy, muffling sound, and even the sheep keep to the dry side of the hill. In distant cities, threads spun from their fiber make a rough cloth—scratchy, durable, meant for work aprons and market sacks. Grandmothers still keep a bundle of dried stalks in the cupboard, to be steeped in boiling water for a tonic against sore throats, or slipped beneath the mattress to ward off restless dreams.

There is a symmetry, though it is uneasy, between those who stand side by side and the way this plant grows—never in neat rows, always in clusters, each stem apart yet together. Words falter, caught by a thousand small spines. Still, the urge for connection persists: a neighbor leaves a single flower in a basket on your doorstep, not for beauty, but for memory. A child gathers seeds from the roadside, blowing them into the breeze, wondering what will take root.

Today, you could walk beside an abandoned lot and notice the first spiked heads pushing through the rubble, untouched. You might brew a bitter cup from last year’s foraged roots and share it, quietly, with someone whose words have also failed. You could tuck a pressed flower into the folds of a well-worn letter, waiting for the day when distance softens. Or simply sit by the window as dusk falls, listening to the hush carried on the wind, letting the silence settle without rushing to fill it.
