Draw a card / WHEEL OF FORTUNE

Gold

運命の輪 · ゴールド

Upright · #FFD700

The card’s wheel turns, marked by symbols of earth and sky, of creatures in different stages of ascent and descent. There is always a moment when the wheel crests and dips, and its rim catches the sun’s glare, momentarily brilliant, declaring a day’s change or a season’s turning. In ancient stories, the wheel’s movement was tied to fate, and so too, in the old hills and river valleys, did people look to the earth for tangible signs of fortune’s favor. They would find it in the streambeds, the flakes and nuggets that set prospectors on journeys, sometimes entire towns rising and falling with the promise discovered in a pan or sifted from silt.

This promise shaped myth and history: rivers in distant mountains where kings sent men to wash sheep’s wool, believing the fleece would emerge heavy with shining metal. There are legends of gilded cities, roofs and statues that glimmered beneath the midday sun, drawing explorers across continents and oceans. Temples rose; their domes and spires were hammered until they shone like a second dawn, meant to flash and catch the attention of gods or fate itself. In marketplaces, coins stamped with rulers’ faces moved from hand to hand, tokens of luck or ambition’s fleeting moment.

Closer to home, surfaces gleam in quiet corners: a ring slipped on for a fresh beginning, an heirloom locket clasped in the palm before an uncertain day, small bars hidden in a drawer as a hedge against sudden change. In kitchens, honey glows in glass jars, poured slow and viscous over bread; saffron threads steep in warm water, lending fragrance and hope to a new recipe. The steady gleam of a harvest—kernels of wheat, ears of corn, the first wildflowers in spring—echo the turning cycle, both rising and falling, fortune always suspended on the axis of time.

Weather is marked by moments when sunlight breaks through heavy cloud, a beam crosswise on the floor, making a patchwork among dust motes. Such light makes its way through stained glass in a chapel, falling on prayer beads and quiet faces. The same radiance gilds the steeple’s cross at the hour when bells ring for noon or vespers, a fleeting spark before the clouds draw shut again. To catch a glimpse of something rare and bright—whether it is the glint on a weathercock or the flash of a fish in a clear stream—reminds one to notice the wheel’s upward turn, however brief.

Today, one might turn over a coin and trace its raised edge, or melt honey into afternoon tea, watching the slow swirl in the cup. One could choose a ring to wear on a finger, as a reminder of promises made or fortune’s possibility. Or, as evening nears, set a bowl of ripe apricots on the table and recall harvests past, the taste both sweet and elusive. Each act is a turning, a participation in the rise and fall that the world offers.
