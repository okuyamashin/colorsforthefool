Draw a card / WHEEL OF FORTUNE

Orange

運命の輪 · オレンジ

Upright · #FFA500

The turning wheel rides the line between what has been and what will be, each spoke marking a shift of season or slice of fate. In ancient markets, fortunes were spun with baskets of fruit stacked high: globes harvested from trees that bear their weight after the heat of summer, rinds pitted and gleaming. The wheel’s promise lies in cycles—rising, ripening, falling, and being gathered up again.

Traveling south through groves planted in the clefts of river valleys, caravans once paused where the land grew fragrant with blossoms in late winter. Here, a single round fruit was considered a prize: not native, but brought from distant kingdoms by sailors and traders. Stories grew around these trees—fabled for their ability to thrive through unpredictable rains and sun, their bounty peaking just when the days turned cool and uncertain. The old healers would save peels, drying them for teas to warm cold hands, or to flavor cakes shared when the moon was full. On feast days, a single fruit might be sliced and shared among many, symbolizing luck's turning and fortune’s sweetness after hardship.

Across the Mediterranean, in tiled kitchens swept clean at dawn, housekeepers arranged porcelain bowls filled with the day’s brightest offerings. To set one on the windowsill was to invite good fortune into the home, to hope that the next turn of the wheel would be gentle. Travelers spoke of cities where trade in these fruits shaped fortunes: ships unloading crates at the docks, their scent mingling with salt air and distant stormclouds. In the spice markets, dried peel mingled with cinnamon and clove, ready to be steeped for warmth as the wind picked up, or for perfuming rooms during celebrations where luck was toasted by the fire.

The same fruit gave its name to a region along the winding river in southern France—its groves standing as proof of fortunes built and lost with the river’s rise and fall. Elsewhere, the word became a motif in woven cloth, from silk brocades exchanged in trade deals to the humble checked napkins spread on tables at harvest. In each, the pattern echoed the round, heavy fruit and its textured skin—promise of abundance, hope of sweetness on the tongue.

Storms come and go, as do seasons, but there are ways even now to catch a moment of luck by the rind. Before breakfast, peel one of these fruits and let its sharp scent wake the senses. Place a bowl of them near the door to invite turning fate to favor your threshold. Brew the dried skin into tea on a cold, uncertain night, and sip slowly, remembering that the wheel never stops—every drop is a turn. Gift a single fruit to a friend, and tell them a story of ships and markets, of seasons that change and come again.
