Draw a card / WHEEL OF FORTUNE

GoldenRod

Upright · #DAA520

A circle spins in the mind: spokes, axle and rim, carrying the weight of chance and turn, the seasons’ wheel. At its crest, the old image of Fortuna, blindfolded and serene, stands at the center of change. Surrounding her, the world marks the progress of the year: fields ripen, leaves tumble, rivers shrink and swell in obedience to a pattern older than memory. Here, the turning is neither reward nor punishment, but the living rhythm of life — a pulse that moves history, weather, and small human fortunes.

In late summer and early autumn, roadsides and meadows are lit by slender stems topped with flower clusters, a tapestry growing dense as the days shorten. These sun-following blooms lift their wands above tangled grass, bending in the breeze that signals the year’s slow turn toward rest. Their arrival measures time for bees and for the people who gather them for their homes. In old homes, bundles of these stalks hang from rafters, prized not just for their color but for what they portend — resilience, a remedy at hand, a sign that the earth’s abundance can return even after lean seasons. Herbalists once cut them with careful hands, believing their presence invited good fortune or soothed away the aches that come with the harvest’s hard work.

Across wide plains and up into low hills, travelers have marked their paths by these late-blooming plants. In some places, the name of a river or a stretch of prairie recalls their presence, as if the land itself held onto the luck that follows in their wake. They nod in the wind, unmoved by storm or drought, their roots tangled deep in the soil. In New England, old wives brewed tea from their gathered blossoms to treat illness; far to the west, children fashioned crowns from their pliant stems, believing the wearer would be favored by chance at games and guessing.

In the workshop, a dyer stirs these wildflowers into steaming water, watching as thread and homespun cloth soak up the essence. Over hours, the fibers change, holding fast to the plant’s gift: garments for a new season, sashes knotted at the waist, a ribbon tied around a notebook of new plans. On rain-whipped mornings, the sight brightens a room and invites memory of sun-drenched days, a reminder that cycles always come round again. The same stems, dried and brittle by winter, become kindling for a hopeful fire, or tucked into a pillow to bring dreams of luck’s return.

Weather shifts as the story of the card unfolds: a sudden wind, a flash of sunlight through thick cloud, the promise of clearing after days of drizzle. In these moments, the heart recalls the upright wheel — the sense that change is not to be dreaded, but to be marked, trusted. To turn the wheel’s fortune into daily life, steep a handful of roadside blossoms for a calming tea, or place a sprig by your front door to welcome better chances. Wrap a length of dyed thread around your wrist, or press the plant’s flowers between the pages of a book where you write your hopes for the coming months. Each gesture, however small, affirms that the wheel keeps turning, and with it, luck is renewed.
