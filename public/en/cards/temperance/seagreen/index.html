Draw a card / TEMPERANCE

SeaGreen

Upright · #2E8B57

On the card, water flows in a steady arc from one cup to another, caught midair by a calm figure standing beside a serene pool. With each exchange, the water does not spill or break apart; it mingles, settles, changes. Around the pool, reeds sway in a quiet wind, and the narrow footpath winds away toward a distant ridge, its surface dappled by the light that filters through early morning mist. All movement here feels measured and purposeful, as if the entire scene is held together by the simple act of careful mixing.

Along certain coasts where river mouths open wide to the sea, marshes stretch for miles, thick with beds of seaweed and samphire. In these tidal places, foragers move in rubber boots at dawn, snipping tender stalks that will be scattered over roasted fish or packed into jars for pickling. The air is laced with salt, and gulls wheel overhead, calling above the hush of gentle waves. Old wooden boats, hauled up on the shore, host drying nets and ropes, while their hulls shine with the damp from last night’s rain. These marshes are neither solely land nor water, but always both, a meeting place where boundaries quietly dissolve and new things take shape.

In kitchens near the marshland, cooks reach for enamel bowls and stone mortars to crush sprigs of parsley and mint, blending them with lemon and oil for a sauce that brings the taste of the sea indoors. Here, hands move slowly, tuned to the rhythm of chopping, stirring, and blending. On the windowsill, jars hold bunches of wild herbs, gathered in the cool of the morning, their stems trailing droplets from the garden tap. A pitcher, its glaze rippling like the shallow tidal pools left by the ebbing sea, stands ready to pour water for tea or for soup, a reminder that nourishment often comes from the simplest mingling of elements.

Further up the footpath, fields bordered by hedges sprout rows of peas and beans. After a soft rain, the soil yields a clean, mineral scent, and gardeners in broad hats move quietly among the rows, picking handfuls of fresh pods and wiping their hands on coarse linen aprons. From the hedgerows, children collect smooth stones and bits of driftwood shaped by countless tides, carrying them home as tokens of the places where water and earth meet.

If you wish to bring balance into your day, fill a jug with water and set it on your table, noticing how the surface settles when left undisturbed. Brew a cup of tea with mint or basil, letting the leaves steep and mingle before drinking. Gather a handful of washed stones or shells and arrange them by your window or on your desk, recalling the way they were shaped by time and tide. Take a moment to walk outside after rain, breathing in the fresh air as you watch droplets gather and trickle along the earth.
