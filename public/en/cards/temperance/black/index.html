Draw a card / TEMPERANCE

Black

Reversed · #000000

The angel of Temperance stands with her vessels, but in this moment, one cup tilts, and water spills out onto the stone. Where harmony should live, imbalance seeps in—each drop flees its intended course, puddling where it will. When the careful blending fails, edges and limits become visible, sharp and unforgiving as basalt cliffs rising from a restless coast. The cup’s contents, meant to be poured with precision, now run unchecked, soaking into the earth or rolling over obsidian pebbles that line a riverbank, each one cold, glassy, and silent.

In the stillness that follows the spill, a sense of consequence lingers. In the old villages, the smith’s anvil sits at the heart of the forge, scarred and enduring. Iron strikes on iron, and every errant blow sends a spray of soot across the hearthstones. The smith wipes his hands on a rag dyed with ink from gall nuts, a deep hue that refuses to yield to washing. These are the stains of effort gone awry, of force directed just off center, where the balance once kept is now lost for a moment. The rag hangs beside a coat, heavy with cinders from long work, and the air carries the scent of char.

Elsewhere, in the countryside, crows gather atop barren fields, their feathers slick and unreflective as coal. They watch with keen eyes for what is overlooked or left behind—seeds not sown, grain spilled from the hopper. The birds call out, their voices neither welcome nor unwelcome, but a reminder of nature’s impartial judgment. In the pocket of a passing farmer, a lump of jet polished smooth by worry rests beside a seed packet. The stone is a talisman of absorption, once believed to ward off storms and misfortune.

Storms themselves come in the form of midnight thunderheads, swallowing the last traces of dusk above the rooftops. The air thickens and the world quiets, waiting for the rain to begin. Under the eaves, a cast-iron kettle sits atop the stove, collecting warmth for the evening ahead. The vessel is plain, practical, shaped by fire and cooled in water, its outside marked by years of use. When the balance is off, and the kettle left too long, steam escapes in a hiss, and the kettle reminds the cook how easily patience can boil over.

In the kitchen drawer, a licorice root twists among loose tea leaves and peppercorns. It is bitter and sweet at once, a flavor that lingers. The hands that reach for it may also collect a silk scarf—dyed with the dye that clings to the rag at the forge, worn on journeys through uncertain weather. The scarf shields against a sudden chill at dusk, when the sky loses its color and the path ahead is defined only by what stands out from the gathering shade.

Today, a person may clean soot from the hearth, polish the kettle until it reflects the lamplight, or lay a piece of jet beside the bed before sleep. One could wear a scarf as evening falls, tying it close and stepping into the quiet that arrives after the spill, knowing the world continues to turn and the water will find its way.
