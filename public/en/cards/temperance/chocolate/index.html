Chocolate

Reversed · #D2691E

The angel stands at the edge of the stream, one foot halfway between water and land, holding two vessels meant for a gentle pour. But as the card turns, the water leaps past the rim of the cup, streaking down, pooling at the angel’s feet. The moment before balance is captured in the spill, when precision fails and gravity decides the outcome. So much depends on the hand that pours, the steadiness of the arm, the presence of mind to notice when the cup is too full.

In the heart of tropical forests, the pods of the cacao tree cluster along thick branches, their riches hidden inside. For centuries, farmers have harvested these pods, cracking them open to reveal the seeds, then fermenting and drying them on wide trays beneath a sky that shifts swiftly from sunlight to sudden rain. The process is delicate—too much heat and the seeds burn, too little and they grow mold. Even in this ancient rhythm, the balance is fragile. When the weather tips, when the rains overstay, a harvest can be ruined, the effort of months lost in a few careless days.

Across the Atlantic, these seeds traveled in the holds of wooden ships, stashed with spices and silks, bouncing with the waves. A storm could roll a barrel loose, shattering it against the planks and scattering its contents. In port towns, these beans became currency, exchanged for fabrics or tools or a night under a dry roof. In a merchant’s home, the grinding of the seeds released a fragrance that filled the close air, promising comfort. But a cook distracted by gossip might scorch the mixture, or spill the precious liquid across the hearth, leaving only a sticky trail for ants to find.

In the splendor of European courts, servants carried ornate trays laden with delicate cups, filled with a bitter-sweet drink whisked to a light froth. A misstep on polished floors tipped a cup, staining brocade and prompting gasps. The cost of one mistake—an expensive treat wasted, a reputation tarnished, a quiet lesson in humility. Even among those who could afford the finest things, perfect balance remained elusive.

At home today, a kettle simmers while a slab of the same ancient treat rests waiting to be chopped. A hand slips and crumbs scatter, catching in the seams of the countertop. The hum of the oven and the clatter of spoons mark the attempt to create something soothing, yet the potential for mess hovers near. The ritual—grating, whisking, pouring—requires patience. There is a pause between steps to wipe the counter, to set down the mug firmly, to breathe. Water may spill, but hands learn to steady with practice.

One might slow the pour of hot liquid into a favorite mug, watching for the rise before overflow. Someone could share a square from a cherished bar with a friend, breaking off just enough to savor. A towel, dampened and wrung, may be used to clean the remnants of a morning mishap, preparing the surface for another try. Or, thinking of distant groves and busy ports, a person could place a piece of the treasured food on their tongue and let it melt, remembering how many hands and chances brought it here.
