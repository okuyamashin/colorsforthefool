ForestGreen

Upright · #228B22

A figure crowned with small wildflowers stands quietly in a hush of woodland, hand resting on the muzzle of a great cat. The lion’s jaws ease shut, not by force, but by the calm certainty of her touch. In this clearing, power is silent. The hush is not absence, but presence—like the deep, breathing pause you feel beneath the boughs of a living forest at first light. In stories, this is the place where old roots entwine and living things hold the memory of centuries. Kings and shepherds, outlaws and poets, have all found refuge beneath these trees, and it is here that strength is both shield and invitation.

There is a particular moss that grows thick on the north side of an ancient oak, soft underfoot, hiding the smallest beetles. The person in the card might have gathered wild herbs here, tufts of sorrel and fern, weaving them into the crown. In the woods, wisdom is patient. Woodcutters pass through the shadows, axes slung over their shoulders, knowing that each log for the winter hearth is earned by a day's careful labor. The air carries the scent of crushed needles and fresh earth, a reminder that endurance is built quietly, layer upon layer. The harvest of the woodland is slow, but certain—resin for healing, leaves for tea, sticks for tinder. The lion, once a sign of danger, dozes now at her feet, tamed by presence and trust.

Legends say that the ancient druids would vanish among firs and pines, listening to the wind’s secret speech. Here, strength is not the roar or the blaze, but the slow, steady growth of living wood. The great cat’s mane might brush the bark of a thousand-year-old tree, its rings recording storms weathered and sunlit seasons endured. Monarchs once claimed the deepest groves as their hunting grounds, but at dusk, it is the silent forager, gathering mushrooms and fallen twigs, who belongs most completely to this place. The forest is home to unseen labor: the careful layering of compost, the slow knitting of roots underground, the pull of water upwards in spring. Once, travelers wandered these woods with nothing but a walking stick and a pouch of bread. They knew to look for signs—markings on a trunk, a sapling bending to the sun, the call of a distant thrush.

When storm clouds gather, the canopy becomes a shelter, branches weaving overhead in patterns more ancient than any roof. The rain falls in hushed applause, and the figure with the lion waits, dry and at ease. There is a lesson in this: true influence requires patience, a steady hand, and willingness to listen. One can rise early and tend a small patch of garden, kneeling in damp soil among sprouting beans and ferns. Another might take a walk in a stand of old trees, pausing to rest their hand against the rough bark, feeling the quiet pulse within. In the kitchen, a pot of soup simmers with parsley and wild greens, nourishment drawn from what the woods have given. At the desk, a letter is written on thick paper, the words as consistent as an unbroken woodland path.
