Reversed · #8B0000

In the world of the tarot, the woman steadies the lion with her touch, a quiet force guiding the wild. But when the card is turned on its head, the touch becomes gripping, the gentleness gone. The lion’s mane bristles beneath her hand; the air in the room thickens. What should be a silent accord grows rigid—like the tightening of a leather strap, like the unyielding bite of a bridle on a horse’s jaw when the rider pulls against resistance, not trust.

Somewhere in the highlands of the Caucasus, old stories tell of warriors who wore iron-studded boots and kept curved blades at their belts, sheathed in the same shade as dried oxblood. These warriors believed that to conquer a wild river, you must first stand in its torrent, planting your staff deep into the stony bed, refusing to be moved. The stream pushes harder. The body stiffens, muscles straining; the staff splinters, and the river roars louder, undaunted. Old mountain shrines are built from stones streaked with the hue of pomegranates split open on the altar. The offering is meant to be reverent, but when the knife presses too roughly, seeds burst, juice running in rivulets down the priest’s wrist. The sweet fruit is there, but force drains it of sweetness.

In the market at dusk, a butcher in a stained apron sharpens his cleaver, setting it down beside a chain of smoked sausages. The air is heavy with the scent of spices, with the memory of harvest and slaughter. If he rushes, gripping the blade too tightly, the meat tears where it should yield, sinews snapping instead of slicing clean. The lesson ripples outward: to force is to risk breaking what could be shaped.

At home, an old velvet curtain hangs over a drafty window. Years ago, a hand embroidered elaborate knots into its hem, finding both beauty and security in the twist of the thread. Now, when the wind rattles the glass, someone draws the curtain closed with a tug—too hard, and the fabric strains against the rod. The room is meant to be warm, but the gesture leaves a faint mark, a memory of the hand’s impatience.

When storms gather over the Carpathian plains, clouds churn low, bringing rain that stains the earth. Shepherds huddle by their fires, remembering tales of fields trampled by armies. The mud sticks to boots, to wagon wheels, to the roots of beet crops clinging to the dark soil. The land yields only what the season allows. If you dig with desperate hands, the roots snap, and what’s left in your palm is less than what you sought.

Today, the lesson of the reversed lion and the hand is this: hold loosely, even as you strive. Place a pomegranate on your table and cut it gently. Oil a leather belt, feeling its weight, but do not tighten it past comfort. Hang velvet upholstery by hand, smoothing out each fold, not pulling to the point of strain. And if the rain falls, let it soften the ground before you plant; force nothing into stubborn earth.
