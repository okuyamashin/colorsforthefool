Khaki

Reversed · #F0E68C

The trumpet has sounded, yet the figure remains seated on the edge of the sarcophagus, shoes resting on old dust, hands curled around knees. The river’s crossing waits in the distance, but determination lingers behind a curtain of hesitation. The scene is suspended in the moment before action, as if breath is held, the air thick with yesterday’s unspoken promises. The resurrection is not denied, only deferred. The world outside moves on: the ground is dry, the morning sun sharp, the call unanswered but loud in the mind.

Somewhere beyond the churchyard, long grass shudders in a restless breeze. This is the grass that stains children’s trousers in the summer, the kind that covers the embankments of old imperial railways, the kind woven into the uniforms that once marched through dust and distant campaigns. The fabric of yesterday’s world—woven rough, sturdy, practical—offers some shelter against indecision, as if wrapping up in an old field jacket passed down and patched, the memories of action stitched into every seam. The cloth is familiar, bearing the scent of earth and faint traces of tobacco and metal polish, smelling of stories not yet shared, of endurance and waiting.

On the breakfast table, a bowl holds potatoes boiled and sliced, their skin left on for substance, flecked with cracked pepper and salt. Alongside, there is bread with a crust dusted in flour, as if remembering the slow rise of dough in a warm kitchen, watched in patience. The meal is not elaborate, only enough to sustain, as if made for someone who may find the courage to answer a call tomorrow. The utensils are old—a dull fork, a chipped enamel cup, the kind that lasts through seasons of neglect and still waits to be useful.

Outside the window, the wind stirs dust along a parched road, the sort that soldiers and shepherds have known. The plain stretches to the horizon, unforgiving and wide, the air full of the scent of sun-warmed grasses and distant thunderstorms. Even the weather seems to pause, holding its breath before a long-expected rain. In the old stories, such a landscape is crossed by those who finally heed the summons and rise to walk, though some sit on the edge and listen for a gentler command, a softer promise.

A person feeling this hesitation can walk in tall grass, letting the seeds brush against their hands, as a reminder that even stillness has a texture. Turning the soil in a garden, they can find old buttons or coins, connecting present restlessness with long-ago moments of action. They might polish a worn belt buckle or mend a jacket pocket, small gestures of readiness for when the answer can no longer be postponed. Or they can simply sit in a patch of sun with a cup of strong tea, watching the dust move, and feel the weight of the day’s choice.
