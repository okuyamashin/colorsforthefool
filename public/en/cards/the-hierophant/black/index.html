A high-backed chair stands empty at the front of the hall. In upright times, a figure might have perched there while a hush settled over the congregation, the air thick with incense and the faint rustle of pages. Now, the seat is unclaimed, the patterns of the ceremony persist but the spirit has wandered elsewhere. The book rests open in the middle of the altar, its pages fluttering, but no voice fills the gap. The silence is not absence, but a low hum running beneath the rituals—familiar gestures performed only because they always have been.

In the old tales, such moments arrive at crossroads. A priest stands on the volcanic slope, his sandals sinking into cooled stone, where centuries ago the mountain spat smoke and hunger into the sky. Once, the townsfolk gathered around the edge to offer a loaf or a jug of wine, believing they could soothe what rumbled below. Years pass, and the mountain’s mouth clots with ash and forgotten coins. The path is still walked, but the prayer is thin, echoing only against rock. Children, born long after the last eruption, play at the rim, tossing pebbles into the depths and listening for a reply that never comes.

In the kitchen, a cast-iron skillet hangs from its hook, its surface seasoned by decades of dinners. The cook’s hands work by memory, flipping pancakes as her grandmother did, even though the family recipe has long since lost its meaning—no one recalls what made it special. Still, the pan is used each Sunday, its heavy weight a comfort. On another day, a seamstress mends a coat with lamp soot-stained fingers, working by the faint light of a dying fire. She stitches quietly, the needle darting through thick wool, each movement a repetition of something she learned from her mother, the purpose blurry but the action precise.

Far from the hearth, night folds over a city’s narrow lanes. Rain falls in sheets, spilling into drains, pooling at the feet of lamplighters who move down alleyways in their heavy coats. The old trade endures, but no one looks up—the glow they coax from each lamp is lost to the wet, and the faces behind windows are lit by other things. A stray cat slips along the gutter, pressing into the shadow of a coal cart abandoned beside the curb. The cart was once needed; now it is a relic, marked by handprints and the faint metallic tang that clings to iron.

These are the tokens of those who keep doing what was always done, even as meaning ebbs. The volcano’s stones, the skillet’s heft, the lamp’s glass globe, the coat stitched in silence: all are remnants of forms clung to when the why has drifted away. It is not emptiness, but persistence, a ritual held for its own sake.

Today, you might place a stone upon a windowsill, remembering a story without words. You could cook breakfast in an old, heavy pan, or walk outside beneath rain that soaks and quiets the world. You may choose to mend something, even if you no longer know who first gave it to you.
