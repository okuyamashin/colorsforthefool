Colors for the Fool

Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / DEATH

Maroon

Reversed · #800000

The card holds a figure in armor, mounted and still, pausing at the edge of a rising day. In one hand, the rider closes fingers around a tarnished crown, unwilling to let it fall to the earth among trampled grass and withered wildflowers. A bishop kneels, unmoved in prayer, while behind the scene, the sun hovers just below the horizon, its light pressing but not yet breaking the spell of night. The procession halts; no one speaks, and even the banners, once lifted high, sag in the stagnant air. This is not the charge of endings, but the restless hush of what refuses to be finished.

Across oceans and centuries, there were once settlements cut out of tangled forests, formed by those who fled from old masters and built their own worlds. In thick jungle clearings, they roasted beans over slow fires, letting the scent drift through reed-thatched homes. Stories say the people dyed their fabrics with gifts from the forest, crushing roots and bark, stirring boiling water in battered iron pots. Cloths and headwraps absorbed the color, carrying with them the mark of a history that would not be erased or forgotten, no matter how many times the river was crossed.

In quiet kitchens, a heavy saucepan sits on the stove, filled with beans left to soak overnight so their skins would soften and their centers become dense and creamy. The cook stirs slowly, glancing out the window at a sky pressed flat by clouds that refuse to move on. The stew thickens, dark and rich, a meal that echoes the way some stories are told over and over, each telling adding depth and weight. On the table, a pomegranate splits open, its arils scattered across a plate, staining fingertips and napkins alike. The fruit's taste lingers, tart and stubborn, never quite yielding all its sweetness at once.

In attics and closets, a velvet cap rests among heirlooms, stitched by hands no one remembers, its folds smoothed each year but never worn or discarded. Such things are kept long past their hour, reminders of years when change was possible, but now only the ritual remains. A storm approaches; air thickens, scented with wet earth and the promise of rain that stalls at the edge of town. Doors rattle, but the storm will not break, and the fields outside drink nothing, waiting for relief that does not come.

You may step outside today and sink your boots into soil that clings and will not let go, feeling the pull of something old beneath your feet. Pick the pomegranate from the counter, taste each seed with intention, noting what stays behind. Wrap yourself in a cloth handed down through your family, tracing the embroidery with your thumb, and listen as thunder gathers without release. Light a candle in a thick, rounded glass, and watch as the flame floats above a pool that never quite spills over.

This is a day for holding on, for carrying the crown from room to room, for standing at the window while the dawn waits—unclaimed and unfinished—beyond the glass.
