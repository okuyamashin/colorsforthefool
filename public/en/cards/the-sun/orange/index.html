Draw a card / THE SUN

Orange

太陽 · オレンジ

Upright · #FFA500

A child rides forward on a patient pony, past the low wall of a garden. Cloth pennants overhead move with the morning breeze. The sun’s heat deepens, and what had been hidden in shadow now reveals its true shape—leaves, fruit, and the soft lines of the earth itself. The outlines of everything sharpen, as if the world has been outlined with a practiced hand. The child laughs, reaching to touch the branches where round fruit dangles, heavy and bright.

People living in warm lands wake early to walk between rows of trees. Their hands reach up to cradle the fruit that ripens in the heart of summer, filling baskets woven from reeds beside the orchard. From the Mediterranean to distant islands, these fruits have passed from hand to hand, tokens of health, wealth, and journeys safely ended. Along ancient trade routes, crates of fruit traveled with spices and silk, leaving their scent in the holds of ships and in the markets of far-off cities. At tables, these fruits rest in bowls, set out for guests as a sign of hospitality.

Some rivers are named for the fruit that brightens their banks. On those winding waters, boats carry crates from the groves, down to harbors where merchants gather. In southern towns, markets display piles of fruit, and vendors press their juice into cups for those passing by. Apothecaries, too, use the peel to flavor medicines; cooks grind zest into sauces and desserts. Artisans shape their vessels after the fruit’s roundness, and children learn to carve lanterns from its hollowed shell, setting candles inside to beckon the sun’s return.

In old stories, the fruit appears as a prize. A youth is sent to a distant garden where rare fruit hangs, each one a gift from the sun itself. Heroes race beneath trees, catching the fruit as it falls to mark the start of new adventure. In the halls of emperors, this same fruit is exchanged during festivals, a wish for happiness and new beginnings. In poetry, it stands for warmth and the promise of a day without shadows.

At home, someone slices into the fruit in the soft light of morning. The sharp scent rises, freshening the kitchen. Segments are arranged in a bowl or dropped into water to flavor the day. The peel, grated fine, is mixed with sugar for cakes or boiled with honey for tea. When winter lingers, a strip of dried peel is tucked into a pocket as a quiet charm, or a jar of preserves is opened to share the memory of sunlit days.

Today, you might carry a piece of the fruit with you, tucked into your bag or lunch. You can choose an old path to walk, imagining the orchards and port towns once linked by this trade. At night, add peel to simmering water, scenting your room before rest. Or knot a length of bright ribbon, dyed in the shade of the fruit, around your wrist before stepping into the light.
