Draw a card / THE SUN

SandyBrown

Reversed · #F4A460

The Sun card, tilted from its throne, scatters its warmth in harsh lines. Light is relentless, swallowing up the gentle gradients between shade and shine, so the path ahead is painted flat and brittle. In the world of the reversed sun, the road stretches as a hard ribbon of dust and heat, shimmering with the memory of ancient trade. Camel caravans once crossed such plains where every pebble and tuft of scruffy grass seemed to tremble under the weight of noon. When light falls too fiercely, the ground itself seems to dry out, and the sandals of the traveler collect grains of the earth, each step a measure of patience and endurance.

There are mornings in the baked cities of the Levant or in the ghost towns of the Southwest when the light hits stucco walls and alley bricks until even shadow withdraws, leaving only the residue of effort in the seams of your clothes. In such hours, the kitchen is a place of refuge, and you reach for the wooden spoon, its handle shaped by time and worn palms, or for the bread whose crust holds the memory of wheat fields at harvest—fields that once rippled beneath sun-withered skies. The baking of bread, the stirring of a simmering stew in an old clay pot, these are rituals that honor the lands where dryness is a teacher.

The myth of Phaethon’s ride comes to mind, when the chariot of the sun ran wild, scorching the earth until the sands ran loose, and the rivers shrank to threads. In the aftermath, people learned to build with what the sun left behind. In North Africa, bricks are pressed from earth and straw, laid out in the open to bake into houses and garden walls. The process is slow, the labor measured, and there is pride in the endurance of a thing that bears the sun’s mark without yielding to it. Old sandals, too, speak of such places: soles battered but holding, straps cracked yet reliable, dust ground into every seam.

In the heart of a dry market town, baskets of dates and flatbreads rest near the cobbler’s stall, where leather shoes—long-walked and patched—wait for another journey. There, the merchant’s voice is soft in the heat, recounting the story of a road that never ends, of feet that keep moving through the dust, of family recipes that preserve the sweetness of harvest even as the fields wait for rain. At the edge of the market, a caravan passes, and the camels shake loose a cloud behind them, the air briefly rich with the smell of sweat, earth, and distant spices. A potter’s hands shape a vessel on the wheel, the clay pulled from the very land that the sun scours—each rotation gathering memory and hope for cool water one day.

If the day feels long and the light unyielding, drink water from an unglazed vessel, feeling the cool seep through the clay. Walk on bare earth or worn planks, letting your soles remind you of how far you have come. Bake bread or tend to a plant that craves dryness, giving it your slow attention. Sit where the sun’s touch is filtered, letting yourself rest a while, as travelers do at the end of a path.
