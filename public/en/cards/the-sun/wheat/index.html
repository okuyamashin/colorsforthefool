Draw a card / THE SUN

Wheat

太陽 · ウィート

Upright · #F5DEB3

On the card, a child rides a sturdy mare, arms open wide, passing through a field bordered by sunflowers. The sunflowers stand tall, their faces turning to follow the warmth that spills across the land. Behind them, a short stone wall traces the line between the open field and the horizon, showing that the world has been shaped and marked out again. Where once the edge of things was hidden in shadow, now every blade and stalk is clear, each plant defined by light. The time for guessing what lies beyond is finished; the field’s outline is drawn by steady hands, the harvest ready, the season’s work gathered in full view.

Since ancient days, these fields determined the wealth and life of villages. The plant, grown from a handful of grain pressed into soil after the floodwaters receded or after the spring thaw, became the measure of a year’s fortune. In old stories, a sheaf carried home on a woman’s back meant safe winters and full plates. Along the rivers of Egypt, in the dry air of the Levant, across the endless flat lands stretching to far horizons, the harvest was the hinge on which all hopes turned. Here, the heavy heads bow as if in gratitude, while the stubble left behind is gathered for bedding, baskets, or the thatching of roofs. What stands above ground is food for body and spirit; what remains beneath knits soil together, holding it against the wind.

In wide-open lands, small towns depended on the turning of the seasons, the sound of sickle and scythe, the carts rolling to granaries before the first storm. Mills rose beside quiet streams, their stones grinding out the meal that would become bread. In northern outposts, where summer is a brief visitor, each seed sown and reaped was counted, every loaf a reason to celebrate. The cycle of planting, waiting, and reaping was a ritual that bound neighbors together, their fortunes rising with the tide of grain.

Inside the home, the memory of the field remains. A wooden bowl, smoothed by generations of hands kneading dough. A broom made from straw, sweeping dust from the hearth. A sack set aside to hold the next day’s meal, its mouth tied with twine, its contents the result of months of sunlight and labor. On brisk mornings, a kettle bubbles on the stove, sending the scent of porridge through the kitchen—a quiet recollection of distant fields and the labor that brought them to the table. Every fragment, every leftover chaff, is used; nothing is lost when the harvest is honored.

Today, you can bake a loaf by hand, letting the slow rise of dough echo the patient growth of the field. Sweep your floor, each stroke a memory of reaping. Walk along the edge of a field or park, observing how care leaves its mark on the land. Give thanks at your table, remembering how each meal is rooted in ground that once seemed empty but now stands complete.
