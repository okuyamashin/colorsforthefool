Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / THE SUN

Olive

Upright · #808000

When the Sun card stands upright, it is a midday inheritance, the return of coherence to a world that might have blurred at the edges. In the card's scene, a child rides forth on a small horse with open arms, all bathed in the clarity of sunlight: there is nothing half-seen, nothing concealed by shadow. The wall behind is sturdy, defining sacred space; sunflowers rise up in ordered ranks, their faces turned toward the shining completeness above. Each stone and each petal now possesses a distinguishable boundary.

From this place of light, the mind returns to southern groves at the world's edge. The gnarled trunks of ancient trees stand rooted in rocky soil, their branches weighed with fruit whose pressed oil once anointed kings and healed the sick. There are jars unearthed from Phoenician shipwrecks, sealed and heavy, each carrying within them the labor of hundreds of pale, elongated drupes. The press itself is a wheel and slab, creaking as it turns—harvesters' hands stained, skins wrinkled, the scent sharp in the sun-warmed courtyards.

To speak of these trees is to speak also of peace parched into treaties: a bough held out in fraught assemblies, a token for peace or surrender, yet also for victory, when woven into the crowns of athletes or conquerors. In the warm Mediterranean afternoon, a fisherman stops to eat, breaking bread and pressing fruit between his thumb and forefinger, the meal simple and enduring as the sea that laps at his home’s stones.

Within the household, jars of brine are stored on cool shelves. The fruit preserved within is pulled from the jar for offerings at new beginnings. Cloths steeped in the same pressed liquid clean wounds and polish wood, carrying the memory of sun and soil through every act of care. In kitchens, stone mortars grind the fruit to paste; in pantries, the oil waits to lamp the night or anoint the brow of the weary. To plant such a tree is to make a covenant with time, for it yields fruit only after years of tending, and then for lifetimes.

Even in weather, the signs are read: when the wind rattles the branches and scatters the small, hard fruit underfoot, it forecasts drier days and clear skies. Mountain villages watch for the spring's first buds, knowing that what follows is abundance and wholeness. In places where the ancient press stands silent, people still gather beneath the leaves, stones cool against their backs, to talk and share news, their laughter carrying out into the bright noon.

Today, you can steep a handful of fruit in warm water and taste what the sun carried through stone and leaf. You can rub a drop of oil into your hands to remember the work of old harvests. Go outside for a walk where there are gnarly, low trees and make a note of their endurance and their shade. Set a small vessel on your table, filled with fruit or branches, to mark your space as peaceful and whole.
