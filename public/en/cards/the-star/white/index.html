Draw a card / THE STAR

White

Upright · #FFFFFF

A woman kneels at the edge of a quiet pool, pouring water from two pitchers—one stream splashing onto the thirsty soil, the other returning to the pool, making ripples that reach the bank. Above her, a great star shines with certain clarity, circled by companions, steady in the twilight. Hope is found here in the gesture itself: a promise that what is poured out will nourish, that what is returned will be received.

In the old tales, when the fields had burned to dust, a traveler searched for the high country where the world was still clean. She found herself on a glacier, where the ground was hidden beneath a thick covering that stretched to the horizon, brilliant and undisturbed. Each step was muffled, the only sound her breath and the wind. There, the world was remade every morning, each animal track erased overnight. In such places, shepherds rose before dawn to lead their flocks out across pastures washed pure by the cold, trusting that their footprints would not last long, but that their journey mattered.

In the cities, hope was woven into daily life. Bakers woke early and dusted their boards with sifted flour, shaping loaves in the hush before sunrise. The flour settled on their hands, in the creases of aprons, a sign of beginnings. Tailors cut unmarked cloth from bolts stacked in quiet rooms, their scissors gliding through material that had not yet been patterned by use or stained by time. On festival mornings, clean linen was hung in doorways and windows, its folds catching the early light, inviting luck and clarity into cramped apartments.

Storms passed over the plains, leaving behind a film on the grass and rooftops, a sign to farmers that the air had shifted, that planting could begin anew. In distant temples, priests unrolled scrolls onto unblemished surfaces and recited prayers, their breaths stirring the fine powder that covered the altar stones. Children played in meadows, gathering petals from wild hedgerows, their pockets filling with the small treasures of a landscape just after rain.

In every household, gestures of renewal could be found. Someone shook out a tablecloth before a meal, let bread cool on a rack, or emptied the last pitcher of water into the garden’s waiting soil. The smallest acts built toward hope: a nurse tying a fresh bandage with steady hands, a letter started on an untouched sheet, a new candle lit in a window as the sky dimmed. Each was a sign that the world could be restored, beginning with what was nearest at hand.

Today, the Star’s promise can be carried forward by opening a window and hanging untreated linen in the morning air, or by folding a napkin slowly and setting it beside your plate. You might write a message on a new page, letting words appear where none have come before, or rinse your face in a basin before bed, as if washing away the dust of old worries.
