MidnightBlue

星 · ミッドナイトブルー

Reversed · #191970

The pitchers have been set down on the stone, their lips resting where the pool’s rim once shone with water. The ground is cool and dry; the surface of the pool is gone, leaving only a memory of reflection. Above, the arch of the night sky is wide and scattered with silent lights, but their shimmer does not touch the earth’s surface as it used to. The air here is hushed, and the wind has stilled to nothing. The promise in the card’s horizon is now a line of distant lamps, untouchable, yet patiently burning. The heart of this place is the edge between water and sky, where longing looks up and finds the stars remote, their song not echoed in the dust.

This is a night not of rainfall but of stillness, when the city finds itself under a sky that presses close and heavy. The edge of the pool could be found in many places: at the threshold of an abandoned public bath where shadows gather behind a locked iron gate, or along the banks of a river after festival lanterns have gone out, leaving the embankment in silence. The world is shaped by the quiet of a late hour when a sailor looks up from the deck, recognizing constellations that guided older ships. In such hours, cathedrals’ domes recede into the sky, the stonework blending into the deep, and even the hum of the city is muffled by the heaviness above.

In the stories of far northern villages, there are tales of fishermen returning after midnight, their boats gliding through narrow fjords. The water is ink and the mountains are just outlines. Their wives wait by the hearth, embroidering blankets in the dim light, each stitch a prayer for safe passage beneath the high, unknowable vault. The night is a roof, a boundary, and a shield. It is also the velvet curtain drawn over the stage of evening, when only the glint of a watch face, a brooch, or a polished shoe betrays the presence of those moving through the darkness. There is a sense of suspended time, a hush that holds secrets―the moment when the last train passes under the catenary wires and the rails gleam briefly before vanishing into the absence of moon.

The pool’s edge drying means the tools of hope are paused or abandoned. The pitchers wait; the hands that carried them are folded in a lap, or absent. In history, such times called for patience. The navigators waiting for clear skies, the astronomers sitting beside cold instruments, the poets listening for a word in the hush. In monasteries, the tide of prayer ebbs for an hour; in city apartments, the tap is turned off and the teacup set aside. The storm has passed or not yet come, and so at the threshold, one listens for what might rise from the silence.

Tonight is a good time to be still by an open window and count the distant blinking lights on a radio tower. You could take out a dark-woven scarf and let it rest across your shoulders, feeling its quiet weight. You might write a short note to someone far away, letting your words travel through the night’s hush. Or, simply tidy the table at the end of the day, setting down glass and pitcher and stepping back from the edge of the pool, at peace with the waiting.
