Today's lucky color, drawn from the tarot

Draw a card / THE CHARIOT

Maroon

Reversed · #800000

The driver in the chariot holds the reins in uncertain hands. The wheels keep spinning, carving twin ruts in the earth, but the beasts yoked to the cart look away from one another. Their bodies strain forward, hooves tearing at the ground, yet their eyes measure different paths. The charioteer feels the movement, but not the forward pull; the road becomes a circle, and each turn is a repetition. The journey matters more than the destination, sometimes more even than the sense of direction.

Long ago, runaways found shelter in dense forests far from crowned cities—places thick with shadows and roots. Their footsteps vanished into the undergrowth, and over time, they raised villages with roofs woven from fibers steeped in dye. Sailors from distant lands traded for these cloths, tracing their fingers over the heavy folds that held the scent of earth after rain. When tempests battered the coast, the sea would toss ships onto sandbars and leave them stranded. Ashore, the castaways mended hulls with pitch and rope, hands stained by the same plants that colored the woven cloth. Here, the urge to move became the need to survive, and the land itself seemed to absorb each restless tread.

Stories grow around these shelters—of storms whose clouds stack high and bruise the sky, and of the fruit that grows close to the ground, tart and puckered, gathered in baskets by those waiting for the clouds to clear. The jam made from this fruit simmers for hours, filling the kitchen with a scent both tart and heavy. In the quiet between gusts of wind, an old chestnut table holds a bowl of these preserves. Bread, torn in uneven pieces, is dipped into the sticky jam as thunder murmurs beyond the shutters.

On another continent, workers in dye-shops tend vats simmering over low fires. Cloth is lowered in, turned with long wooden paddles so the fibers drink in the liquid. The cloth emerges heavy, draping over the arms of the apprentice who learned the trade from grandparents. Each bolt is folded and stacked beside the hearth, ready for tailors or for merchants passing through with news and coins. Even in cities, where stone streets twist in confusion, there are those who wear a scarf or a sash from this cloth, a reminder of where they came from or where they hope to go.

There is a dry wind today, the kind that sweeps across savanna and mountain, scattering leaves and dust. The sky is clouded, but there’s no rain—just the restless sway of branches and the call of birds shifting place. In these moments, the feeling of being adrift sharpens, like a ship stranded just beyond the safe line of the harbor. Yet, even a shipwreck brings together unlikely hands: to build fires, share bread, trade stories, and wait for the next tide.

Today, you can slice a piece of bread and spread it with thick preserves, letting the tartness linger on your tongue. You can wrap yourself in a heavy shawl, its weave comforting against your skin, and listen for the distant sound of thunder. You can turn over an old wooden bowl in your hands, tracing the grain with your thumb, remembering the hands that shaped it. Or you could find a story of those who wandered far and made a home, reading it aloud as the wind moves outside your window.
