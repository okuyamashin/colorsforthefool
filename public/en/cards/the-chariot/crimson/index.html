Draw a card / THE CHARIOT

Crimson

Upright · #DC143C

The canopy above the charioteer is heavy silk, embroidered with the pomegranate and the rose, a shield against the glare and dust of the noon road. Below, the two beasts strain at their harness, each muscle visible as they steady themselves at the city gate. This is not their first journey together. The old gate is painted with the stories of conquerors and merchants, all those who have ridden out beneath banners dyed by the hands of far-off traders. The charioteer's fingers close around the reins, rawhide toughened by sun, soft where sweat has polished it smooth. He is ready to cross the threshold, to pair the wild and the tame, to hold both in tension and move forward.

Beyond the walls, the fields ripple with the movement of cochineal harvesters. They gather the scale insects from prickly pear branches, each worker dropping cotton bags full of little bodies into baskets. These same insects once rode across empires, hidden in trunks bound for Venice and Istanbul, there to be rendered into a powder that stains fingertips and boiling water alike. In dyeing rooms, women and men stir vats with long wooden paddles, watching as wool and silk soak up the precious tincture. The traders who bear these bundles to market are watched by children sitting on steps, lips marked from the syrup of mulberry and sour cherry. In the morning, the merchants will kneel on velvet prayer rugs, the fabric vibrant from years of careful dyeing.

The city gate itself has seen processions and farewells. Knights in armor once pressed their lips to ribbons tied to their lances, tokens from beloveds back in garden courtyards among the pomegranate trees. During festival nights, those same gates frame the sky above the city as lanterns drift upward, each flame mirrored in a pool where the petals of bloodroot and hibiscus float. The charioteer has watched such nights, learning that every departure is paired with a homecoming, every conquest balanced by the memory of safe return. The beasts before him—one bred for speed, the other for stubborn strength—shift together. The movement only happens when both yield and hold.

There is an old story told in these lands about a river that once ran with the juice of madder root, when the dyers let their vats empty at the water’s edge, painting the stones for a league beyond the walls. The bones of fish were stained for seasons after, and washing women found their linen sashes brighter than before. In monasteries and court ateliers, the record-keepers ink their ledgers with gall and resin, but the seals are always impressed in wax melted from the same pigment, signifying the authority to cross boundaries, to make binding what was only intention.

Tonight, at your own threshold, you might tie a silk scarf around your wrist, a relic from a loved one or a discovery in your grandmother’s chest. You could slice open a pomegranate and scatter its seeds at your door, naming aloud the journeys you intend to begin. Stir a pot of beetroot soup before nightfall and carry its warmth to a neighbor. Write a note of resolve in a ledger or journal, and seal it with wax, pressing your mark into its surface.
