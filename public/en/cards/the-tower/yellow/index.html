Draw a card / THE TOWER

Yellow

塔 · イエロー

Upright · #FFFF00

A lone tower looms against a stormy sky, stone stacked on stone, its form a testament to ambition and the impulse to shelter something precious inside. The night bristles with silence until, in a moment both sudden and absolute, a bolt from the heavens splits the air. Lightning spears the crown atop the tower, and it falls, tumbling end over end. The walls shudder and break, and everything once guarded—hopes, secrets, stories too volatile to speak—flies outward into the chaos. The world within is exposed, and the night is forever changed by what emerges.

This is the aftermath of upheaval, a landscape littered with fragments and the promise of rebirth. In gardens scorched by disaster, spring’s earliest daffodils push through the earth, their trumpets unfurling where only brittle stalks remained. These first flowers ring out in silent chorus, persistent, refusing to be contained. Across kitchen counters in houses rattled by change, someone slices open a lemon, pressing its flesh against the ridged surface of a grater. The juice leaps out in sudden arcs, puckering the air with brightness, hinting at flavor and renewal. A honeycomb, freshly cracked, glistens in the pantry; its waxen cells spill syrup onto the counter, evidence that sweetness, though often hidden, will not stay secret forever.

Geography remembers cataclysm in its own silent language. Along ancient trade routes, travelers carried blocks of sulfur chipped from the rim of volcanoes, goods born from fire and upheaval, traded for salt and silk. In high mountain passes, monks tended to fields of chrysanthemum, harvesting the tightly curled blossoms just before they opened. When dropped in boiling water, each bud relaxes, its petals opening in a gesture that mirrors the release of tension after a storm. Silk banners, dyed from the stigma of crocus or the heart of the wild saffron, adorn temples and courts. As the wind moves through a broken window, the fabric catches the sun, shining with the memory of long journeys and deep transformation.

History is a chronicle of towers rising and falling. Miners once sent canaries ahead into unlit seams, birds whose song would falter at the first sign of danger, their presence a reminder that warning is sometimes the only gift we receive before collapse. Rulers of old wore tunics spun from the threads of marigold and chrysanthemum, tokens of survival won through storms, each robe a claim to grace after ruin. In fields battered by summer hail, wheat stalks bow and, with the first warm day, stand again. On sacred mornings, bakers break open loaves rich with honey, offering what was hidden within to mark the beginning of something new.

Amid upheaval, a person might squeeze lemon into water and use the mixture to cleanse the remnants of yesterday’s disaster. Another could light a beeswax taper, letting its gentle glow turn cracked walls into something gentler. Gathering daffodils in a battered garden, one might arrange them in a pitcher beside a window, or stir honey into hot tea, tasting the aftermath of storm as a quiet kind of hope.
