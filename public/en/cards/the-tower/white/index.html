Draw a card / THE TOWER

White

Upright · #FFFFFF

Lightning splits the night, a single jag of energy hurled from the clouds. Far below, stone cracks. At the tip of the ruined spire, a circlet falls, its story undone in a moment. The Tower stands no more, its chambers exposed to the air. What was contained, hidden behind centuries of masonry, pours outward—ash, feathers, and the unspoken. In the sudden storm, the world is made open, what was secret released by force.

In old myths, destruction always comes with a trial of cleansing. Floods sweep away cities; sacred fires eat through ancient roofs. There is a memory of salt crust left after the deluge, gleaming in the morning. Cathedrals and castles once burned beneath thunderstruck skies, leaving only chalk lines where walls had stood and the dust of powdered shells in the street. When Rome suffered its worst nights, priests once scattered flour across thresholds to mark the broken sanctity and to begin again from emptiness.

Near the ruins, winter weather follows calamity. A hush falls as the first snow begins, flakes gathering on shattered stones and tangled roots. Each flake is a boundary erased, a softly falling pause between what was and what is to come. The world underneath is covered, not destroyed but quieted, given space to breathe. In northern lands, after the storm has passed, the fields are blanketed in the same way, the remnants of violence stilled by the hush of new powder.

Inside homes, the survivors of the night gather. They press linen against wounds, fold napkins for a meal made from what is left. Bread, simple and pale, is broken and shared. The tablecloth is laid fresh, threadbare but honest, a sign that life continues. In moments of upheaval, people reach for what is clean and unmarked—pages ready to record new names, milk poured to calm a restless child, a single candle lit for remembrance. The ritual is plain: the eggshell on the floor after a hasty breakfast, the steam rising from a pot of rice. Each thing is a beginning, the ordinary made sacred by the clarity after everything else has fallen away.

History keeps traces of endings and starts in the same breath. In monasteries long ago, monks scrubbed marble steps after a night of trouble, then set out new parchment to begin a chronicle. On the Silk Road, traders packed their goods in spun fibers, hoping for a safe journey after the storm. In fields, the bones of animals were gathered, cleaned, and set aside, allowed to rest in the open air. The world renews itself by showing its scars, letting light in through the cracks.

Today, a person might scatter flour on the threshold to mark the place of a fresh beginning or fold a soft napkin over their lap and share a meal in the quiet after argument. Writing a new letter on an empty sheet, or lighting a candle by the window, brings the calm after the collapse into a moment of daily life. These are ways to honor what has come through the storm.
