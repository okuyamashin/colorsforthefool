Black

Upright · #000000

The night looms over the stone tower, ancient and tall, its sharp silhouette broken only by a sudden vein of lightning. The sky is a thunderhead, heavy and roiling. In one violent instant, that flash strikes the highest parapet, splitting stone and sending the steepled crown clattering into the unseen below. The world holds its breath as loose stones plummet, vanishing into the absence beneath their fall. The air is thick with a scent like burnt timber and the taste of iron-heavy rain.

At the tower’s foot, the land is silent and strange. It is the hour when ravens are most awake, their wings beating upward from sleeping fields, sharp eyes seeking what the storm has scattered. The fields themselves are hidden, their furrows pressed flat beneath a blanket of earth and shadow. Somewhere near, a coal pit breathes out its memory of fire, all spent fuel and spent ambition, now only darkened husk and the shimmer of unyielding mineral. The well, shrouded and still, promises depth, its rope slick and cool to the hand, its water echoing far below, a secret held in the earth.

On the road that winds through this disquiet, a merchant’s cart carries a bolt of fabric, as dark as the inside of a new moon night. The cloth is folded and heavy, woven tightly, intended for an undertaker’s coat or the curtains of a room where sleep is absolute. The merchant’s hands are stained from the handling of dye, from the ink pressed into parchment and ledgers, from the soot that settles after the lamps are doused. He remembers the trade roads where charcoal is exchanged, where lumps of pitch and tar are heated to seal boats against the river’s secrets, where obsidian knives are wrapped in oiled rags, sharp enough to split silence itself.

Beyond the path, in the hedgerow, grows a patch of nightshade. Its berries, glossy and round, are untouched by birds—known and ignored, a warning and a promise. Alongside it, the bitter roots of licorice and the deep-seeded iris lie dormant, waiting for a chance to reach the surface, for something to break open old ground. The storm’s arrival is an invitation: what was buried, what was hidden in chests and behind veils, now comes forward with unrepentant clarity. The cypress in the graveyard leans, unmoved by wind, its trunk a marker for memory and for endings.

Within the home, the hearth is swept out after the storm. The ashes, fine and lightless, are scattered on the garden to feed next season’s soil. A cast-iron cauldron sits on the stones, cool now, but marked and seasoned by a thousand meals and a hundred emptyings, its surface absorbing every history and every hunger. A lockbox, stored under the bed, holds a diary bound in oilskin, a keepsake from a voyage across moonless seas—pages filled by candle smoke and the sound of distant bells tolling in fog.

Tonight, after the lightning has done its work, a person might sweep their own hearth, gathering up what remains after the fire. They could light a stick of incense made from charred wood, letting the smoke mark a boundary between what stays and what goes. It is a good hour to walk under the cypress and let the roots speak, or to brew a cup of coffee, letting its darkness settle in the cup before drinking deep. Each act is a quiet recognition of what the collapse reveals: the truth beneath, waiting for the next beginning.
